Cyber risk management

How organisations understand and manage the risk of cyber attacks and technology failures, from baseline controls to incident response and regulatory obligations.

Intermediate About 13 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Cyber risk is an operational risk driven by criminals, state actors, insiders and accidental failures. Ransomware, credential theft and supply chain attacks are among the most serious threats.
  • Frameworks such as the NIST Cybersecurity Framework organise cyber risk management into govern, identify, protect, detect, respond and recover.
  • The Australian Signals Directorate's Essential Eight sets out baseline mitigation strategies, assessed against maturity levels.
  • APRA-regulated entities must meet CPS 234, and most businesses with turnover of $3 million or more must report ransomware payments within 72 hours.
  • Preparation matters most: tested backups, rehearsed incident response and oversight of service providers.

What cyber risk is

Cyber risk is the risk of harm from the failure, misuse or attack of technology and data. It is a type of operational risk, and for many organisations now one of the largest. It covers both deliberate attacks by criminals, states or insiders, and accidental failures such as misconfigured systems.

ThreatWhat happens
Ransomware and extortionCriminals encrypt or steal data and demand payment to restore it or not publish it.
Phishing and credential theftStaff or customers are tricked into giving up passwords or approving logins.
Business email compromiseCriminals take over or impersonate email accounts to redirect payments.
Supply chain compromiseAttackers get in through a service provider, software update or shared platform.
Exploiting unpatched systemsKnown vulnerabilities in internet-facing systems are used to gain access.
Insider threatStaff or contractors misuse access, deliberately or carelessly.
Denial of serviceOnline services are overwhelmed and made unavailable.

The Essential Eight

The Australian Signals Directorate's Essential Eight is a set of baseline mitigation strategies that make it much harder for attackers to compromise systems. It is mandatory for many Commonwealth agencies and widely used as a benchmark by others. Each strategy is assessed against maturity levels from 0 (not meaningfully implemented) to 3 (mitigates sophisticated, targeted adversaries).

StrategyIn plain English
Application controlOnly approved programs can run.
Patch applicationsFix known weaknesses in software quickly, especially internet-facing software.
Configure Microsoft Office macro settingsBlock macros from the internet and untrusted sources.
User application hardeningTurn off risky features in browsers and other applications.
Restrict administrative privilegesLimit powerful accounts to the people and tasks that need them.
Patch operating systemsKeep operating systems up to date and replace unsupported versions.
Multi-factor authenticationRequire more than a password, especially for remote and privileged access.
Regular backupsBack up important data, protect backups from attackers, and test restoring them.

Incident response

  1. Prepare

    Response plans and playbooks (for ransomware, data theft, provider compromise), roles, external support (forensics, legal), and regular exercises, including with the board.

  2. Detect and analyse

    Confirm what happened, what systems and data are affected, and start the awareness clock for reporting.

  3. Contain

    Isolate affected systems and accounts to stop the spread.

  4. Eradicate and recover

    Remove the attacker, restore from clean backups, and return critical operations within tolerance levels.

  5. Notify and communicate

    Regulators, affected people, the ASD's Australian Cyber Security Centre and, if relevant, police and insurers.

  6. Learn

    Root cause analysis and fixes, fed back into the risk profile and controls.

The regulatory picture

ObligationWhoKey points
CPS 234 Information SecurityAPRA-regulated entitiesInformation security capability matched to threats, controls tested, material incidents notified to APRA within 72 hours.
CPS 230 Operational Risk ManagementAPRA-regulated entitiesCyber disruption to critical operations, service provider risk and business continuity.
Privacy Act, Notifiable Data Breaches schemeMost organisations with turnover over $3 million, and othersAssess suspected data breaches within 30 days; notify the OAIC and affected people if serious harm is likely. See Privacy law.
Cyber Security Act 2024Businesses with turnover of $3 million or more, and critical infrastructure entitiesReport ransomware or cyber extortion payments to the Australian Signals Directorate within 72 hours (since 30 May 2025).
Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018Owners and operators of critical infrastructure assets, which can include some financial services assetsRisk management programs and incident reporting for covered assets.
Directors' dutiesAll companiesBoards are expected to oversee cyber risk. In ASIC v RI Advice Group (2022), the Federal Court found an AFS licensee breached its obligations by failing to have adequate cyber security risk management.

Third-party cyber risk

Many of the largest recent breaches came through service providers. Good practice includes assessing providers' security before contracting, contract terms on security, notification and audit rights, monitoring (including of the provider's own suppliers), and planning for a provider compromise. See Third-party risk.

Questions boards can ask

  • What are our crown jewels, and how well are they protected?
  • Where are we against the Essential Eight, and what is the plan to close gaps?
  • How quickly could we restore critical operations from backups after a ransomware attack, and when did we last test it?
  • Which service providers could cause us a serious breach?
  • Have we rehearsed our response, including a decision on whether we would ever pay a ransom?
