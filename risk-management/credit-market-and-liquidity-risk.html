Credit, market and liquidity risk (the basics)

The three classic financial risks: someone doesn't pay you back, prices move against you, or you can't get cash when you need it.

Beginner About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Credit risk: a borrower or counterparty fails to pay what it owes. Expected loss is often estimated as probability of default × loss given default × exposure at default.
  • Market risk: losses from movements in interest rates, exchange rates, share prices and other market prices.
  • Liquidity risk: not being able to pay obligations on time, or only at a high cost. It can sink an otherwise solvent institution very quickly.
  • The three are closely linked. In a crisis they tend to strike together.
  • APRA regulates them through prudential standards such as APS 220 (credit risk), APS 116 and APS 117 (market and interest rate risk), APS 210 (liquidity) and, for super funds, SPS 530 (investment governance).

Where the risks sit: a bank's balance sheet

A simple bank balance sheet shows where each risk comes from. The same ideas apply, with changes, to insurers and super funds.

Credit risk

In plain English: you lend money (or sell on credit, or enter a contract), and the other party doesn't pay you back in full or on time.

Credit risk is the biggest risk for most banks, because lending is their core business. But it appears everywhere: an insurer's reinsurer might not pay a claim; a super fund's bond investments can default; a business's customers may not pay their invoices. The other party in a financial contract is called a counterparty, so you'll also hear "counterparty risk".

Measuring it: expected loss

A standard way of estimating the average credit loss uses three ingredients:

  • PD, probability of default: how likely the borrower is to default over the next year.
  • LGD, loss given default: if they default, what share of the exposure is lost after recoveries, such as selling the house that secures a mortgage.
  • EAD, exposure at default: how much is owed at the time of default.

Expected loss = PD × LGD × EAD. Banks price loans and hold provisions to cover expected losses, and hold capital to absorb unexpected losses beyond that.

Default values: a $600,000 home loan with a 1.5% annual chance of default, and a 20% loss if it defaults (because the house can be sold). Illustrative numbers only.

Managing it

  • Underwriting standards: checking the borrower's ability to repay before lending (for consumers, also a legal requirement under the responsible lending obligations in the National Consumer Credit Protection Act).
  • Collateral and guarantees that reduce loss given default.
  • Limits on exposure to single borrowers, industries and regions, to avoid concentration risk.
  • Monitoring arrears and early-warning signs, and managing problem loans.
  • Capital: APRA's capital standards (APS 112 for the standardised approach and APS 113 for internal models) set how much capital banks hold for credit risk. APS 220 Credit Risk Management sets requirements for the credit risk management framework.

Market risk

In plain English: you hold something whose value depends on market prices, and the price moves against you.

TypeWhat movesExample
Interest rate riskInterest ratesA bank funds 5-year fixed-rate loans with deposits whose rates rise when the cash rate goes up, squeezing its margin. Bond prices fall when rates rise.
Equity riskShare pricesA super fund's balanced option falls 10% in a sharemarket downturn.
Foreign exchange riskExchange ratesAn unhedged holding of US shares loses value in Australian dollars when the Australian dollar rises.
Commodity riskCommodity pricesA trading position in oil or gold.
Credit spread riskThe extra yield investors demand for credit riskCorporate bond prices fall when investors become more nervous, even without any default.

For banks, APRA distinguishes the trading book (positions held for trading, covered by APS 116 Capital Adequacy: Market Risk) from interest rate risk in the banking book (the mismatch between rates on loans and deposits, covered by APS 117). Common measurement tools include sensitivity analysis ("what happens if rates rise 1%?"), Value at Risk and stress testing.

A super fund difference

In a bank, market risk is borne by shareholders. In an accumulation super fund, investment returns (good and bad) flow directly to members' balances. So the trustee's job is less about protecting its own capital and more about investment governance: setting suitable investment objectives and strategies for each option, managing risk against those objectives, and stress testing. APRA's SPS 530 Investment Governance sets requirements for this.

Liquidity risk

In plain English: you are worth more than you owe, but you can't get enough cash in time to pay the bills that are due.

Liquidity risk comes in two flavours: funding liquidity risk (you can't raise cash, for example because depositors withdraw or lenders won't roll over funding) and market liquidity risk (you can only sell assets at a steep discount because there are few buyers).

It is dangerous because it can move very fast. A rumour can trigger a run on deposits within days, and a solvent institution can fail simply because it runs out of cash. That is why regulators treat it so seriously.

  • Banks: under APS 210 Liquidity, larger ADIs must meet a Liquidity Coverage Ratio of at least 100%, meaning enough high-quality liquid assets to survive a severe 30-day stress scenario, and a Net Stable Funding Ratio of at least 100%, meaning longer-term assets are funded by stable sources. Smaller ADIs are subject to a simpler minimum liquidity holdings requirement.
  • Super funds: trustees must be able to pay benefits, rollovers and investment switches. SPS 530 requires a board-approved liquidity management plan and liquidity stress testing for each fund. The COVID-19 early release of super scheme in 2020 was a real-world test of fund liquidity.
  • Insurers: must be able to pay claims when due, including after a catastrophe such as a major flood.

How they connect

Financial risks rarely arrive alone. In the 2008 global financial crisis, falling US house prices caused credit losses, which caused market prices of related securities to collapse, which made it hard for banks to raise funding, turning a credit problem into a liquidity crisis. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank in the United States failed after rising interest rates reduced the value of its bond holdings (market risk), and a rapid withdrawal of deposits (liquidity risk) forced it to sell those bonds at a loss.

This is why enterprise risk management and stress testing look at risks together, not only one at a time.
