Control design and testing

A control is only worth having if it targets the right risk, is performed consistently, and can be shown to work. This page covers how to design controls well and how to test them.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • A control is any measure that changes a risk. Controls can prevent, detect or correct problems, and can be manual or automated.
  • A well-designed control addresses a specific cause, and says clearly who does what, when, how, what evidence is kept, and what happens with exceptions.
  • Testing checks two things: design effectiveness (would it work?) and operating effectiveness (did it work, consistently, over time?).
  • APRA's CPS 230 requires regulated entities to regularly monitor, review and test controls for design and operating effectiveness, report results to senior management, and fix gaps in a timely way.
  • Failed tests are valuable. The real output of testing is understanding why a control failed and fixing the root cause.

Types of control

TypeWhat it doesExamples
PreventiveStops the problem happening.System blocks a payment over an approval limit; segregation of duties; mandatory fields on a form; access restrictions.
DetectiveFinds the problem after it happens, so it can be fixed quickly.Daily reconciliations; exception reports; quality assurance sampling; review of access logs.
CorrectiveLimits the damage and restores normal operations.Backup and restore; incident response plans; customer remediation processes.
DirectiveTells people what to do.Policies, procedures, training, codes of conduct. Necessary but weak on their own.

Controls also differ by how they are performed:

  • Automated controls are performed by a system. Once working, they operate consistently, but they depend on general IT controls (such as change management and access security) remaining effective.
  • Manual controls are performed by people. They are flexible but more prone to error, fatigue and being skipped under pressure.
  • IT-dependent manual controls combine the two: for example, a person reviews a system-generated exception report. The control is only as good as the report's completeness and accuracy.

A key control is one that, alone or with a few others, is essential to keeping a significant risk within appetite. Key controls get the most testing attention.

Seeing controls with a bow-tie

Bow-tie analysis is a simple way to check whether you have the right controls in the right places.

Anatomy of a well-designed control

A control description should let someone who has never seen the process understand exactly what happens, and let a tester check it. A useful checklist is who, what, when, how, evidence and exceptions.

ElementWeak descriptionStrong description
Who"Operations""The Contributions Team Leader (or delegate when on leave)"
What"Reviews contributions""Reconciles total contributions received in the bank account against total contributions allocated to member accounts"
When"Regularly""Each business day by 11 am for the previous day's receipts"
How / precision"Checks for issues""Investigates any difference over $100 or any item unallocated for more than 2 business days"
Evidence(none)"Signs off the reconciliation in the workflow system, attaching the variance report"
Exceptions(none)"Unresolved differences over $10,000 or older than 5 days are escalated to the Head of Operations and logged as an incident"

Assessing design effectiveness

Before testing whether a control operates, ask whether it could work if performed perfectly. Try the checklist.

Control design self-check

Testing controls

Control testing checks whether controls are designed and operating effectively. Under APRA's CPS 230, regulated entities must regularly monitor, review and test their controls for design and operating effectiveness, with frequency matched to the materiality of the risks; report results to senior management; and rectify gaps in a timely way.

The testing process

  1. Plan

    Pick controls to test based on risk (key controls first), define the test objective, the period covered and the population.

  2. Understand and walk through

    Confirm how the control really works by following one transaction end to end. This tests design and often reveals that practice differs from the procedure.

  3. Test operating effectiveness

    Select a sample (or the whole population using data analytics) and check evidence that the control operated correctly each time.

  4. Evaluate exceptions

    For every failure: was it a one-off or systemic? What was the root cause? Did it cause harm (a possible incident or breach)?

  5. Rate and report

    Conclude on effectiveness, agree actions with owners, report to management and track actions to closure.

Testing methods

MethodWhat it involvesStrength of evidence
InquiryAsking the control owner how the control works.Weakest. Never enough on its own.
ObservationWatching the control being performed.Shows it happens at that moment only.
InspectionExamining evidence (sign-offs, reports, system logs) for a sample of occurrences.Good, if the evidence is reliable.
Re-performanceIndependently performing the control again and comparing results.Strong.
Data analyticsTesting 100% of transactions with data tools, for example checking every payment against approval limits.Strong and complete, if data is complete and accurate.

Rating the result

RatingTypical meaning
EffectiveWell designed and operated as intended throughout the period, with no (or only trivial, explained) exceptions.
Partially effectiveSome design gaps or exceptions; the risk is still largely managed but improvement is needed.
IneffectiveSignificant design flaw or repeated failure; the control cannot be relied on and residual risk should be re-assessed.

From test failure to incident

A failed control test can reveal that harm has already happened, for example payments made without approval. That makes it a potential incident or breach, which needs to be assessed under the incident and breach process, not just recorded as a testing finding. CPS 230 also requires identified control gaps and weaknesses to be included in the entity's operational risk profile until they are fixed.

Who tests?

  • First line control owners and business quality teams perform control self-assessment and quality checks.
  • Second line risk and compliance teams run independent monitoring and testing programs, and challenge first-line results.
  • Third line internal audit tests controls as part of its independent assurance, and may rely on others' work if it has checked its quality.
  • External assurance, for example reports on controls at service organisations prepared under the Australian standard ASAE 3402 (and, for investment services such as custody and administration, guidance statement GS 007). These help organisations get comfort over controls run by service providers.

Coordinating these (sometimes called "combined assurance" or assurance mapping) avoids both gaps and duplicated testing.

Check your understanding

Preventive or detective?

1. The payments system will not release any payment over $50,000 until a second authorised person approves it.

It stops an unapproved payment before it happens: preventive (and automated).

2. Each month, a manager reviews a report of all new user accounts created in the admin system.

It finds inappropriate access after it has been granted: detective (and IT-dependent manual, since it relies on the report).

3. Quality assurance checks a 5% sample of processed claims each week.

It catches errors after processing: detective. Note it only covers a sample.

4. The person who sets up a new supplier in the finance system cannot also approve payments to suppliers.

Segregation of duties prevents one person from creating and paying a fake supplier: preventive.
