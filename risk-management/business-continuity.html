Business continuity

Disruptions will happen. Business continuity is about making sure the services people depend on keep running, or come back fast enough, when they do.

Intermediate About 21 minutes Last reviewed: 7 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Business continuity management plans for the impact of disruption, whatever the cause, so that critical services continue at an acceptable level.
  • The modern focus is operational resilience: assume disruption will happen, and make sure you can stay within tolerance through it.
  • Under APRA's CPS 230, regulated entities must identify critical operations, set board-approved tolerance levels, and maintain and test a credible business continuity plan.
  • A business impact analysis works out how quickly harm builds up and what each service depends on. RTOs and RPOs must fit inside tolerance levels.
  • A plan that hasn't been tested with severe but plausible scenarios, including loss of a key service provider, shouldn't be relied on.

Related ideas, and how they differ

TermFocus
Business continuity management (BCM)Keeping important business activities going, or recovering them quickly, after a disruption.
Operational resilienceThe broader ability to prevent, adapt to, respond to and recover from disruption while continuing to deliver critical operations. It starts from the customer's point of view and assumes disruption will happen.
Disaster recovery (DR)Restoring IT systems and data after a failure. A key part of continuity, but only one part.
Crisis managementHow senior leaders make decisions, communicate and coordinate during a major event.
Incident managementResponding to and managing individual incidents. See Incident and breach management.

What CPS 230 requires

Since 1 July 2025, APRA's CPS 230 has set business continuity requirements for banks, insurers and super trustees, replacing the earlier CPS 232 and SPS 232. In summary, an entity must:

  • Identify its critical operations: processes that, if disrupted beyond tolerance, would have a material adverse impact on depositors, policyholders, beneficiaries or other customers, or on the entity's role in the financial system. Some operations must be treated as critical unless the entity can justify otherwise, such as payments for banks, claims processing for insurers, and investment management and fund administration for super trustees.
  • Set tolerance levels, approved by the board, for each critical operation, covering the maximum period of disruption it would tolerate, the maximum data loss it would accept, and the minimum service levels it would maintain while operating under alternative arrangements.
  • Maintain a credible business continuity plan that sets out how it will maintain critical operations within tolerance through severe disruptions, including triggers for activating the plan, actions to take, resources needed and a communications strategy.
  • Test the plan through a systematic testing program covering all critical operations, including an annual business continuity exercise using a range of severe but plausible scenarios, including disruptions to service providers.
  • Notify APRA as soon as possible, and no later than 24 hours, after a disruption to a critical operation outside tolerance.

Business impact analysis and recovery objectives

A business impact analysis (BIA) asks, for each important service: how fast does harm build up if it stops, what does it depend on (people, systems, data, premises, suppliers), and how quickly must it be restored? The results produce recovery objectives:

Maximum tolerable period of disruption (or CPS 230 tolerance level for time)
The longest the service can be down before the harm becomes unacceptable.
Recovery time objective (RTO)
The target time to restore the service or system. It must be shorter than the tolerance, leaving a safety margin.
Recovery point objective (RPO)
How much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. An RPO of 15 minutes means data must be backed up or replicated at least every 15 minutes.
Minimum service level
The reduced level of service you will maintain while running on workarounds, for example "urgent benefit payments within 2 business days".

What goes in a business continuity plan

  • Scope: the critical operations covered, their tolerance levels and key dependencies (mapped end to end, including service providers).
  • Activation: clear triggers, who can activate the plan, and how.
  • Roles: a crisis or incident management team with named people and deputies.
  • Response actions: workarounds and recovery steps for each critical operation, for loss of people, premises, technology, data or a key supplier.
  • Resources: alternative sites, backup systems, contact lists, and what's needed to execute.
  • Communications: who tells what to staff, customers, service providers, regulators and the media, and when.
  • Return to normal: how to restore normal operations and reconcile any backlog or data gaps.

Testing

Test typeWhat happensConfidence it gives
Plan review / walkthroughParticipants read through the plan step by step to check it is complete and current.Low
Tabletop exerciseA facilitated discussion of a realistic scenario, with "injects" (new developments) that force decisions.Medium: tests decision-making and roles
SimulationA more realistic rehearsal, often under time pressure, possibly involving service providers.Medium to high
Technical recovery testActually failing over systems to backup sites or restoring data from backups.High for technology recovery
Live testRunning a real process on alternative arrangements (e.g. staff working from an alternate site).High, but risky and costly

Good scenarios are severe but plausible: a ransomware attack that encrypts core systems and backups; the failure of a major administrator or cloud provider; a regional flood that closes the main office; a pandemic that takes out a large share of staff. Testing should be honest about what failed, and the lessons should be tracked to completion.

Try it: a tabletop exercise

Scenario: your fund administrator goes down

You are on the crisis management team of a super trustee. At 8:30 am on a Monday, your outsourced administrator reports a ransomware attack. Its systems are offline and it can't say when they'll be back. Member payments and contributions processing are both critical operations, with a tolerance of 2 business days for benefit payments.

Inject 1 (8:45 am): What should happen first?

A disruption to critical operations with no restoration time is a clear trigger. Activating early costs little; activating late can blow the tolerance.

Inject 2 (11:00 am): The administrator now estimates 4 to 5 business days to restore. What does that mean?

The estimate exceeds the 2-day tolerance. Use manual workarounds for urgent payments (e.g. hardship, death benefits) to maintain minimum service levels. Under CPS 230, APRA must be notified within 24 hours of a disruption to a critical operation outside tolerance, and a material operational risk incident within 72 hours of becoming aware.

Inject 3 (2:00 pm): The administrator says member personal information may have been taken. Which other obligation now comes into play?

The trustee remains responsible for members' information held by its service provider. It needs to assess whether this is an eligible data breach (OAIC), and whether it is a material information security incident (APRA CPS 234, within 72 hours).

Inject 4 (day 3): Members are posting on social media about delayed payments. What's the best communication approach?

Clear, regular communication reduces harm and complaints. The trustee is accountable to members even when a service provider caused the problem.

Standards and guidance

  • ISO 22301:2019 Security and resilience – Business continuity management systems – Requirements is the international standard for a business continuity management system. Unlike ISO 31000, organisations can be certified against it. An amendment on climate action was published in 2024.
  • APRA CPS 230 and CPG 230 set the requirements and APRA's expectations for regulated entities.
  • APRA CPS 234 covers information security, which is closely linked to technology recovery and cyber resilience.

Roles in business continuity

RoleResponsibility
BoardApproves tolerance levels and oversees business continuity; is ultimately accountable under CPS 230.
Executive owner of each critical operationOwns the plan, the tolerance evidence and fixing gaps.
Crisis management teamSenior leaders who make decisions and coordinate communications in a major disruption.
Business continuity coordinatorMaintains the framework, runs exercises, tracks lessons.
TechnologyDisaster recovery for systems and data, aligned to RTOs and RPOs.
Risk function and internal auditChallenge and independent assurance over the program.

Common mistakes

  • Plans built around buildings and IT, not around the critical operations customers rely on.
  • Recovery objectives that don't fit tolerances, for example an RTO longer than the tolerance level.
  • Exercises that are too easy, or never include the loss of a key service provider.
  • Lessons recorded but not fixed.
  • Contact lists and plans out of date, or only stored on systems that may be down.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Could we stay within tolerance for each critical operation in our worst plausible scenario?
  2. What did our last exercise show, and what has been fixed?
  3. Which service provider failures have we tested?
  4. Who decides to activate the plan, and how quickly can they be reached at 2am on a Sunday?

Frequently asked questions

Is disaster recovery the same as business continuity?

No. Disaster recovery restores technology and data. Business continuity covers the whole operation, including people, premises, suppliers and communications.

Do we need to be certified to ISO 22301?

No. ISO 22301 is a voluntary standard that some organisations use as a framework. APRA-regulated entities must meet CPS 230, whether or not they use ISO 22301. See ISO 22301.

How often must the plan be tested?

CPS 230 requires a systematic testing program and an annual business continuity exercise using severe but plausible scenarios. Many organisations also run smaller tests through the year.
