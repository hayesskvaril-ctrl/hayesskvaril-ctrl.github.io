Risk appetite and tolerance

Risk appetite is the board's answer to the question "how much risk are we prepared to take, and of what kinds?" Tolerances and limits turn that answer into numbers people can manage to.

Intermediate About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Risk capacity is the most risk you could bear. Risk appetite is how much you choose to take. Tolerances and limits are the measurable boundaries.
  • APRA-regulated banks, insurers and super trustees must have a board-approved risk appetite statement (CPS 220 / SPS 220) that sets tolerances for each material risk and explains how breaches are monitored and acted on.
  • A good statement combines qualitative statements (what we will and won't do) with quantitative metrics and early-warning triggers.
  • Appetite only works if it cascades into business decisions, limits and key risk indicators, and if breaches actually lead to action.
  • Terms are used inconsistently across frameworks. Always check how your organisation and regulator define them.

The idea in plain English

Think about a household budget. Your capacity is the absolute most you could spend before you couldn't pay the mortgage. Your appetite is how much you're comfortable spending on, say, holidays, well inside that capacity. Your limit is the hard number you've agreed ("no more than $5,000 a year"), and you might set an early-warning trigger ("if we hit $4,000 by October, we talk about it").

Organisations do the same thing for risk. The board decides how much risk, of each type, the organisation is willing to take to achieve its strategy, then management turns that into measures it can track.

The key terms

Risk capacity
The maximum risk the organisation could absorb before it breached regulatory minimums (such as capital requirements), could no longer meet its obligations to customers or members, or failed.
Risk appetite
The aggregate amount and types of risk the organisation is willing to take to achieve its strategy, set within risk capacity. The Financial Stability Board's 2013 principles describe it in these terms.
Risk tolerance
In APRA's CPS 220, the maximum level of risk the institution is willing to operate within for each material risk, expressed as a risk limit. Some other frameworks use "tolerance" to mean the acceptable variation around appetite.
Risk limits and triggers
More detailed, measurable thresholds allocated to business units, products or activities. Triggers (often "amber") are set before limits (often "red") so that problems are caught early.
Risk profile
Where the organisation's risks actually sit today. Comparing profile with appetite is the whole point.

Watch out: words vary

"Tolerance" means different things in different places. APRA's CPS 220 uses it for the maximum level of each material risk. APRA's CPS 230 separately uses "tolerance levels" for the maximum acceptable disruption to a critical operation. Some organisations use "tolerance" to mean a buffer zone above appetite. None of these is wrong, but mixing them causes confusion, so define terms in your framework and stick to them.

What APRA requires

APRA's Prudential Standard CPS 220 Risk Management (for banks and insurers) and SPS 220 (for super trustees) require a board-approved risk appetite statement as part of the risk management framework. Under CPS 220, the statement must cover, among other things:

  • the degree of risk the institution is prepared to accept in pursuit of its strategic objectives and business plan, considering the interests of depositors and/or policyholders;
  • for each material risk, the maximum level of risk it is willing to operate within (the risk tolerance), expressed as a risk limit and based on its risk appetite, risk profile and capital strength;
  • the process for making sure tolerances are set at an appropriate level, based on the likely impact if a tolerance is breached and the likelihood of each material risk occurring; and
  • the process for monitoring compliance with each tolerance and for taking appropriate action if one is breached.

APRA's prudential practice guide CPG 220 gives more detail on its expectations. SPS 220 contains broadly equivalent requirements for super, framed around the interests of beneficiaries. Always read the current standard itself for the precise wording.

From board statement to everyday decisions

Monitoring and escalation

A statement on a shelf achieves nothing. Organisations track their metrics regularly, usually on a traffic-light dashboard, and have agreed escalation rules for what happens at each threshold. Try it below.

What good escalation looks like

  • Clear ownership: each metric has a named owner responsible for monitoring and acting.
  • Defined timeframes: for example, an amber trigger is reported to the relevant executive within 5 business days with an action plan; a red breach goes to the CRO immediately and to the board risk committee at its next meeting (or sooner if severe).
  • Decisions, not just reports: the response to a breach should be a decision: bring the risk back within tolerance, or formally accept it for a period (risk acceptance), or change the appetite.
  • No "watermelons": metrics that are green on the outside but red on the inside. If a metric is always green while incidents keep happening, it is probably measuring the wrong thing.

Common pitfalls

"Zero appetite" for everything

Saying you have "zero appetite" for a risk you cannot avoid (errors, cyber attacks, breaches) sets up a statement that is breached constantly and loses meaning. A more useful approach separates what is truly unacceptable ("no appetite for deliberate breaches of the law") from what must be minimised ("low tolerance for errors that affect members, measured by…").

Metrics that don't match the statement

If the words say "low appetite for poor member outcomes" but the only metric is a count of complaints, most of the risk is unmeasured. Each statement should have metrics that genuinely reflect it.

Set and forget

Appetite should be reviewed when strategy, the environment or the risk profile changes, not just ticked off annually.

Appetite that doesn't influence decisions

A good test: can you point to a recent decision (a product, a supplier, an investment) where risk appetite changed the outcome? If not, the framework may be for show. Researchers have criticised narrow, box-ticking versions of risk appetite as contributing to a false sense of security before the global financial crisis (Power, 2009).
