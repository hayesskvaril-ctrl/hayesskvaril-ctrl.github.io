The economics of cyber risk

Why cyber security is as much about incentives as technology, how much to spend on it, and why cyber losses are so hard to measure and insure.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Many security failures are failures of incentives: the party who could prevent harm often isn't the one who bears it.
  • The Gordon-Loeb model suggests that, under its assumptions, it rarely makes sense to spend more than about 37% of the expected loss on protecting an information set.
  • Cyber losses are heavy-tailed: most incidents are cheap, but a few are extreme, so averages mislead.
  • Share-price reactions to serious breaches can far exceed the direct costs.
  • Cyber risk is hard to insure because losses can be correlated, data is thin and insurers know less than the insured.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain how incentives and externalities shape cyber security outcomes.
  2. Apply the Gordon-Loeb model to a security investment decision and describe its limits.
  3. Interpret evidence on the distribution of cyber losses and its implications for risk measurement.
  4. Critically evaluate cyber insurance as a risk transfer tool.

The idea in plain English

Cyber risk is usually discussed as a technical problem: patching, passwords and firewalls. Economists add a different lens. They ask who has the incentive to invest in security, how much investment is worth it, and what incidents actually cost. The answers help boards set risk appetite for cyber, decide on budgets and judge whether insurance is worth buying. This page builds on cyber risk and CPS 234.

Theory and research

Incentives and externalities

Anderson and Moore (2006) argued that security fails as often because of misaligned incentives as because of poor technology. When the party who could prevent a breach doesn't bear its cost, for example a software vendor whose product is exploited, or a company whose customers carry the fallout of a data breach, too little is spent on security. Economists call this an externality. It helps explain why regulators impose security and notification duties, such as APRA's CPS 234 and the notifiable data breaches scheme under the Privacy Act.

How much to invest: the Gordon-Loeb model

Gordon and Loeb (2002) modelled the choice of how much to spend protecting a set of information. The expected benefit of investment is the reduction in expected loss (the chance of a breach multiplied by the loss). Because each extra dollar of security buys less protection than the last, the best investment is where the last dollar spent reduces expected loss by exactly one dollar. For the broad class of functions they studied, the optimal investment never exceeds about 37% (1/e) of the expected loss.

Worked example (illustrative). Suppose a member database has a 20% chance a year of a breach costing $5 million if it happens. The expected loss is $1 million a year. Under the Gordon-Loeb result, the best annual investment in protecting it would be no more than about $370,000 (37% of $1 million). The real figure depends on how effective extra spending is, which is hard to estimate, and on losses the model leaves out, such as regulatory action and loss of trust.

What incidents actually cost

Romanosky (2016) analysed a large dataset of cyber incidents and found the typical cost was modest compared with headline figures, which may help explain why some firms under-invest. But averages hide the tail. Eling and Wirfs (2019) applied extreme value methods to operational loss data and found cyber losses are heavy-tailed: most events are small, while a few extreme events drive total losses. This matters for measurement: a model built on the average incident will badly understate the risk (see quantitative operational risk).

Reputation and market reactions

Kamiya et al. (2021) studied successful cyberattacks on listed US firms and found that attacks exposing personal financial information led to shareholder losses much larger than the direct costs, and that affected firms tended to strengthen their risk management afterwards. The indirect cost of lost trust can dominate.

Can cyber risk be insured?

Biener et al. (2015) analysed 994 cyber loss events and assessed cyber risk against the standard criteria for insurability. They identified problems including correlated losses (one attack or outage can hit many policyholders at once), a lack of good data, and information asymmetry between insurers and the insured. Insurance can transfer part of the loss, but it does not transfer regulatory accountability.

Critical perspectives

  • Models need inputs no one has. Gordon-Loeb assumes you know the breach probability and how it falls with spending. In practice these are guesses, so the model is better at framing the decision than producing a number.
  • Data quality. Public incident datasets over-represent incidents that must be disclosed, and costs are often estimated rather than observed.
  • Systemic risk. Firm-level economics can miss losses that spread across many firms through shared providers, a concern behind concentration risk work.
  • Security is not only a cost. Treating security purely as an expense to minimise can overlook its role in trust and resilience.

Seminar questions

  1. Identify an externality in cyber security affecting super fund members. Who bears the cost, and what could change the incentives?
  2. Use the Gordon-Loeb framework to argue for or against a proposed increase in a fund's security budget. Which inputs are you least sure about?
  3. Eling and Wirfs find cyber losses are heavy-tailed. How should this change the way a board sets cyber risk appetite and tolerances?
  4. Given Biener and colleagues' findings, when is cyber insurance a sensible part of a cyber risk strategy, and when is it not?

Further reading

  • Anderson, R., & Moore, T. (2006). The economics of information security. Science, 314(5799), 610–613. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1130992 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that security failures are often failures of incentives rather than technology, for example when the party who could prevent harm is not the one who bears it.
  • Gordon, L. A., & Loeb, M. P. (2002). The economics of information security investment. ACM Transactions on Information and System Security, 5(4), 438–457. https://doi.org/10.1145/581271.581274 Peer-reviewed
    The Gordon-Loeb model: under its assumptions, the optimal investment to protect information never exceeds about 37% of the expected loss from a breach.
  • Eling, M., & Wirfs, J. (2019). What are the actual costs of cyber risk events? European Journal of Operational Research, 272(3), 1109–1119. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2018.07.021 Peer-reviewed
    Uses operational loss data and extreme value methods to show cyber losses are heavy-tailed: most are small, but a few extreme events dominate, so averages understate the risk.
  • Biener, C., Eling, M., & Wirfs, J. H. (2015). Insurability of cyber risk: An empirical analysis. The Geneva Papers on Risk and Insurance – Issues and Practice, 40(1), 131–158. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2577286 Peer-reviewed
    Analyses 994 cyber loss events and discusses what limits the insurability of cyber risk, including correlated losses, lack of data and information asymmetry.
  • Kamiya, S., Kang, J.-K., Kim, J., Milidonis, A., & Stulz, R. M. (2021). Risk management, firm reputation, and the impact of successful cyberattacks on target firms. Journal of Financial Economics, 139(3), 719–749. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.05.019 Peer-reviewed
    Successful cyberattacks that expose personal financial information cause shareholder losses far larger than the direct costs, and affected firms tend to strengthen risk management afterwards.
  • Romanosky, S. (2016). Examining the costs and causes of cyber incidents. Journal of Cybersecurity, 2(2), 121–135. https://doi.org/10.1093/cybsec/tyw001 Peer-reviewed
    Analyses a large dataset of cyber incidents and their costs, finding the typical cost smaller than often claimed, which raises questions about firms' incentives to invest in security.
