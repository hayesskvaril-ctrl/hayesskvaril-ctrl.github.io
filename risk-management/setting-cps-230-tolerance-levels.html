Setting CPS 230 tolerance levels

A practical method for deciding how long a critical operation can be disrupted, how much data can be lost, and what service must continue, with worked examples.

Advanced About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Under CPS 230, every critical operation needs board-approved tolerance levels for three things: the maximum period of disruption, the maximum extent of data loss, and the minimum service levels while operating under alternative arrangements.
  • Set tolerances from the outside in: start with the harm to customers or members, legal obligations and the wider system, and find the point where harm becomes unacceptable. Don't start from what current systems can achieve.
  • A tolerance is only credible if testing shows the entity can stay within it in severe but plausible scenarios, including when a key service provider fails.
  • If capability falls short, good practice is to close the gap by improving recovery or contingency arrangements, not by loosening the tolerance.
  • Document the reasoning and evidence, and revisit tolerances after real disruptions, tests and changes to the business.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the three dimensions of a CPS 230 tolerance level: maximum disruption time, maximum data loss and minimum service levels.
  2. Set a tolerance level by assessing harm to members or customers over time, using the builder on this page.
  3. Relate tolerance levels to research on operational resilience and organisational response to adversity.
  4. Critically assess the judgements and evidence behind tolerance levels, and why they should not simply be set from current recovery capability.

This page builds on Business continuity and the CPS 230 explainer. It describes one practical method. APRA's standard sets the requirements and its guide, CPG 230, gives guidance, but neither prescribes a single method. Entities take different approaches, and practice is still maturing.

The three dimensions of a tolerance level

DimensionThe question it answersTypical form
Maximum period of disruptionHow long can this operation be unavailable before the harm is unacceptable?Hours or business days, e.g. "benefit payments resumed within 2 business days".
Maximum extent of data lossHow much data can be lost or corrupted, and still be recovered or reconstructed, without unacceptable harm?A period of transactions, or a statement that no completed transactions may be lost.
Minimum service levelsWhat must keep running, and at what level, while on workarounds?A list of priority services and volumes, e.g. "death and hardship benefit payments continue manually".

A step-by-step method

  1. Define the operation from the customer's point of view. Describe the outcome it delivers ("members receive their benefit payments") rather than a system or team. Map it end to end: people, processes, technology, data, premises and service providers.
  2. Identify who is harmed and how. Consider customers or members (especially vulnerable people who depend on the payment), legal and regulatory obligations, the entity's finances, and its role in the financial system.
  3. Assess harm over time. For a series of time points (hours for fast operations, business days for slower ones), judge how severe the harm would be. Use evidence: complaints and incident history, legal deadlines, payment cycles and industry events.
  4. Find the intolerable point, then set the tolerance before it. The maximum period of disruption must end before harm becomes intolerable, with some headroom for uncertainty.
  5. Set the data loss tolerance. Work out which data could be reconstructed (for example from bank files, employer contribution files or a provider's records) and which could not. For many financial transactions the tolerance is that no completed transaction may be permanently lost.
  6. Set minimum service levels. Decide what must continue during the disruption, at what volume, and how (workarounds, manual processing, alternative providers).
  7. Test achievability. Compare with tested recovery times and contingency arrangements, including scenarios where a key provider fails. Record any gaps and the plan to close them.
  8. Approve, document and review. The board approves the tolerances. Keep the rationale and evidence, and review after real disruptions, tests, and business or provider changes.

Don't work backwards from capability

A common mistake is to set the tolerance at whatever the current system recovery time happens to be. That makes every tolerance "achievable" but tells the board nothing about harm. The tolerance should reflect harm; the recovery time should then be designed and tested to fit inside it.

Worked example 1: a super fund's benefit payments

Illustrative only. Every fund needs its own analysis. Under CPS 230, fund administration and investment management are deemed critical operations for RSE licensees unless the licensee can justify otherwise. Paying benefits is a core part of administration.

5 business daysWidespread hardship and complaints.Breaches of the rollover timeframe likely across many requests.Intolerable

Resulting tolerances (illustrative):

  • Maximum period of disruption: 2 business days, leaving headroom before the severe harm and legal deadlines at 3 business days.
  • Maximum data loss: no completed payments, contributions or rollovers permanently lost; transactions since the last backup can be reconstructed from bank and clearing house records within the recovery period.
  • Minimum service levels: death benefit, terminal medical condition and financial hardship payments continue by manual processing from day 1; pension payments are made on schedule using the previous cycle's file if needed.

Worked example 2: a bank's retail payments

For ADIs, payments are deemed a critical operation under CPS 230 unless justified otherwise. Harm builds much faster than in super, so the time points are hours rather than days.

Evidence APRA and auditors may expect to see

  • A register of critical operations, with the reasoning for any deemed operation the entity judges not to be critical.
  • For each tolerance: the impact-over-time analysis, the data and judgement used, and the board approval.
  • End-to-end mapping showing the resources and service providers each operation depends on.
  • Test results showing recovery within tolerance in a range of severe but plausible scenarios, and plans to close any gaps.
  • Records of reviews after incidents, and of APRA notifications: CPS 230 requires notification within 24 hours of a disruption to a critical operation outside tolerance.

Common pitfalls

  • One tolerance for everything. Different operations harm people at very different speeds.
  • Tolerances set by IT. System recovery objectives are inputs, not the tolerance. The business, which understands customer harm, should lead.
  • Ignoring minimum service levels. They are often the most useful dimension in a real disruption, because they say what to prioritise.
  • No headroom. A tolerance set exactly at the intolerable point leaves no margin for the uncertainty in any real incident.
  • Never revisited. Tolerances should change when the business, member base, providers or technology change.

Theory and research

Tolerance as a statement about harm, not capability

A tolerance level asks when disruption to a critical operation becomes unacceptable to the people who rely on it. This reverses the traditional continuity question, which started from how quickly systems could be recovered. The historical review by Herbane (2010) shows why the shift happened: continuity management moved from recovering technology to protecting the services an organisation provides, under pressure from major disruptions and regulators. Setting tolerance from harm, and then testing whether recovery capability meets it, is what gives the concept its force.

What resilience research suggests

Research distinguishes operational resilience, keeping critical processes running, from strategic resilience, adapting to change (Annarelli & Nonino, 2016). Tolerance levels are an operational tool, but the research warns against treating them as purely technical targets. Williams et al. (2017) show that an organisation's response to adversity depends on capabilities built before the event: resources held in reserve, practised routines and the ability to improvise. A tolerance that is only met on paper, by a recovery plan never tested at scale, gives little assurance. Linnenluecke (2017) notes that resilience research has little agreement on how to measure resilience, which is one reason regulators focus on scenario testing against tolerance levels rather than a single metric.

Critical perspectives

  • Harm is hard to quantify. How long is too long for a death benefit payment, or a hardship release? The answer involves judgements about vulnerable people that data only partly informs. Documenting who made the judgement, and on what evidence, matters.
  • Anchoring on current capability. The easiest tolerance to "meet" is one set from the current recovery time. That defeats the purpose. Watch for tolerances that match existing plans suspiciously closely.
  • One number hides variation. A single maximum disruption time may be tolerable for most members but not for those in hardship or awaiting a death benefit. Minimum service levels during disruption help address this.
  • Limited evidence base. Impact tolerances are a recent regulatory concept, and we found little published research testing how well they work. Much of the practice is still developing.

Seminar questions

  1. Use the builder to set a tolerance for a critical operation you know. At what point does harm become intolerable, and for whom? Would your answer change for vulnerable members?
  2. Why should a tolerance level be set from harm rather than from recovery capability? What happens when the two don't match?
  3. Williams and colleagues emphasise capabilities built before a crisis. What capabilities would you need to meet the benefit payment tolerance in worked example 1?
  4. How would you present tolerance levels to a board so that directors can challenge the judgements behind them?

  • Herbane, B. (2010). The evolution of business continuity management: A historical review of practices and drivers. Business History, 52(6), 978–1002. https://doi.org/10.1080/00076791.2010.511185 Peer-reviewed
    Traces business continuity management from 1970s IT disaster recovery to an organisation-wide discipline, showing how major events and regulation drove each phase and how 'resilience' became the organising idea.
  • Williams, T. A., Gruber, D. A., Sutcliffe, K. M., Shepherd, D. A., & Zhao, E. Y. (2017). Organizational response to adversity: Fusing crisis management and resilience research streams. Academy of Management Annals, 11(2), 733–769. https://doi.org/10.5465/annals.2015.0134 Peer-reviewed
    Brings together crisis management and resilience research to explain how organisations anticipate, respond to and recover from adversity, and why capabilities built before a crisis shape the response.
  • Annarelli, A., & Nonino, F. (2016). Strategic and operational management of organizational resilience: Current state of research and future directions. Omega, 62, 1–18. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omega.2015.08.004 Peer-reviewed
    A systematic review and co-citation analysis of organisational resilience research, distinguishing strategic resilience (preparing and adapting) from operational resilience (keeping critical processes running).
