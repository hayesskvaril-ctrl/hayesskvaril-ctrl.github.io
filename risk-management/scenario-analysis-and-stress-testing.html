Scenario analysis and stress testing

How organisations test what would happen in a severe but plausible crisis, and work backwards from failure to find the scenarios that would break them.

Advanced About 27 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Scenario analysis asks "what if this specific event happened?" Stress testing measures the impact on capital, liquidity, operations or members. Reverse stress testing starts from failure and asks what would cause it.
  • Good scenarios are severe but plausible: bad enough to test resilience, realistic enough that people take them seriously.
  • APRA requires scenarios and stress testing in several standards, including CPS 230 (business continuity), CPS 190 (recovery and exit planning), the ICAAP for banks and insurers, and SPS 530 for super investment options.
  • The value is in the decisions a test leads to: changes to limits, buffers, contingency plans or strategy. A test that changes nothing has usually been too mild.
  • Common failures are optimistic assumptions, scenarios built in silos, and giving too much credit for management actions that might not work in a real crisis.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish sensitivity analysis, scenario analysis, stress testing and reverse stress testing, and explain what each can and can't show.
  2. Design a severe but plausible scenario and explain how to make 'plausible' systematic rather than arbitrary.
  3. Evaluate the research evidence on whether stress tests work as early warning tools.
  4. Critically assess the organisational and behavioural limits of scenario work.

The toolkit, from simple to demanding

What "severe but plausible" means

APRA uses the phrase "severe but plausible" throughout its standards. It has no precise definition, which is deliberate: it requires judgement. In practice it means:

  • Severe: far worse than a normal bad year. The scenario should seriously test buffers, contingency plans and people, not just confirm that things are fine.
  • Plausible: it could realistically happen. It is grounded in history, in events at other organisations, or in a credible chain of causes. It is not science fiction.
  • Relevant: tailored to the organisation's own business model, dependencies and weak points, not a generic industry scenario.

A useful check: if the board reads the scenario and says "that could never happen to us", either the scenario is implausible or the board is overconfident. Both are worth a conversation.

Building a scenario, step by step

  1. Choose the risk to test. Start from the risk profile, emerging risks, recent near misses and external events. Include combinations: crises rarely arrive one at a time.
  2. Write the narrative. A short story of what happens, in what order, over what period. Specific details (which system, which supplier, which market) make it concrete.
  3. Calibrate the severity. Put numbers on it using history (for example, past market falls or outage durations), external events and expert judgement. Record the basis.
  4. Trace the impacts. Follow the effects through operations, customers or members, finances, liquidity, people, third parties, regulators and reputation. Look for knock-on effects.
  5. Compare with limits. Would the organisation stay within its risk appetite, capital and liquidity limits and tolerance levels?
  6. Test the responses. Which actions would be taken, how long would they take, and would they really work in a crisis? Be sceptical of actions that depend on markets or counterparties behaving normally.
  7. Decide and act. Record findings, agree actions (buffers, limits, plans, investments) and report to the board. Track the actions to completion.

Reverse stress testing

Ordinary stress tests start with a scenario and ask how bad the outcome is. Reverse stress tests start with an outcome the organisation could not accept, such as breaching minimum capital, running out of liquid assets, or failing to deliver a critical operation for weeks, and ask what would have to happen to get there. The Basel Committee's stress testing principles describe it as a way to identify vulnerabilities that forward-looking scenarios can miss.

The approach is powerful because it defeats a common bias: when people design scenarios, they tend to choose ones the organisation survives. Starting from failure forces the uncomfortable conversation.

Worked example: when would a fund breach its liquidity minimum?

A fictional $10 billion fund holds $1.2 billion in liquid assets and has a policy minimum of liquid assets equal to 5% of total assets. The grid below tests combinations of member outflows (switches out and rollovers, as a share of total assets) and falls in the value of the liquid assets.

Liquid assets as a share of total assets after the stress (policy minimum 5%). Illustrative.
OutflowsNo fall in liquid assetsLiquid assets fall 10%Liquid assets fall 20%
3% of assets9.3% (within)8.0% (within)6.8% (within)
5% of assets7.4% (within)6.1% (within)4.8% (breach)
7% of assets5.4% (within)4.1% (breach)2.8% (breach)

How the figures work: remaining liquid assets = $1.2b × (1 − fall) − outflows, divided by total assets after outflows. For example, 5% outflows ($500m) with a 20% fall: ($960m − $500m) ÷ $9.5b = 4.8%.

The key question is then one of judgement: how plausible are outflows of 5–7% of assets in a short period, combined with a sharp market fall? The answer draws on the fund's own membership, past events (such as the COVID-19 early release of super in 2020) and the scenario narrative. If the breach scenarios are plausible, the fund might hold a larger buffer, change its liquidity plan, or review options with illiquid assets.

Frequently asked questions

Why run system-wide tests if each entity already stress tests?

Each entity models its own reactions. When many react the same way at once, such as selling the same assets, the combined effect can be much worse. System-wide tests try to capture those second-round effects.

How severe should a scenario be?

Severe enough to test the entity's resilience, but plausible enough that management takes it seriously. Reverse stress testing approaches the question from the other end: what would break us?

Common weaknesses

  • Not severe enough. Scenarios quietly tuned so the organisation passes.
  • Silos. Operational, financial and liquidity teams each test their own scenario, missing the combined effect. See Risk aggregation and correlation.
  • Heroic management actions. Assuming assets can be sold at normal prices, or that a supplier will be replaced within days.
  • Static scenarios. Reusing last year's scenario without updating it for new risks, products or dependencies.
  • No link to decisions. Results are reported but don't change limits, buffers, plans or strategy.
  • Poor documentation. Assumptions and their basis aren't recorded, so results can't be challenged or reproduced.

Theory and research

Many techniques under one name

"Scenario analysis" covers very different practices. Bradfield et al. (2005) trace scenario methods back to military planning and Shell's corporate planning, and classify them into distinct schools with different purposes: some aim to explore possible futures and challenge assumptions, others to produce probabilistic forecasts. Regulatory stress testing borrows the language of the first and the machinery of the second. Being clear which one you are doing helps. A scenario meant to challenge a board's thinking should be judged by the questions it provokes, not by whether it "came true".

Choosing severe but plausible scenarios

Most scenarios are chosen by experts in workshops, which invites the biases described on the human factors page: availability (last year's crisis), anchoring (last year's scenario) and the inside view (Kahneman & Lovallo, 1993). Breuer et al. (2009) proposed a more systematic approach for financial risks: define a region of plausible scenarios statistically, then search within it for the scenario that does the most damage to the actual portfolio. Their point applies beyond financial models. Hand-picked scenarios often miss the combination of events that hurts most, which is also the logic behind reverse stress testing.

Do stress tests work?

Borio et al. (2014) asked whether macro stress tests live up to expectations. Their answer was sobering: tests run before the global financial crisis did not flag the vulnerabilities that mattered, because the models could not capture the non-linear, self-reinforcing dynamics of real crises, such as fire sales and funding runs. They concluded that stress tests are more useful for managing a crisis, for example to check whether banks have enough capital once trouble is visible, than for predicting one. For organisations running their own scenarios, the lesson is to treat results as a test of resilience under stated assumptions, not a forecast, and to look hardest at the feedback loops the model leaves out.

Critical perspectives

  • "Plausible" is a judgement. Severe-but-plausible has no objective definition. Too plausible, and the test reassures; too severe, and it is dismissed. Documenting why a scenario was chosen, and who challenged it, matters as much as the result.
  • Models miss feedback loops. Real crises involve behaviour changing in response to the crisis itself: customers leaving, counterparties withdrawing, staff unavailable. Few scenario models capture this, which is the core of the critique by Borio and colleagues.
  • Stress tests can become compliance exercises. When the main audience is the regulator, the incentive is to pass. The Common weaknesses list above describes the symptoms.
  • Operational scenarios have little data. Most research concerns financial stress tests. There is less evidence on how well operational and business continuity scenarios, such as those CPS 230 requires, predict real disruptions.

Seminar questions

  1. Choose an operational risk for an Australian super fund and write a severe but plausible scenario. How would you defend it against the charge that it is either too mild or too extreme?
  2. Borio and colleagues argue stress tests are better for crisis management than early warning. Does the same apply to CPS 230 scenario analysis? Why or why not?
  3. How could Breuer and colleagues' idea of a systematic search for the worst plausible scenario be adapted to non-financial risks?
  4. Reverse stress testing starts from failure and works backwards. What biases might this help overcome, and what new ones might it introduce?
