Climate risk management

How climate change creates financial risks for banks, insurers and super funds, and how those risks are identified, measured and managed within the normal risk framework.

Intermediate About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Climate risk has two main sources: physical risk (damage from extreme weather and long-term changes such as rising sea levels) and transition risk (the effects of moving to a lower-emissions economy, such as policy, technology and market changes).
  • These are not a new risk category so much as drivers of existing risks: credit, market, insurance, liquidity, operational and strategic risk.
  • APRA's prudential practice guide CPG 229 sets out its expectations for banks, insurers and super trustees on governance, risk management, scenario analysis and disclosure of climate risks.
  • Scenario analysis is the main tool, because climate risks play out over long horizons that historical data can't capture.
  • Climate risk management now connects directly to mandatory climate-related financial disclosures.

Physical and transition risk

TypeWhat it meansExamples
Physical: acuteDamage from extreme events.Floods, bushfires, cyclones, hailstorms.
Physical: chronicGradual shifts in climate.Sea level rise, higher average temperatures, changing rainfall affecting agriculture.
Transition: policy and legalNew laws and regulation to reduce emissions, and litigation.Carbon pricing or emissions limits, disclosure rules, climate-related lawsuits.
Transition: technologyLow-emissions technology replacing existing assets.Renewables and electric vehicles reducing demand for fossil fuels.
Transition: market and reputationChanging customer, investor and community expectations.Falling demand for emissions-intensive products; reputational damage from financing them.

The two types pull in opposite directions over time. A fast, orderly transition reduces long-term physical risk but increases short-term transition risk; a slow transition does the reverse. That is why scenario analysis tests both.

How climate risk becomes financial risk

International supervisors describe climate risk drivers flowing through "transmission channels" into the financial risks institutions already manage (Basel Committee, 2021).

What it looks like by sector

SectorMain exposures
BanksMortgages on properties exposed to flood, bushfire or coastal erosion; lending to emissions-intensive industries; agricultural lending affected by drought.
General insurersRising claims from extreme weather; reinsurance costs; premiums becoming unaffordable in high-risk areas, leading to under-insurance.
Super funds and investment managersInvestment values falling as markets reprice emissions-intensive assets ("stranded assets"); physical damage to property and infrastructure holdings; very long member time horizons.
All organisationsOperational disruption to offices, data centres and service providers from extreme weather; regulatory, disclosure and greenwashing risk.

Integrating climate into the risk framework

  1. Governance

    Board oversight and clear management accountability, with climate within the remit of the board risk committee; climate expertise on the board or accessible to it.

  2. Risk appetite and strategy

    How much climate exposure the organisation is prepared to accept, for example limits on lending to certain sectors or emissions intensity targets for portfolios.

  3. Identification and materiality

    Which physical and transition risks matter for this business model, over what time horizons, using a materiality assessment.

  4. Measurement

    Portfolio exposure data (locations, sectors, emissions), scenario analysis and stress testing.

  5. Management

    Pricing, underwriting and lending standards; engagement with investee companies or borrowers; diversification or divestment; operational resilience planning for extreme weather.

  6. Monitoring, reporting and disclosure

    Metrics and targets reported to the board and, under the new regime, publicly.

Scenario analysis for climate

Because climate risks unfold over decades and have no historical precedent, institutions rely on scenarios rather than statistical models built on past data. Many use the scenarios published by the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS), a group of central banks and supervisors. They broadly cover:

  • Orderly transition: early, steady policy action limits warming; transition risks are moderate and physical risks are lower.
  • Disorderly transition: action is delayed and then sudden, creating sharper transition shocks.
  • Hot house world: limited action; transition risk is low but physical risks become severe.

APRA has run its own exercises. Its first Climate Vulnerability Assessment, with the five largest banks, reported in 2022 that lending losses could rise in the medium to long term, and that while not expected to cause severe stress on their own, climate impacts could leave the banking system more vulnerable to future downturns. A later assessment for general insurers explored how the affordability of home insurance might change to 2050. For general guidance on building scenarios, see Scenario analysis and stress testing.

APRA's expectations: CPG 229

CPG 229 Climate Change Financial Risks, finalised in November 2021, is guidance: it doesn't create new requirements, but explains how APRA expects banks, insurers and super trustees to manage climate risks under their existing obligations in standards such as CPS 220. It covers:

  • Governance: the board's understanding and oversight of climate risks, and management's roles.
  • Risk management: identifying, measuring, monitoring and managing climate risks as part of the risk management framework.
  • Scenario analysis: using scenarios to assess resilience, proportionate to size and complexity.
  • Disclosure: APRA encouraged disclosure consistent with international frameworks. This has since been overtaken by the mandatory regime for entities above the thresholds.

Metrics commonly used

MetricWhat it shows
Financed or portfolio emissionsThe emissions attributable to loans or investments (part of scope 3 for financial institutions).
Emissions intensityEmissions per dollar invested or lent, allowing comparison across portfolios.
Exposure to emissions-intensive sectorsShare of lending or investment in sectors most exposed to transition risk.
Physical risk exposureShare of properties or assets in high-risk locations (flood, bushfire, coastal).
Scenario resultsEstimated losses or value changes under each scenario and time horizon.

Recent APRA work

  • Insurance Climate Vulnerability Assessment (March 2026): working with five large general insurers covering about 80% of the market, APRA modelled home insurance to 2050 under higher physical risk and higher transition cost scenarios. Under both, the share of homes without adequate insurance rose from about one in seven to about one in four by 2050, around one million more homes, with rural households worst affected. Weather losses drove the physical scenario; rising construction costs mattered more in the transition scenario.
  • Climate risk self-assessment surveys: APRA's second survey found climate risk maturity had improved at large banks since 2022 but was broadly unchanged at large insurers and super trustees. Entities were stronger on governance, strategy and risk management than on disclosure and metrics and targets.
  • System Risk Stress Test (2025, results June 2026): while not a climate exercise, it showed APRA's growing use of system-wide scenarios.

Worked example: transition risk in a super portfolio

A super fund's balanced option holds 3% of its assets in companies whose revenue depends heavily on thermal coal. The investment and risk teams look at the exposure under a fast transition scenario:

  1. Identify: holdings exposed to policy changes, such as carbon prices or falling demand.
  2. Estimate: under the scenario, these holdings fall in value by 40% over five years, reducing the option's value by about 1.2 percentage points, before any offsetting gains elsewhere.
  3. Compare: the trustee considers whether this fits its risk appetite and investment beliefs, and its obligations to act in members' best financial interests.
  4. Act and disclose: options include engagement, reducing the exposure gradually, or accepting it with monitoring. Climate disclosures under AASB S2 may also apply to large funds.

Frequently asked questions

Is climate risk a separate risk type?

Usually not. Most entities treat it as a driver of existing risks, such as credit, market, insurance, operational and strategic risk, which is the approach in APRA's CPG 229.

Is CPG 229 binding?

No. It is a practice guide setting out APRA's view of sound practice. The binding requirements come from standards such as CPS 220 and SPS 220.

Challenges

  • Data: emissions and location data for borrowers and investee companies is often incomplete or estimated.
  • Time horizons: climate impacts play out over decades, beyond typical planning and budgeting cycles.
  • Uncertainty: results depend heavily on scenario assumptions and models. Present them as ranges, not forecasts.
  • Greenwashing risk: public commitments and targets must be backed by credible plans. See Climate-related financial disclosures.
