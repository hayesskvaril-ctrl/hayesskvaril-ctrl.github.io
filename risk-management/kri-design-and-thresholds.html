KRI design and statistical thresholds

How to choose indicators that actually predict risk, set amber and red thresholds that mean something, and test whether a KRI works, with an interactive control chart.

Advanced About 21 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • A good KRI is linked to a specific risk, moves before harm happens, uses reliable data and triggers a defined response.
  • Combine methods: statistics show what is unusual, risk appetite shows what is unacceptable.
  • Thresholds based only on history can normalise an unacceptable level of risk.
  • Run rules catch gradual drift that single thresholds miss.
  • Test each KRI regularly: did it signal before problems (hits), signal without problems (false alarms), or miss problems entirely?

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain how key risk indicators connect to a causal model of a risk, and what makes an indicator 'leading'.
  2. Compare appetite-based, statistical, percentile and benchmark thresholds, and justify a combination for a given indicator.
  3. Apply control chart logic, including run rules, and explain the trade-off between false alarms and missed signals.
  4. Critically assess how measurement changes behaviour (Campbell's law) and the limited evidence that KRIs predict losses.

The idea in plain English

A key risk indicator is only useful if it changes before harm happens and someone acts when it does. Most KRI dashboards fail on one of three counts: the indicator doesn't actually predict the risk, the thresholds are set so wide that nothing ever turns red, or amber and red don't trigger any real decision.

This page covers how to choose indicators that carry signal, how to set thresholds using statistics and appetite together, and how to test whether a KRI works. It builds on risk appetite and tolerance and the KRI library template.

Four ways to set thresholds

MethodHowStrengthWeakness
Appetite-based (top down)Derive limits from the board's risk appetite and tolerance: what level is unacceptable?Anchored to what the board actually wants.Can be arbitrary if not tested against data; may never trigger.
Statistical control limitsMean plus a multiple of the standard deviation of a stable baseline period.Separates normal variation from unusual movement.Assumes the baseline was acceptable; treats "normal" as "fine".
Percentiles of historySet amber and red at, say, the 90th and 99th percentiles of past values.No distribution assumptions; easy to explain.Needs enough history; inherits past problems.
Peer or external benchmarksCompare with industry data, service standards or regulatory timeframes.Adds an outside view.Peers may differ in size, mix and definitions.

The most defensible approach combines them: statistics tell you what is unusual, appetite tells you what is unacceptable. When the two disagree, that disagreement is itself useful information for the board.

The normalisation trap

Thresholds calculated purely from history quietly accept whatever level of risk the organisation has been running. Sociologist Diane Vaughan called the wider pattern the "normalization of deviance" (Vaughan, 1996): in her study of the Challenger launch decision, repeated anomalies came to be treated as acceptable because nothing had gone wrong yet. Re-anchor statistical thresholds to appetite, and question baselines after incidents.

With classic control limits or the appetite limits, the drift never reaches red. A run rule, which looks for a sustained shift rather than a single extreme value, flags it months earlier. Statistical process control has used rules like this for decades (for example the Western Electric and Nelson rules; Nelson, 1984). They are well suited to KRIs, where gradual deterioration matters more than one bad month.

Counts, rates and small numbers

  • Normalise for volume. 50 errors in a month with twice the transactions is an improvement, not a deterioration. Track errors per thousand transactions where volume varies.
  • Rare events need different tools. For indicators that are usually zero (for example, late breach reports), standard deviations are meaningless. Use "any occurrence is red", or time between events.
  • Seasonality is not a trend. End-of-financial-year peaks in super are expected. Compare with the same month last year, or adjust for the seasonal pattern.

Testing whether a KRI actually works

Treat each KRI as a hypothesis: "when this indicator rises, the risk of harm rises". Test it at least annually.

  1. Lead-lag check: line up the indicator against later incidents, losses or complaints. Did it move beforehand, and by how many periods?
  2. Hits and false alarms: for past amber and red signals, count how many were followed by a real problem (hits) and how many weren't (false alarms). Also count problems that arrived with no signal (misses).
  3. Response check: for each red, was a decision recorded? A KRI that goes red without consequence isn't a control.
  4. Retire or replace: indicators with no predictive value take attention away from ones that have it.

Here the KRI caught 7 of 9 problems (78%), and 7 of its 12 signals were real (58%). That is a reasonably useful indicator. Tightening thresholds would catch more problems at the cost of more false alarms. That trade-off is a judgement for the risk owner, not a statistical fact.

Theory and research

Indicators need a causal model

An indicator is only "key" if it sits on a plausible path from cause to harm. Scandizzo (2005) proposed building KRIs from a map of the business process: identify where in each step operational risk arises, then choose variables that estimate the likelihood or severity of loss at that step, and design controls around them. The discipline matters more than the method. Without a causal story, a dashboard becomes a collection of operational statistics that happen to be easy to collect.

Whether an indicator really leads is an empirical question. In one of the earliest tests of the idea for performance measures, Ittner and Larcker (1998) examined whether customer satisfaction predicted later financial results. It did in some settings, but the relationship was not simply "more is better", which is a caution against assuming that any movement in a leading indicator translates proportionally into risk. For KRIs, the equivalent test is whether past spikes in the indicator were followed by incidents, losses or complaints.

Control charts: separating signal from noise

Statistical process control gives the logic behind the "control limits" method. A process varies naturally (common causes); the aim is to detect variation that is unlikely to be random (special causes). Single points beyond a limit catch sudden shifts, while run rules catch gradual ones, such as several points in a row on one side of the mean (Nelson, 1984). Every rule trades false alarms against missed signals: tighter limits and more rules find real shifts sooner but raise more false alarms, and adding rules together raises the false alarm rate further. Woodall (2000) describes the long-running debates in the field, including whether control charts should be treated as formal hypothesis tests and how their performance should be judged. For KRIs, two practical points follow. The baseline period must itself be stable and acceptable, and indicators that are counts of rare events, or that move with seasons and volumes, break the assumptions behind simple limits.

Measurement changes behaviour

Once an indicator is reported to senior management, people start managing the number. Campbell (1979) observed that the more a quantitative indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the process it was meant to monitor. Kerr (1975) made a related point about rewards: organisations often reward the measurable thing while hoping for something else. For risk indicators, the classic example is incident counts. If fewer reported incidents are praised, reporting falls, and the indicator improves while the risk worsens. Pairing indicators (incident counts with near-miss reporting rates, for example) and checking data quality are practical defences.

Critical perspectives

  • Little evidence that KRIs predict losses. KRIs are required or expected by regulators and widely used, but published tests of whether particular operational risk indicators predict losses are scarce. Most organisations rely on judgement about causal links rather than evidence. Back-testing your own indicators is one of the few ways to find out.
  • Statistics can't say what is acceptable. Control limits describe what is unusual for the process, not what the board is willing to accept. A stable process can be stably bad. This is why the page recommends combining statistical and appetite-based thresholds.
  • The false alarm problem. If amber and red trigger too often, people stop responding. If they trigger too rarely, the dashboard gives false comfort. There is no universally right balance: it depends on the cost of a missed signal compared with the cost of investigating a false one.
  • Leading or just early? Many "leading" indicators are simply earlier lagging indicators (for example, complaints before remediation). They give warning time but may not point to causes.
  • Gaming is not always deliberate. Distortion can come from changes in definitions, reporting practices or workload, not only from people managing the number. Treat sudden improvements with the same curiosity as sudden deteriorations.

Seminar questions

  1. Choose a material risk for a super fund, such as member benefit payment errors. Map the process steps where it arises and propose one leading and one lagging indicator for each step. What is the causal story for each?
  2. Using the chart on this page, compare the control limit and appetite methods. Which would you recommend for board reporting, and why? What would you report to management instead?
  3. How would you back-test whether a KRI actually predicts incidents, given that serious incidents are rare? What data would you need, and what would count as evidence that it works?
  4. Give an example of Campbell's law from risk or compliance reporting. How would you redesign the indicator, or pair it with another, to reduce the distortion?
  5. Adding run rules increases false alarms. How should an organisation decide how many false alarms it is willing to accept for a given indicator?

Check your understanding

1. A KRI's thresholds are set at the mean plus 3 standard deviations of the past year. What is the main risk?

Wide statistical limits accept the historical level as normal and miss slow drift.

2. Why use a run rule such as '8 periods in a row above the mean'?

Run rules detect shifts in the process level, which single-point thresholds miss.

3. A KRI signalled 12 times; 7 were followed by real problems, and 2 problems had no signal. How many problems did it catch?

Problems = 7 hits + 2 misses = 9. It caught 7 of them.
