Change, project and reputational risk

Why so many problems happen during change, how to keep projects honest, and how to manage the reputational fallout of other risks.

Intermediate About 11 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Change risk is the risk that changing systems, products, processes or providers introduces new risks or breaks existing controls. Many major failures happen during change.
  • Material change should be risk-assessed before approval, tested thoroughly, have go/no-go criteria and a back-out plan, and be closely monitored after go-live.
  • Project risk is managed through clear sponsorship, stage gates, honest evidence-based reporting and independent assurance for major programs.
  • Reputational risk is usually a consequence of other risks. Consider it in decisions, monitor early warning signs and prepare crisis communications.
  • In superannuation, fund mergers and administration migrations are among the highest-risk changes.

Three related risks

RiskWhat it meansExamples
Change riskThe risk that changing products, systems, processes, structures or providers introduces new risks or breaks existing controls.A system migration loses member data; a new product launches without the controls it needs; a restructure removes a key reviewer.
Project riskThe risk that a project fails to deliver its benefits on time and on budget.A regulatory program misses its deadline; a technology build runs years late and far over budget.
Reputational riskThe risk of losing the trust of customers, members, staff, investors, regulators or the community.Media coverage of poor treatment of customers; public criticism of an investment; a regulator's enforcement action.

Change risk

Many major operational failures happen during change, not in steady state. Controls that worked are switched off, data is moved and transformed, staff are learning new processes, and attention is on delivery deadlines. In superannuation, fund mergers (successor fund transfers) and administration platform migrations are high-risk examples: errors can affect many members' balances, insurance and payments at once.

Good practice is to treat material change as a risk event in its own right:

  • Assess risk before approving the change, including impacts on critical operations, obligations, controls, data and service providers.
  • Involve risk and compliance early, not just at sign-off.
  • Test thoroughly, including data reconciliation and parallel running where practical.
  • Have go/no-go criteria and a back-out plan.
  • Watch closely after go-live (often called "hypercare"), with extra monitoring and quick escalation.
  • Update the risk and control registers for the new state.

APRA's CPS 230 expects entities to consider operational risk when making business decisions and to maintain critical operations through change, and APRA's April 2026 letter on AI highlighted change management and assurance as areas needing improvement.

Change readiness check

Project risk

Projects fail for familiar reasons: unclear objectives, optimistic plans, changing scope, weak sponsorship, dependency on a few key people or vendors, and reluctance to report bad news. Research on large projects has repeatedly found that cost and schedule overruns are common and that the biggest projects tend to overrun most (Flyvbjerg, 2014).

  • Governance: an accountable sponsor, a steering committee with the authority to stop or reshape the project, and stage gates.
  • Honest reporting: status based on evidence (milestones actually met) rather than optimism. Beware "watermelon" reporting: green outside, red inside.
  • Independent assurance for major programs, from the second line, internal audit or external reviewers.
  • Regulatory programs need special care, because the deadline is fixed by law.

Reputational risk

Reputational risk is usually a consequence of other risks, such as conduct failures, operational outages, data breaches or controversial investments, rather than a standalone risk. It matters because trust, once lost, is slow to rebuild, and because it can quickly turn into financial and regulatory consequences, such as customers leaving or regulators intervening.

  • Consider it in decisions: ask "how would this look to our members, staff, regulators and the public if it were on the front page?"
  • Include it in risk assessments as a consequence dimension on the risk matrix.
  • Monitor complaints, social media, media coverage and stakeholder feedback as early warning indicators.
  • Prepare crisis communications: who speaks, how quickly, and how customers and members are told.
  • Avoid greenwashing and overclaiming. Saying more than you do is itself a reputational (and legal) risk. See Climate-related financial disclosures.
