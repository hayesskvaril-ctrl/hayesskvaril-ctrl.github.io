Issue and action management

How organisations record, rate, fix and verify control weaknesses, so problems found by audits, testing and incidents actually get fixed.

Intermediate About 10 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • An issue is a weakness in controls, processes or systems that needs fixing. An action is the specific task, with an owner and due date, that fixes it.
  • Issues come from many sources: internal audit, control testing, incidents, compliance reviews, regulators and self-identification. Keeping them in one register makes themes visible.
  • Consistent ratings drive how quickly issues must be fixed and what reaches the board.
  • Separate closure (the owner says it is done) from validation (someone independent confirms the fix works), especially for high-rated issues.
  • Overdue actions, repeated extensions and repeat issues are warning signs about control effectiveness and culture.

Issues, incidents and breaches

These terms overlap, and organisations define them differently, but a common approach is:

TermMeaningExample
IncidentAn event that has happened, or nearly happened, causing or threatening harm.Members were charged the wrong fee last month.
BreachA failure to meet an obligation, which may need reporting.The fee was not permitted by the PDS.
IssueA weakness in controls, processes or systems that needs fixing, whether or not harm has occurred yet.The fee rate table can be changed without independent review.
ActionA specific task, with an owner and due date, that fixes an issue.Introduce maker-checker approval for rate table changes by 31 March.

One incident can reveal several issues, and one issue may take several actions to fix. See Incident and breach management for the incident side.

The issue lifecycle

Rating issues and setting timeframes

A consistent rating scale lets different teams' issues be compared and prioritised. Ratings usually consider the potential impact if the weakness is exploited or fails, and how likely that is, much like a risk rating.

Illustrative only. Organisations set their own scales and target timeframes.

CriticalCould cause severe harm to customers, breach of law or significant loss; immediate attention.Interim fix within days; full fix within 1–3 monthsIndependent validation required

Writing good actions

  • Fix the root cause, not just the symptom. "Refund affected members" fixes the harm; "introduce independent review of rate changes" fixes the cause. Both may be needed.
  • Be specific and testable: what will be done, by whom, by when, and what evidence will show it's done.
  • Break big fixes into milestones so progress is visible and slippage is caught early.
  • Consider interim controls to reduce risk while the permanent fix is built.

Closing and validating

A common failure is issues that are "closed" but not fixed. Good practice separates closure (the owner says the action is done and provides evidence) from validation (someone independent, often the second line or internal audit, confirms the fix is in place and working, sometimes after it has operated for a period). Regulators and auditors often find repeat issues, where a previously closed issue reappears, and these are a strong signal of weak issue management.

Extensions and overdue actions

  • Extensions should need a reason and approval at a level matching the rating, with a limit on how often dates can move.
  • Report overdue actions, and issues whose dates have been extended repeatedly, to the risk and audit committees.
  • Track the ageing of open issues. A growing backlog of old issues is a warning sign about risk culture and capacity.

Looking across issues

A single register for all sources (audit, control testing, incidents, compliance reviews, regulators and self-identified issues) makes it possible to spot themes, such as repeated weaknesses in change management or in one business unit. Thematic analysis often tells the board more than any single issue. APRA's prudential inquiry into the Commonwealth Bank, for example, highlighted slow and ineffective resolution of known issues as a sign of wider cultural problems.
