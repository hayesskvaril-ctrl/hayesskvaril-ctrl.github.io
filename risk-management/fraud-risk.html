Fraud risk management

How organisations prevent, detect and respond to fraud by staff, customers, criminals and suppliers, and how fraud connects to scams.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Fraud is dishonestly obtaining a benefit or causing a loss by deception. It can come from inside (staff), outside (customers and criminals) or third parties (suppliers and agents).
  • The fraud triangle explains why people commit fraud: pressure, opportunity and rationalisation. Controls work mainly by removing opportunity.
  • A fraud control framework covers governance, risk assessment, prevention, detection and response.
  • Tips from staff and others are the most common way fraud is found, so whistleblowing channels are a key fraud control.
  • Fraud can trigger reporting to ASIC, AUSTRAC, APRA and the OAIC, and overlaps with the new scams rules.

Types of fraud

TypeWhoExamples in financial services
Internal fraudEmployees, contractors or executivesRedirecting member benefits or customer funds; fake suppliers; expense fraud; manipulating results to hit bonus targets.
External fraudCustomers, criminals, organised groupsIdentity theft and account takeover; fraudulent early release of super; false insurance claims; loan application fraud.
Third-party fraudSuppliers, agents, brokersInflated invoices, kickbacks, falsified broker applications.
ScamsCriminals deceiving customersA customer is tricked into making the payment themselves, for example an investment or impersonation scam.

The difference between fraud and scams matters. In classic fraud, the customer didn't authorise the transaction. In a scam, the customer authorised it but was deceived. Scams are now subject to the Scams Prevention Framework. See Consumer protection.

Why people commit fraud: the fraud triangle

A fraud control framework

The Australian Standard AS 8001 (Fraud and corruption control) and the Commonwealth Fraud and Corruption Control Framework describe similar building blocks. In our own words:

  1. Governance and culture

    Board oversight, a fraud policy, clear accountability and a culture where fraud isn't tolerated and concerns are raised.

  2. Fraud risk assessment

    Regularly identifying how fraud could happen in each product, process and system, including how controls could be bypassed.

  3. Prevention

    Segregation of duties, access controls, pre-employment and supplier screening, customer identity verification and approval limits.

  4. Detection

    Data analytics and transaction monitoring, reconciliations, whistleblowing channels, and reviewing exceptions.

  5. Response

    Investigation by skilled people, preserving evidence, recovery of funds, disciplinary action, reporting to police and regulators, and fixing the control gap.

Detection: what works

In the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners' 2024 global study of occupational fraud, tips were the most common way frauds were detected, in 43% of cases, far ahead of internal audit and management review. Around half of tips came from employees. This is why strong whistleblower channels are a fraud control, not just a compliance requirement.

Data analytics is the other major tool, for example flagging:

  • changes to bank account details followed soon after by a large payment (a classic sign of account takeover);
  • staff accessing records of customers they have no reason to deal with;
  • supplier bank accounts matching employee bank accounts;
  • unusual spikes in claims, withdrawals or overrides.

Fraud in superannuation

Super balances are an attractive target. Common patterns include criminals using stolen identities to open accounts or take over existing ones and then roll balances out, fraudulent claims on early release grounds, and scams persuading members to move super into sham schemes or self-managed funds. Controls include strong identity verification, call-back and multi-factor checks before changing contact or bank details, holds and alerts on unusual rollovers, and member education.

Reporting obligations

  • Fraud may be a reportable situation to ASIC (gross negligence or serious fraud is reportable in its own right). See Breach and incident reporting obligations.
  • Reporting entities may need to lodge suspicious matter reports with AUSTRAC. See AML/CTF fundamentals.
  • A material fraud may be a material operational risk incident for APRA under CPS 230, and data compromises may trigger the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme.

Worked example: assessing one fraud risk

An illustrative assessment, following the framework above:

StepExample: member account takeover at a super fund
ScenarioA criminal uses stolen identity details to take over a member's online account, change the bank details and request a benefit payment.
Existing controlsMulti-factor authentication at login; a waiting period after bank detail changes; a call-back to the member's registered phone number.
WeaknessesContact details can be changed in the same session; call centre staff can override the waiting period.
AssessmentLikely, with high impact on affected members and the fund's reputation.
ResponseRequire extra verification before contact detail changes; remove overrides or require a second approver; monitor for patterns such as many changes from one device.
Owner and indicatorHead of member services; indicator: number of bank detail changes followed by a withdrawal within 14 days.

Common mistakes

  • Assessing fraud only once a year, while criminals change methods monthly.
  • Controls that staff can easily override under customer pressure.
  • Treating fraud as a finance issue when it is also a customer, cyber and conduct issue.
  • Not sharing intelligence with industry and law enforcement.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between fraud and a scam?

In everyday use, a scam tricks the customer into acting themselves (such as transferring money), while fraud often involves someone acting without the customer's authority. The Scams Prevention Framework creates specific obligations for scams in some sectors.

Are super funds targets?

Yes. APRA and ASIC have warned about identity theft, account takeovers and early release fraud. See APRA's 2025 letter on authentication in the CPS 234 page.
