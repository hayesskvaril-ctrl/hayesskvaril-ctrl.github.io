Assurance mapping and combined assurance

A way to see who checks what across the organisation, so that the biggest risks get enough independent assurance and effort isn't wasted on duplication.

Intermediate About 10 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • An assurance map shows, for each key risk, which assurance providers check it, how much and how well.
  • Assurance comes from the first line (management), second line (risk and compliance), third line (internal audit) and external providers such as auditors and regulators.
  • The map reveals gaps (material risks with little independent assurance) and duplication (the same controls checked repeatedly).
  • Combined assurance coordinates providers so the board gets one joined-up picture, while protecting internal audit's independence.
  • The level of assurance should match the level of risk.

Why map assurance?

Large organisations receive assurance from many directions: managers checking their own teams' work, risk and compliance reviews, internal audit, external auditors, actuaries, independent experts and regulators. Without coordination, the same controls get tested three times while other material risks get none. An assurance map is a simple picture that shows, for each key risk, who provides assurance, how much, and how good it is.

Sources of assurance

LineExamplesIndependence
First lineManagement reviews, supervisor checks, quality assurance teams, risk and control self-assessments, first-line control testing.Lowest: people checking their own area.

Building an assurance map

  1. Start with the risks. Use the top risks from the risk profile and key obligations, mapped to the risk taxonomy.
  2. List the assurance activities for each risk: who, what scope, how often, and when last done.
  3. Assess coverage and quality. Not all assurance is equal. Consider independence, competence, scope, testing depth (see control testing) and how recent it is.
  4. Compare with risk. Higher risks warrant more, and more independent, assurance.
  5. Identify gaps and overlaps, and agree changes to plans across the lines.
  6. Report and maintain. Share the map with the audit and risk committees and refresh it at least annually.

Combined assurance

Combined assurance goes a step further: the assurance providers coordinate their plans, use common risk and rating language, rely on each other's work where it's good enough, and give the board a joined-up view. The idea was popularised by South Africa's King corporate governance codes and is now widely used. The IIA's Three Lines Model makes the same point: the lines need to be aligned and coordinated to serve the organisation's objectives.

  • Benefits: fewer gaps, less duplication, less "audit fatigue" in the business, and a clearer picture for the board.
  • Risks: blurred independence if internal audit relies too heavily on management's own testing, and inconsistent quality across providers. Internal audit should assess others' work before relying on it.
