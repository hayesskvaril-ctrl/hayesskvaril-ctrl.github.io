Liquidity stress testing for super funds

Why liquidity risk in super is distinctive, how the denominator effect and stale valuations catch funds out, and how to design stress tests that matter, with a simulator.

Advanced About 26 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • SPS 530 requires a liquidity management plan and a comprehensive stress testing program completed at least annually, with results reported to the board.
  • Super liquidity pressure combines member switching, rollovers, currency hedge settlements and policy shocks such as the 2020 early release scheme.
  • When listed markets fall faster than unlisted valuations, unlisted assets grow as a share of the option: the denominator effect.
  • Stale valuations can transfer value from members who stay to members who leave. Liquidity and valuation governance are linked.
  • Passing a coverage test isn't enough: test timing, behaviour, asset liquidity and whether the strategy can be maintained.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain how member switching, rollovers, benefit payments and currency hedging create liquidity demands on a super option.
  2. Model the denominator effect and liquidity coverage for a stressed Balanced option.
  3. Use research on fund flows, stale valuations and liquidity spirals to explain why liquidity stress can be self-reinforcing.
  4. Critically assess the fairness issues between members that arise when illiquid assets are valued with a lag.

The idea in plain English

A super fund must always be able to pay benefits, rollovers and switches when members ask, even in a market crash. Most of the time that's easy: contributions come in, and most assets can be sold quickly. The risk is a combination: markets fall, members switch or leave, currency hedges need cash, and the unlisted assets that looked like good long-term investments can't be sold quickly or at a fair price.

Liquidity stress testing asks how that combination would play out, and what the fund would need to do. This page explains the mechanics that make super liquidity distinctive, with a simulator to explore them. It builds on SPS 530 Investment Governance and scenario analysis and stress testing.

What SPS 530 requires (summary)

  • A liquidity management plan for the fund and its investment options, identifying who manages and oversees liquidity risk and the key metrics reported to the board.
  • Liquidity stress testing as part of a comprehensive stress testing program, completed at least annually, with documented methodology, assumptions, triggers for ad hoc testing and processes to ensure data is reliable.
  • Board reporting that shows the outcomes, the assumptions, and the actions available where tolerances would be breached.

Paraphrased from SPS 530 as strengthened from 1 January 2023. APRA has also published material on the governance of unlisted asset valuation and liquidity risk management (December 2024).

Why super liquidity is different

DriverHow it creates liquidity pressure
Member switchingMembers can switch from growth options to cash, often after markets have fallen. The option must sell assets to meet switches, just when prices are low.
RolloversMembers can move to another fund. A complete rollover request must generally be actioned as soon as practicable and within 3 business days (SIS Regulations reg 6.34A), so funds can't wait for markets to recover.
Currency hedgingIf the Australian dollar falls sharply, hedging contracts on foreign assets can require large cash settlements, at the same time as markets fall.
Policy shocksGovernment decisions can create sudden outflows. The COVID-19 early release scheme paid about $36.4 billion from super accounts to 4.8 million applications in 2020.
Unlisted assetsProperty, infrastructure and private equity or credit can't be sold quickly, and their valuations update more slowly than listed markets.

Two effects that catch funds out

The denominator effect

When listed markets fall and unlisted valuations don't (yet), unlisted assets become a bigger share of the option, even though nothing was bought. Outflows paid from liquid assets make it worse. An option can drift above its strategic range for unlisted assets without anyone making an investment decision.

Unfairness between members

If unlisted assets are carried at stale, higher values while listed markets have fallen, the unit price overstates what the option is really worth. Members who leave are paid out at that price, and members who stay bear the difference. This is why valuation governance and liquidity management are linked: both are about treating members fairly as well as paying them on time.

Try it: stress a Balanced option

All figures per $100 of option assets. Listed assets are whatever isn't cash or unlisted.

A deliberately simple, one-period model. Real stress tests model timing (daily, weekly, monthly), contributions, the order in which assets can be sold, and management actions.

With the default settings, the option can comfortably pay its outflows, yet unlisted assets end up well above the strategic maximum. Passing a coverage test doesn't mean the option is fine.

Designing a liquidity stress test

  1. Scenarios: include a historical scenario (such as March 2020), a hypothetical severe scenario, and a reverse stress test: what combination of events would make the option unable to pay, or force a breach of its strategy?
  2. Behavioural assumptions: switching and rollover rates are the hardest to estimate and the most important. Use your own data, test sensitivity and assume worse behaviour in a crisis than in normal times.
  3. Timing: test over short horizons (days and weeks), not just one year. Liquidity problems are about timing.
  4. Liquidity of assets: classify assets by how fast they can realistically be sold and at what discount in stress, including listed assets in thin markets.
  5. Management actions: list them in advance (rebalancing, using cash buffers, pausing some processes where the law allows), with triggers and decision rights. Don't assume actions that would harm remaining members.
  6. Metrics and limits: liquidity coverage under stress, unlisted asset ranges and time to restore strategic allocation, each linked to the risk appetite statement.

Worked example: a liquidity coverage check

A $10 billion Balanced option is stress tested over 30 days. The test asks whether liquid assets, after realistic discounts for selling in a crisis, cover the stressed outflows.

The option passes comfortably, but that isn't the whole story. Paying $1.35 billion from liquid assets while unlisted values lag pushes the unlisted share up, as the simulator above shows, and a run of switching over several months would compound it. Good tests look at the path over time, the strategy limits and fairness between members, not just one ratio.

What APRA has found

APRA's December 2024 thematic review of 23 trustees found that 12 needed material improvement in unlisted asset valuation governance, liquidity risk management or both. On liquidity, the weaknesses were in:

  • trigger frameworks that warn of potential liquidity stress;
  • managing the liquidity risks of unlisted assets; and
  • liquidity action plans that say what the trustee will do when stress arrives.

APRA's May 2026 System Risk Outlook noted that about 16% of APRA-regulated super investments are in private markets, around half offshore, and that funds are closely monitoring unlisted valuations and member switching. See SPS 530 for APRA's wider findings and its 2026 proposals.

Common mistakes

  • One-year horizons only, missing the days and weeks when liquidity actually runs out.
  • Behaviour assumptions taken from calm periods, not from March 2020 or worse.
  • Ignoring currency hedging and capital calls, which can need cash at the worst moment.
  • Assuming listed assets are always liquid, even in thin or stressed markets.
  • Triggers with no pre-agreed actions or decision rights.
  • Testing liquidity separately from valuation, so the fairness problem of stale prices is missed.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. In our worst stress scenario, how long could each option pay members before needing to sell assets at a discount?
  2. Where do our switching and rollover assumptions come from, and how did members behave in March 2020?
  3. How far could unlisted assets drift above their strategic ranges, and how long would it take to return?
  4. What are our liquidity triggers, and what exactly happens when one is hit?
  5. How do our stress results feed into investment strategy and the valuation policy?

Theory and research

First-mover advantage and run risk

Research on pooled investment funds explains why liquidity stress can feed on itself. Chen et al. (2010) found that outflows from funds holding illiquid assets react more strongly to poor performance than outflows from funds holding liquid assets. The reason is a payoff complementarity: when investors leave, the fund sells assets and bears trading costs, which fall on those who stay, so each investor has more reason to leave if others do. Goldstein et al. (2017) found a similar asymmetry in corporate bond funds, where outflows respond more to bad performance than inflows respond to good performance, which can amplify selling in stressed markets. Super funds differ from these funds (members switch between options within the fund, and most cannot withdraw until retirement), but switching and rollovers create a similar first-mover advantage when unit prices lag reality.

Stale valuations

Unlisted assets are valued periodically, so their reported values move slowly. Getmansky et al. (2004) showed that the smooth, serially correlated returns of funds holding illiquid assets mostly reflect this smoothing rather than genuinely low risk. Smoothed values understate volatility and, in a falling market, overstate what the assets are worth. That is the mechanism behind both effects on this page: the denominator effect (unlisted assets become a larger share of the option) and the transfer of value from members who stay to members who leave.

Liquidity spirals

Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) modelled how market liquidity (the ease of selling assets) and funding liquidity (the ease of meeting cash demands) reinforce each other: selling to meet cash needs pushes prices down, which raises margin and collateral demands, which forces more selling. For super funds, currency hedge settlements are the clearest example. When the Australian dollar falls sharply, hedging contracts can require large cash payments at the same time as listed markets fall and members switch to cash, which is what happened in March 2020.

Critical perspectives

  • The research is mostly about other funds. The flow studies use US mutual fund data. Australian super has compulsory contributions, preservation rules and within-fund switching, so the size of these effects in super is an empirical question with limited published research.
  • Behavioural assumptions dominate. The most important inputs to a liquidity stress test, switching and rollover rates, depend on member behaviour in a crisis, which past data may not predict, especially with faster digital switching and advice tools.
  • Fairness and liquidity pull in different directions. Revaluing unlisted assets quickly protects remaining members but can lock in losses early; waiting risks unfairness. There is no consensus on the right frequency, which is why APRA has focused on valuation governance.
  • Unlisted assets may genuinely be less volatile. Some argue that long-term investors are right to look through short-term market falls. The counter-argument is that a fund must still pay members who leave at a fair price today.

Seminar questions

  1. Using the simulator, find settings where the option passes its liquidity coverage test but breaches its unlisted asset maximum. What should the trustee do?
  2. Explain Chen, Goldstein and Jiang's payoff complementarity in the context of member switching between super options. What design features could reduce the first-mover advantage?
  3. How would you use Getmansky, Lo and Makarov's insight about smoothed returns when reviewing an option's reported volatility and risk label?
  4. Reconstruct the liquidity pressures on a large super fund in March 2020 using Brunnermeier and Pedersen's idea of a liquidity spiral. Which feedback loops were present?

Further reading

  • Chen, Q., Goldstein, I., & Jiang, W. (2010). Payoff complementarities and financial fragility: Evidence from mutual fund outflows. Journal of Financial Economics, 97(2), 239–262. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2010.03.016 Peer-reviewed
    Funds holding illiquid assets see outflows respond more strongly to poor performance, because investors who leave early impose trading costs on those who stay: evidence of first-mover advantage and run risk.
  • Getmansky, M., Lo, A. W., & Makarov, I. (2004). An econometric model of serial correlation and illiquidity in hedge fund returns. Journal of Financial Economics, 74(3), 529–609. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.04.001 Peer-reviewed
    Shows that smooth, serially correlated returns in funds holding illiquid assets mostly reflect stale or smoothed valuations, which understate true volatility and overstate risk-adjusted performance.
  • Brunnermeier, M. K., & Pedersen, L. H. (2009). Market liquidity and funding liquidity. The Review of Financial Studies, 22(6), 2201–2238. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhn098 Peer-reviewed
    A model of 'liquidity spirals': when funding tightens, forced selling reduces market liquidity, which tightens funding further, so liquidity can dry up suddenly.

Check your understanding

1. Listed markets fall 30%, unlisted valuations barely move, and outflows are paid from liquid assets. What happens to the unlisted share of the option?

This is the denominator effect, made worse by paying outflows from liquid assets.

2. Why can stale unlisted valuations be unfair between members?

Valuation governance is about fairness as well as accuracy.

3. How often must an RSE licensee's comprehensive stress testing program be completed under SPS 530?
