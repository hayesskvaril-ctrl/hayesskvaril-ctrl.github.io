Liquidity stress testing for super funds

Why liquidity risk in super is distinctive, how the denominator effect and stale valuations catch funds out, and how to design stress tests that matter, with a simulator.

Advanced About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • SPS 530 requires a liquidity management plan and a comprehensive stress testing program completed at least annually, with results reported to the board.
  • Super liquidity pressure combines member switching, rollovers, currency hedge settlements and policy shocks such as the 2020 early release scheme.
  • When listed markets fall faster than unlisted valuations, unlisted assets grow as a share of the option: the denominator effect.
  • Stale valuations can transfer value from members who stay to members who leave. Liquidity and valuation governance are linked.
  • Passing a coverage test isn't enough: test timing, behaviour, asset liquidity and whether the strategy can be maintained.

The idea in plain English

A super fund must always be able to pay benefits, rollovers and switches when members ask, even in a market crash. Most of the time that's easy: contributions come in, and most assets can be sold quickly. The risk is a combination: markets fall, members switch or leave, currency hedges need cash, and the unlisted assets that looked like good long-term investments can't be sold quickly or at a fair price.

Liquidity stress testing asks how that combination would play out, and what the fund would need to do. This page explains the mechanics that make super liquidity distinctive, with a simulator to explore them. It builds on SPS 530 Investment Governance and scenario analysis and stress testing.

What SPS 530 requires (summary)

  • A liquidity management plan for the fund and its investment options, identifying who manages and oversees liquidity risk and the key metrics reported to the board.
  • Liquidity stress testing as part of a comprehensive stress testing program, completed at least annually, with documented methodology, assumptions, triggers for ad hoc testing and processes to ensure data is reliable.
  • Board reporting that shows the outcomes, the assumptions, and the actions available where tolerances would be breached.

Paraphrased from SPS 530 as strengthened from 1 January 2023. APRA has also published material on the governance of unlisted asset valuation and liquidity risk management (December 2024).

Why super liquidity is different

DriverHow it creates liquidity pressure
Member switchingMembers can switch from growth options to cash, often after markets have fallen. The option must sell assets to meet switches, just when prices are low.
RolloversMembers can move to another fund, and rollover requests generally must be processed within short legal timeframes.
Currency hedgingIf the Australian dollar falls sharply, hedging contracts on foreign assets can require large cash settlements, at the same time as markets fall.
Policy shocksGovernment decisions can create sudden outflows. The COVID-19 early release scheme paid about $36.4 billion from super accounts to 4.8 million applications in 2020.
Unlisted assetsProperty, infrastructure and private equity or credit can't be sold quickly, and their valuations update more slowly than listed markets.

Two effects that catch funds out

The denominator effect

When listed markets fall and unlisted valuations don't (yet), unlisted assets become a bigger share of the option, even though nothing was bought. Outflows paid from liquid assets make it worse. An option can drift above its strategic range for unlisted assets without anyone making an investment decision.

Unfairness between members

If unlisted assets are carried at stale, higher values while listed markets have fallen, the unit price overstates what the option is really worth. Members who leave are paid out at that price, and members who stay bear the difference. This is why valuation governance and liquidity management are linked: both are about treating members fairly as well as paying them on time.

Designing a liquidity stress test

  1. Scenarios: include a historical scenario (such as March 2020), a hypothetical severe scenario, and a reverse stress test: what combination of events would make the option unable to pay, or force a breach of its strategy?
  2. Behavioural assumptions: switching and rollover rates are the hardest to estimate and the most important. Use your own data, test sensitivity and assume worse behaviour in a crisis than in normal times.
  3. Timing: test over short horizons (days and weeks), not just one year. Liquidity problems are about timing.
  4. Liquidity of assets: classify assets by how fast they can realistically be sold and at what discount in stress, including listed assets in thin markets.
  5. Management actions: list them in advance (rebalancing, using cash buffers, pausing some processes where the law allows), with triggers and decision rights. Don't assume actions that would harm remaining members.
  6. Metrics and limits: liquidity coverage under stress, unlisted asset ranges and time to restore strategic allocation, each linked to the risk appetite statement.

Check your understanding

1. Listed markets fall 30%, unlisted valuations barely move, and outflows are paid from liquid assets. What happens to the unlisted share of the option?

This is the denominator effect, made worse by paying outflows from liquid assets.

2. Why can stale unlisted valuations be unfair between members?

Valuation governance is about fairness as well as accuracy.

3. How often must an RSE licensee's comprehensive stress testing program be completed under SPS 530?

At least annually, with triggers for ad hoc testing.
