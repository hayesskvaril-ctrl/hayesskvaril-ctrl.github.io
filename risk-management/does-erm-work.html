Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence

What the research actually shows about whether enterprise risk management creates value, where the evidence is weak, and what that means for risk functions.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Several studies find ERM is associated with higher firm value or better crisis performance, particularly for insurers and banks.
  • Banks with stronger, more independent risk functions had lower tail risk in the global financial crisis.
  • The benefit seems to depend on fit: ERM helps most when matched to a firm's uncertainty, complexity, size and board oversight.
  • Measuring ERM is hard, so much of the evidence relies on proxies such as hiring a chief risk officer or ratings, and causation is uncertain.
  • Critics argue ERM can become a compliance ritual that produces comfort rather than insight.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Summarise the empirical evidence on ERM and firm value.
  2. Explain the contingency view of ERM and apply it to an Australian financial institution.
  3. Identify the main methodological problems in ERM research, including measurement and causation.
  4. Critically evaluate Power's critique of ERM and its implications for risk appetite and assurance.

The idea in plain English

APRA requires banks, insurers and super trustees to have a risk management framework, and most large organisations run some version of enterprise risk management (ERM): managing all material risks together, linked to strategy and appetite. But does it work? Does it make organisations more valuable, more resilient or less likely to fail? This page reviews the evidence. It assumes you know the basics from enterprise risk management.

Theory and research

Why ERM should create value, in theory

In a world of perfect markets, shareholders can diversify risks themselves, so managing risk inside the firm adds nothing. In practice, frictions make risk costly: financial distress, taxes, costly external finance and the difficulty of assessing firms from outside. Managing risks together, rather than in silos, should reduce the chance of distress and help firms take the right risks. ERM also aims to improve decisions by making risk-return trade-offs explicit.

Evidence on firm value

The most cited study is Hoyt and Liebenberg (2011), who examined 275 publicly traded US insurers from 1998 to 2005 and found that insurers engaged in ERM had significantly higher market valuations. McShane et al. (2011), using Standard & Poor's ERM ratings of insurers, found a more nuanced pattern: firm value rose with traditional risk management capability, but there was no further increase for firms rated as having more advanced ERM. Gordon et al. (2009) argued the relationship is contingent: ERM improved performance when it matched a firm's environmental uncertainty, competition, size, complexity and board monitoring.

Evidence from banks in the crisis

The global financial crisis provided a natural test. Ellul and Yerramilli (2013) built a risk management index measuring the strength and independence of the risk function at US bank holding companies. Banks with a higher index before the crisis had lower tail risk, fewer bad loans and better performance during the crisis. Aebi et al. (2012) found that banks whose chief risk officer reported directly to the board performed better than banks where the CRO reported to the CEO. These studies suggest that the independence and authority of risk management matter, not just its existence.

The critique

Power (2009) offered an influential critique written after the crisis. He argued that ERM had become boundary-preserving rather than boundary-challenging: organised around a "logic of the audit trail" that documents and verifies, rather than confronting interconnected risks. Risk appetite, in particular, had been treated as a number rather than as the outcome of an organisational process involving values. On this view, ERM can produce comfort that is limited or illusory. Bromiley et al. (2015) reviewed the academic literature and found weak measures of ERM, limited theory and little research on how ERM is actually practised inside organisations.

Applying the evidence: how a board might test its own ERM

The research suggests ERM works when it fits the organisation and influences decisions. A board could look for evidence like this:

QuestionEvidence that ERM is working
Does risk information change decisions?Examples where appetite or risk analysis led to a different decision, not just a documented one.
Are breaches of appetite acted on?Breaches with clear responses, owners and timeframes.
Are surprises falling?Fewer major incidents that weren't on any risk register.
Is the risk function independent and heard?The CRO's views appear in board minutes and sometimes prevail.
Does the framework fit the business?Effort focused on the material risks, not spread evenly across everything.

An interpretive application for discussion, not a finding of fact about any organisation.

Frequently asked questions

If the evidence is mixed, why is ERM required?

Regulators require risk management frameworks because the costs of failure are high and the evidence, while imperfect, points to benefits from strong, independent risk functions.

Critical perspectives

  • What counts as "ERM"? Studies identify ERM by keyword searches, the hiring of a chief risk officer or rating agency scores. None directly observes how risk is managed, so studies may be measuring signals rather than substance.
  • Reverse causation. Well-run, valuable firms may be more likely to adopt ERM, rather than ERM making them valuable. Most studies can't fully separate the two.
  • Mostly US insurers and banks. Evidence for Australian super funds, which are trusts run for members rather than shareholders, is very limited. "Value" for a profit-for-member fund means member outcomes, not share prices.
  • Compliance can crowd out judgement. Power's critique suggests regulatory requirements can push ERM towards documentation. Whether this has happened in Australia under CPS 220 and SPS 220 is an open question.
  • Absence of failure is hard to credit. Good risk management prevents events that never happen, which makes its benefits hard to see and hard to measure.

Seminar questions

  1. Hoyt and Liebenberg found ERM associated with higher value. Design a study that could test whether ERM causes higher value, rather than the reverse.
  2. Using Gordon and colleagues' contingency factors, assess how much ERM an Australian super fund of your choice needs, and in what form.
  3. Ellul and Yerramilli emphasise the independence of the risk function. What features of independence would you look for in an Australian bank or insurer?
  4. Is Power's "risk management of nothing" a fair description of practice today? Use an example of risk appetite reporting to support your view.
  5. How should a super trustee measure whether its risk management framework "works"?

    Studying 275 US publicly traded insurers from 1998 to 2005, finds that firms engaged in ERM have a significantly higher market valuation.
  • Ellul, A., & Yerramilli, V. (2013). Stronger risk controls, lower risk: Evidence from U.S. bank holding companies. The Journal of Finance, 68(5), 1757–1803. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12057 Peer-reviewed
    Banks with a stronger, more independent risk management function before the crisis (a higher 'risk management index') had lower tail risk and better performance during the crisis.
  • Power, M. (2009). The risk management of nothing. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 34(6–7), 849–855. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2009.06.001 Peer-reviewed
    A critique of ERM after the financial crisis: an impoverished idea of risk appetite and a 'logic of the audit trail' made ERM boundary-preserving rather than challenging, so its security was limited or illusory.
  • Bromiley, P., McShane, M., Nair, A., & Rustambekov, E. (2015). Enterprise risk management: Review, critique, and research directions. Long Range Planning, 48(4), 265–276. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2014.07.005 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews ERM research and criticises it for weak measures of ERM, limited theory and little evidence on how ERM is actually practised, and sets out research directions.
