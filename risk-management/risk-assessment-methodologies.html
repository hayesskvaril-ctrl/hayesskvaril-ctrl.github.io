Risk assessment methodologies

How organisations find their risks, size them up and decide which matter most, from simple heat maps to statistical models.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Risk assessment has three parts: identify the risks, analyse them, and evaluate them against your criteria and appetite.
  • The most common method rates each risk by likelihood and consequence on defined scales and plots it on a heat map.
  • Distinguish inherent risk (before controls), residual risk (after controls) and target risk (after planned actions).
  • Qualitative methods are quick and accessible; quantitative methods are more precise but need data and expertise. Many organisations mix them.
  • Heat maps are useful for communication but have well-documented weaknesses. Treat ratings as structured judgement, not precise measurement.

The three steps

StepQuestionCommon techniques
IdentifyWhat could happen, why, and with what effect?Workshops and interviews; process mapping; risk and control self-assessments; reviewing incidents, near misses, complaints and audit findings; horizon scanning of regulatory and industry developments; bow-tie analysis; scenario analysis.
AnalyseHow likely is it, how bad could it be, and how well do our controls work?Rating scales (likelihood × consequence); control effectiveness ratings; loss data analysis; statistical modelling; expert judgement.
EvaluateIs it acceptable? What needs action first?Comparing ratings with risk appetite and tolerance; ranking; deciding on treatment.

Write risks properly first

A vague risk ("cyber", "regulatory change") can't be assessed well. A useful pattern is cause → event → consequence:

Weak: "Key person risk."

Better: "Because unit pricing relies on one experienced analyst and procedures are not documented (cause), their unexpected absence (event) could lead to late or incorrect unit prices, causing member losses, remediation costs and a reportable breach (consequences)."

The better version tells you what controls might help (documentation, cross-training, a backup), and makes rating the consequence much easier.

    Inherent, residual and target risk

    • Inherent risk: the level before considering controls. It shows how much you depend on your controls. Definitions vary: some organisations mean "with no controls at all", others "if key controls failed".
    • Residual risk: the level after taking existing controls into account. This depends on how effective the controls really are, which is why control testing matters.
    • Target risk: where the risk should be once planned actions are complete. It should sit within appetite.

    Going further: quantitative methods Advanced

    Quantitative risk assessment tries to describe risk as a range of possible losses with probabilities, rather than a single rating.

    • Expected loss. The simplest measure: probability × impact. A 10% annual chance of a $2 million loss has an expected annual loss of $200,000. Useful for comparing the cost of a control with the loss it prevents, but it hides the severity of rare, large events.
    • Frequency and severity modelling. Model how often events happen (for example with a Poisson distribution) and how large each is (for example with a lognormal distribution), then combine them to get a distribution of total annual losses. This is common in operational risk and insurance.
    • Monte Carlo simulation. Run thousands of simulated years to see the full range of outcomes, including tail losses.
    • Value at Risk (VaR) and similar measures. The loss that will not be exceeded with a given confidence over a period (for example, a 99.5% one-year loss). Widely used in market risk and capital modelling, but it says nothing about how bad losses beyond that point could be.
    • Scenario analysis and stress testing. Rather than predicting probabilities, ask "what if a severe but plausible event happened?" and trace the impacts. Essential where data is scarce, and required in many prudential contexts.

    The limits of heat maps

    Heat maps are everywhere because they are simple and visual. But research has identified real problems, and a good risk professional knows them.

    • Poor resolution. Cox (2008) showed mathematically that typical risk matrices can correctly compare only a small fraction of randomly chosen pairs of risks, can give identical ratings to very different risks, and can even rate a smaller risk higher than a larger one.
    • Ambiguous inputs. Words like "likely" mean very different probabilities to different people.
    • Human bias. People estimate likelihood partly by how easily examples come to mind (the availability heuristic) and are pulled towards starting numbers (anchoring), as documented by Tversky and Kahneman (1974). Recent incidents get over-rated; things that have never happened get under-rated.
    • No aggregation. Ten "medium" risks with a common cause might add up to one extreme risk, but a heat map shows ten separate dots.
    • Missing dimensions. Speed of onset ("velocity"), how long effects last, and connections between risks aren't captured.

    Making heat maps better

    • Anchor scales to real numbers (probabilities, dollar ranges, member numbers) and calibrate them with past data.
    • Assess in groups with challenge, and record the reasoning, not just the score.
    • Rate on the worst credible consequence, and add scenario analysis for severe risks.
    • Look for common causes and connections across the register.
    • Use the matrix to start a conversation, not end one.
