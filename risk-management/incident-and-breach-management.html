Incident and breach management

When something goes wrong, the way an organisation responds matters as much as the event itself: containing harm, assessing it properly, telling regulators on time, fixing the cause and putting people right.

Intermediate About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • An incident is something that went wrong (or nearly did). A breach is a failure to meet an obligation. One event can be both, and can trigger several reporting regimes at once.
  • The lifecycle: identify and record, contain, assess, notify, investigate root cause, remediate, fix and close, learn.
  • Reporting clocks are short and start early, often when the organisation first becomes aware, not when the investigation ends. Key examples: 24 and 72 hours (APRA), 30 calendar days (ASIC reportable situations), and prompt assessment of data breaches (OAIC).
  • ASIC's reportable situations regime applies to AFS and credit licensees. Some breaches are "deemed significant" and must be reported without further judgement.
  • Remediation should be prompt, proactive and fair. ASIC's RG 277 sets out its expectations.

Incidents, breaches and near misses

TermMeaningExample
IncidentAn event that caused, or could have caused, loss, harm or disruption.A batch job fails and 3,000 insurance premiums are deducted twice from members' accounts.
Near missAn incident that was caught before causing harm.The duplicate deduction is spotted in a reconciliation and reversed before it hits member accounts.
BreachA failure to comply with an obligation (law, standard, licence condition, code, policy).Members were charged fees that the product disclosure did not allow.
IssueA known weakness that needs fixing, which may not yet have caused an incident.A control test finds that reconciliations are often signed off without evidence.

The overlap matters. The duplicate premium deduction above is an incident. If it breaches the fund's governing rules or the law, it is also a breach. If it affects many members, it may be a material operational risk incident for APRA and a reportable situation for ASIC. So one event can start several clocks at once.

The incident and breach lifecycle

1. Identify and record

Anyone in the organisation should be able to raise a potential incident or breach easily, and should be expected to. Record the facts known so far, when it was discovered and by whom. The date of awareness matters because many reporting clocks start from it.

2. Contain

Stop the harm spreading: pause a faulty process, block compromised access, stop incorrect payments. Containment comes before perfect understanding.

3. Assess

Rate the severity (using the organisation's incident rating scale), then answer three separate questions: Is it a breach of an obligation? Is it reportable to anyone? Who needs to know internally? Record the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

4. Notify

Report to regulators within the required timeframes (see below), escalate internally, and consider whether affected customers must be told (for example under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme).

5. Investigate root cause

Work out not just what happened but why, and how far it goes: how many customers, over what period, through which other processes. See root cause analysis below.

6. Remediate

Put affected customers or members back in the position they would have been in, usually through refunds or compensation with interest or lost earnings. See remediation below.

7. Fix and close

Fix the control weakness that allowed it, check the fix works, and close the matter only with evidence. Under CPS 230, control gaps and weaknesses must stay in the operational risk profile until remediated.

8. Learn

Update risk assessments, look for themes across incidents, and share lessons. Repeated incidents with the same root cause are a warning sign regulators look for.

Breach assessment: ASIC reportable situations

Australian financial services (AFS) licensees and credit licensees must report "reportable situations" to ASIC under the Corporations Act (s 912D and s 912DAA) and the National Consumer Credit Protection Act. The regime started on 1 October 2021. In simplified terms, reportable situations include:

  • significant breaches (or likely breaches) of "core obligations", such as the general licensee obligations and key financial services laws;
  • investigations into whether there is a significant breach that continue beyond a set period (ASIC relief has extended this from 30 to 60 days);
  • additional matters such as gross negligence or serious fraud by the licensee or its representatives; and
  • in some cases, reportable situations involving other licensees, such as financial advisers.

When does the 30-day clock start?

A report must be lodged within 30 calendar days after the licensee first knows, or is reckless about whether, there are reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen. That is usually well before an investigation has confirmed every detail. ASIC's view is that licensees shouldn't delay reporting until they are certain.

Root cause analysis

Root cause analysis asks why something happened, repeatedly, until you reach a cause that, if fixed, would stop it happening again. "Human error" is almost never a root cause: the question is why the process allowed a human error to cause harm.

Worked example: the "5 whys"

  1. Why were premiums deducted twice? The overnight batch ran twice.
  2. Why did it run twice? An operator restarted it after a timeout, not knowing the first run had completed.
  3. Why didn't they know? The job gives no completion status, and the runbook doesn't say how to check.
  4. Why doesn't the system stop a duplicate run? The duplicate-check control was switched off during a system upgrade two years ago.
  5. Why wasn't that noticed? Post-implementation testing didn't cover control settings, and there is no periodic check of key automated controls.

Root causes: weak change management testing and no monitoring of automated controls. Retraining the operator alone would not prevent a repeat.

Other techniques include fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams, which group possible causes under headings such as people, process, systems and external factors, and timeline analysis for complex events.

Remediation

Remediation puts affected customers or members back in the position they would have been in had the problem not happened. ASIC's Regulatory Guide 277 Consumer remediation applies to remediations by AFS and credit licensees started on or after 27 September 2022. Among other things, ASIC expects licensees to:

  • start remediation promptly and proactively, without waiting for customers to complain;
  • include everyone who has, or may have, been affected, automatically where possible;
  • make remediation free for consumers;
  • where information is missing, use assumptions that are beneficial to consumers; and
  • pay compensation that includes a component for the time value of money (interest or lost earnings).

For super funds, remediation often means correcting account balances and restoring lost investment earnings, including for members who have since left the fund.

What good looks like

  • A speak-up culture: people report incidents early without fear. A sudden drop in incident reports is often bad news, not good news.
  • Clear roles: the first line owns and manages incidents; the second line sets the framework, challenges assessments and usually decides or advises on regulatory reportability.
  • Consistent, documented judgements: similar events are assessed the same way, with reasons recorded.
  • Timeliness tracked: time from discovery to recording, to assessment and to lodgement is measured and reported.
  • Themes analysed: incidents are grouped by root cause, product and process to find systemic problems.
  • Joined-up reporting: one event that triggers APRA, ASIC and OAIC obligations is coordinated, so each regulator gets a consistent picture.
