Risk aggregation and correlation

How hundreds of individual risks become one enterprise risk profile, and why the way risks move together matters as much as their size.

Advanced About 23 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Boards need to see the organisation's total risk profile against its risk appetite, not a list of 300 separate risks. Getting from one to the other is aggregation.
  • Heat-map scores can't be added or averaged: they are rankings, not measurements. Qualitative roll-ups need clear rules and judgement.
  • When risks are quantified, the total depends on correlation: how likely risks are to go wrong together. Simply adding them assumes they always do; assuming independence can badly understate the total.
  • Correlations tend to rise in a crisis, so "diversification benefits" can vanish exactly when they're needed.
  • Concentration and interdependency, such as many critical operations relying on one provider, system or person, are often the biggest hidden aggregate risks.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why aggregating risks is harder than measuring each one, for both qualitative and quantitative risk profiles.
  2. Calculate a diversified total with the variance-covariance method and explain its assumptions.
  3. Explain tail dependence and why correlations estimated in normal times understate risk in a crisis.
  4. Critically assess diversification benefits in economic capital, drawing on research on integrated risk models.

Why aggregation is hard

A typical risk register contains hundreds of risks, owned by different teams and rated on a 5×5 matrix. Several problems arise when trying to sum them up:

  • Ratings are ordinal. A risk rated 16 is not "twice as bad" as one rated 8. Adding or averaging scores produces numbers that look precise but mean little.
  • Different units. Operational risks might be rated for financial loss, compliance risks for regulatory impact, strategic risks for member outcomes.
  • Overlap. The same underlying cause, such as an ageing administration system, appears in many risks under different names.
  • Dependence. Risks that look separate may be triggered by the same event.

Qualitative aggregation: rolling up the taxonomy

Most organisations aggregate qualitatively using their risk taxonomy: detailed risks roll up into categories (for example, "technology risk"), which roll up into top-level risk types that match the risk appetite statement.

Common roll-up rules each have strengths and weaknesses:

RuleHow it worksWatch out for
Worst-ofThe category takes the rating of its highest-rated risk.Simple and prudent, but hides whether one or twenty risks are high.
Count-basedReport the number of risks in each rating band, and outside appetite.Transparent, but treats all risks as equal and can be gamed by splitting or merging risks.
WeightedWeight each risk by importance and combine.Looks rigorous, but depends on arbitrary weights and still does arithmetic on ordinal scores.
Judgement overlayRisk owners and the risk function agree an overall rating, informed by the data and themes.Captures interactions the rules miss, but needs challenge and a documented rationale.

In practice, the most useful aggregate reports combine a transparent rule with a documented judgement overlay, and add thematic analysis: common root causes, shared controls that are failing, and trends across incidents, KRIs and audit findings.

Quantitative aggregation and correlation

Where risks are measured in dollars (for example, as a 1-in-100 year loss), the question becomes how to combine them. Three reference points help:

  • Simple sum: assumes all risks hit their bad outcome in the same year. This is the most conservative view (for most risk measures).
  • Independence: assumes risks have nothing to do with each other. The total is much smaller than the sum, because bad years rarely coincide.
  • Correlated: somewhere in between, depending on how strongly risks move together. The simplest method, the variance-covariance approach, combines standalone amounts using a correlation matrix. More sophisticated methods simulate risks jointly using dependence models called copulas.

The difference between the simple sum and the combined figure is often called the diversification benefit. It is real in normal conditions, but it depends entirely on the correlation assumptions, which are hard to estimate for risks with little data.

Try it: combine three risks

Uses the variance-covariance formula: total = √(Σ Σ ρij × xi × xj). This is a teaching simplification: it is exact only for certain distributions, and real correlations are uncertain. Nothing you enter leaves your browser.

Correlations rise in a crisis

Correlations estimated in normal times can understate how risks behave in a crisis. Research on share markets found that correlations between markets increase in bear markets (Longin & Solnik, 2001). More generally, a single correlation number can't capture "tail dependence", where risks are unrelated most of the time but fail together in extreme events (Embrechts et al., 2002). For operational risk, a single cyber attack, pandemic or provider failure can trigger losses across several risk types at once. Prudent practice is to stress correlation assumptions and to test combined scenarios directly. See Scenario analysis and stress testing.

Concentration and interdependency

Some of the largest aggregate risks come not from many risks adding up but from many things depending on one thing:

  • Provider concentration: one administrator, cloud provider or custodian supporting several critical operations, or several of your providers relying on the same fourth party. CPS 230 requires entities to manage the risks of their material service providers, including concentration.
  • Technology and data: a single core system, data store or identity platform whose failure would stop many processes.
  • People: key person dependencies in specialist roles.
  • Financial concentration: large exposures to one counterparty, sector, region or asset type. See concentration risk.

The practical tool is dependency mapping: mapping each critical operation to the people, processes, technology, data and providers it relies on, then looking for the nodes that many operations share. Those shared nodes deserve their own risk assessment, controls and scenarios.

Building the enterprise risk profile: a practical sequence

  1. Clean the inputs. Consistent taxonomy, rating scales and definitions across registers; remove duplicates.
  2. Roll up using stated rules, and record judgement overlays.
  3. Add the connectors: themes, root causes, shared dependencies and concentrations.
  4. Quantify where it helps, for material risks with enough data, stating the correlation assumptions.
  5. Stress the aggregate with combined scenarios.
  6. Compare with appetite for each top-level risk type, and explain the trend and the actions. See Board risk reporting design.

The Basel Committee's principles for risk data aggregation and reporting (BCBS 239) set out the data foundations large banks need for this: accurate, complete and timely risk data that can be aggregated quickly, including in a crisis. APRA's CPG 235 gives guidance on managing data risk.

Worked example: how correlation changes the total

Two risks need $100 million and $60 million of capital on their own. Using the common formula √(a² + b² + 2ρab), the combined amount depends on the correlation ρ:

Correlation between the two risksCombined capital ($m)Diversification benefit
1 (move together perfectly)160None
0.5140$20m
0.25129$31m
0 (independent)117$43m

The diversification benefit is attractive, but correlations estimated in normal times often rise in a crisis, so the benefit can vanish when it is needed most. Many entities therefore cap diversification benefits or test results with correlations set closer to 1.

Frequently asked questions

Is adding up risk ratings across the taxonomy a valid aggregation?

It can give a useful overview, but ratings are ordinal labels, not numbers, so adding or averaging them can mislead. Show the distribution and the highest risks, not just an average.

What is a concentration?

Exposure to the same driver through different routes, such as one service provider supporting several critical operations. Concentrations can make risks that look separate fail together.

Theory and research

Correlation is not dependence

The variance-covariance method on this page treats a single correlation number as a full description of how risks move together. That is only true for a narrow family of distributions. Embrechts et al. (2002) showed why linear correlation is misleading for skewed and heavy-tailed risks: two risks can have a low correlation yet a high chance of extreme losses together (tail dependence), and the correlation can even be impossible to reach for some combinations of distributions. Their recommended alternative is to model dependence with copulas, which separate the shape of each risk from the way they are joined.

Dependence changes in a crisis

Empirical evidence supports the concern. Longin and Solnik (2001) used extreme value theory to show that correlations between international share markets increase in bear markets but not in bull markets. Diversification is weakest exactly when it is needed. The same pattern is plausible for operational and conduct risks, where a single cause such as a cyber attack or a failed provider can trigger losses across several categories at once.

How much diversification benefit is real?

Rosenberg and Schuermann (2006) built the joint distribution of market, credit and operational risk for a large bank using copulas, and showed how much the total depends on the dependence assumptions: the diversification benefit can range widely between reasonable choices. Kretzschmar et al. (2010) went further. They compared the modular approach common in practice, where each risk is measured separately and combined with a correlation matrix, with a fully integrated model driven by economic scenarios. The modular approach understated risk, and their title states the conclusion: it could leave banks undercapitalised. For a board, the practical lesson is to treat any diversification benefit as an assumption to be stress-tested, not a saving to be banked.

Critical perspectives

  • Correlation estimates are fragile. For operational and conduct risks there is rarely enough data to estimate correlations at all. Values are usually expert judgements, which brings back the biases discussed in human factors and bias.
  • More complex is not always better. Copula models capture tail dependence, but they add parameters that are even harder to estimate. A simple model with stressed correlations may be more honest than a sophisticated one with false precision.
  • Qualitative aggregation has its own problems. Rolling up ratings by averaging or taking the maximum can hide concentrations or overstate them. There is little research on which qualitative roll-up rules produce the best decisions.
  • The biggest risks are often structural. Concentration on a single provider or system is a form of dependence that no correlation matrix shows. Mapping dependencies, as in mapping critical operations, may reveal more than statistics.

Seminar questions

  1. Use the calculator to find the diversification benefit at correlations of 0, 0.5 and 1. How would you explain to a board why the benefit is uncertain?
  2. Give an example of two operational risks that are nearly uncorrelated most of the time but likely to occur together in a crisis. How would you represent this in an aggregation model?
  3. Kretzschmar and colleagues argue that modular aggregation leaves banks undercapitalised. What would a super fund's equivalent problem look like?
  4. Is it better to aggregate risks with a simple sum and accept conservatism, or to model dependence and risk false precision? Argue your position.
