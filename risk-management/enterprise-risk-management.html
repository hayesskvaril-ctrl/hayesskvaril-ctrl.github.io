Enterprise risk management (ERM)

ERM means managing all of an organisation's risks together, as one portfolio, linked to its strategy, rather than in separate silos.

Intermediate About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 7 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • ERM looks at risk across the whole organisation, including how risks connect and add up, and uses that view to inform strategy and decisions.
  • In Australia, APRA's CPS 220 and SPS 220 effectively require an enterprise-wide risk management framework covering all material risks, approved and overseen by the board.
  • A shared risk taxonomy (a common list of risk categories) is the backbone that lets risks be compared and aggregated.
  • Risk culture often matters more than the framework documents. APRA requires boards of the entities it regulates to form a view of their risk culture.
  • Research finds ERM is associated with higher firm value in some studies, but critics warn it can become a paperwork exercise. Evidence of cause and effect is limited.

From silos to an enterprise view

Traditionally, risks were managed where they arose: the finance team handled financial risks, IT handled technology risk, legal handled legal risk, and each kept its own list. That works until risks interact. A cyber attack is an IT problem, but also an operational, legal, regulatory, reputational and member-harm problem at the same time.

ERM adds an organisation-wide layer on top. It doesn't replace specialist risk management; it connects it.

FeatureTraditional (siloed)ERM
ScopeIndividual risk types, managed separatelyAll material risks, as a portfolio
FocusAvoiding lossesAchieving objectives; downside and upside
Link to strategyConsidered after strategy is setConsidered when strategy is set
OwnershipSpecialist functionsEveryone, with clear roles (see Three Lines)
ReportingSeparate reports by risk typeIntegrated risk profile against appetite

The parts of a risk management framework

APRA's Prudential Standard CPS 220 (for banks and insurers) and SPS 220 (for super trustees) set out what an enterprise-wide risk management framework must include. In summary, the framework must cover all material risks and includes:

  • A risk appetite statement, approved by the board.
  • A risk management strategy, approved by the board, describing the key elements of the framework and how the entity manages each material risk.
  • A business plan that considers the entity's risks.
  • Policies and procedures for identifying, measuring, monitoring and controlling risk.
  • Clear roles and responsibilities, including a risk management function that is operationally independent, headed by a Chief Risk Officer (CRO).
  • Management information systems that give the board and management timely, accurate risk information.
  • Review: regular review of the framework, plus a comprehensive review by operationally independent, competent people at least every three years.

Boards of banks and insurers must also make an annual risk management declaration to APRA about the framework. APRA's CPG 220 gives guidance on its expectations. The requirements are principles-based, so the size and complexity of the framework should fit the organisation.

Seeing the whole: aggregation and connections

The most valuable thing ERM adds is the ability to see things the silos can't:

  • Aggregation: many small exposures to the same thing (one supplier, one region, one system) can add up to a large concentration.
  • Correlation and contagion: risks that tend to happen together, for example a market crash that also drives up member switching, liquidity needs and complaints.
  • Common causes: a single weakness, such as an outdated core system, that drives many different risks.
  • Emerging risks: new or changing risks (for example climate-related financial risks, new technologies such as artificial intelligence, or regulatory direction) that don't yet fit neatly in a silo. APRA has published guidance on managing climate-related financial risks (CPG 229).

Risk culture

Risk culture is how people in the organisation actually think about and behave towards risk: whether they speak up, whether bad news reaches the top, whether rules are followed when nobody is watching, and whether risk is part of everyday decisions.

APRA's CPS 220 requires the board to form a view of the entity's risk culture, and the extent to which it supports operating within risk appetite, and to identify and address any changes needed. APRA's 2018 Prudential Inquiry into the Commonwealth Bank found that the bank's continued financial success had "dulled the senses of the institution", contributing to complacency and a reactive approach to non-financial risks, and put culture and accountability at the centre of its 35 recommendations.

Signs of a healthy risk culture

  • Incidents and near misses are reported quickly, without fear of blame.
  • Leaders ask "what could go wrong?" and reward people who raise problems.
  • The second line is consulted early and its challenge is welcomed, not worked around.
  • Remuneration and performance reviews consider how results were achieved, not only what.
  • Audit and regulatory findings are fixed on time and at the root cause.

ERM maturity

Organisations often describe their ERM journey in stages. A simple, illustrative model:

StageWhat it looks like
1. InitialRisk managed ad hoc, in silos, mostly after problems occur.
2. DevelopingA framework and risk register exist; compliance-driven; limited link to decisions.
3. DefinedCommon taxonomy and method; risk appetite with metrics; regular reporting to the board.
4. IntegratedRisk considered in strategy, planning, investment and product decisions; good data and aggregation.
5. OptimisedRisk-informed culture; forward-looking scenario analysis; continuous improvement.

Does ERM work? What the research says Advanced

ERM has been studied for about two decades. The evidence is mixed and should be read carefully:

  • What drives adoption? Beasley, Clune and Hermanson (2005) found that organisations were further along in implementing ERM when they had a chief risk officer, a more independent board, visible support from the CEO and CFO, and were larger; certain industries, such as banking and insurance, were also further along.
  • Does it add value? Hoyt and Liebenberg (2011) studied US insurers and found that those using ERM had significantly higher firm value. Other studies have found smaller or no effects, and it is hard to prove ERM causes better performance rather than well-run firms being more likely to adopt it.
  • The critique. Power (2009) argued that ERM can become a defensive, audit-trail exercise that gives a false sense of security, and that narrow ideas of risk appetite contributed to the failures of the global financial crisis.

The practical lesson: ERM adds value when it genuinely changes decisions and behaviour, and adds little when it is mainly documentation.

What CPS 220 and SPS 220 expect of the framework (summary)

For APRA-regulated entities, ERM is largely set by CPS 220 (banks and insurers) and SPS 220 (super trustees). See CPS 220 for the detail.

ElementIn brief
Risk management strategyHow the institution manages its material risks, approved by the board.
Risk appetite statementThe degree of risk the board is prepared to accept, with tolerances for material risks.
Business planHow strategy is delivered, linked to risk.
Policies and proceduresCovering each material risk.
Risk management functionOperationally independent of the business, headed by a chief risk officer, with a designated compliance function.
ReviewRegular review, and a comprehensive, operationally independent review at least every three years.
Risk management declarationBanks and insurers give APRA an annual board risk management declaration.

Common mistakes

  • ERM as a reporting exercise, separate from strategy and decisions.
  • A taxonomy nobody uses, so risks can't be added up across the organisation.
  • Emerging risks ignored because they don't fit existing categories.
  • Risk culture assumed rather than assessed.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. How does risk information change our strategic decisions?
  2. What are our top emerging risks, and who is watching them?
  3. When was our framework last independently reviewed, and what did it find?

Frequently asked questions

Do we need ISO 31000 or COSO?

Neither is legally required. Both are frameworks organisations use to structure ERM. APRA-regulated entities must meet CPS 220 or SPS 220 whichever framework they use. See Core frameworks compared.

Who owns ERM?

The board is ultimately responsible; management runs it; the risk function designs the framework and challenges the business; internal audit provides independent assurance.
