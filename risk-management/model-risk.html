Model risk management

How organisations manage the risk that the models they rely on for decisions, from credit scores to actuarial valuations and AI, are wrong or misused.

Intermediate About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Model risk is the risk of loss or harm from decisions based on models that are wrong or used inappropriately.
  • Models include statistical, actuarial and financial models, AI and machine learning, and complex spreadsheets.
  • The core controls are a complete model inventory, risk-based tiering, independent validation, effective challenge, and ongoing monitoring.
  • AI models need the same discipline plus attention to explainability, bias and drift.
  • International regulators have recently refreshed their guidance, and APRA's April 2026 AI letter raised expectations for AI models in Australia.

What counts as a model

A model is a method that turns input data into estimates or outputs used to make decisions, using statistical, mathematical, financial, actuarial or machine learning techniques. In financial services, models are everywhere:

AreaExamples
CreditCredit scoring, probability of default and expected credit loss models.
Capital and liquidityInternal ratings-based models, stress testing and liquidity forecasting.
InsurancePricing, reserving and claims liability valuations prepared by actuaries.
Superannuation and fundsUnit pricing and crediting rates, asset valuation of unlisted assets, retirement income projections and calculators.
Financial crimeTransaction monitoring, fraud detection and sanctions screening rules and scores.
AI and machine learningCustomer triage, document processing, chatbots and generative AI tools.

Complex spreadsheets and other "end-user computing" tools often act as models too, and are a frequent source of errors.

Where model risk comes from

  • The model is wrong: flawed design or assumptions, poor or unrepresentative data, coding or implementation errors.
  • The model is used wrongly: used for a purpose, product or population it wasn't built for, or relied on without understanding its limitations.
  • The world changes: a model that worked well stops working as conditions shift ("drift"), such as a credit model built before a sharp rise in interest rates.

A model risk management framework

  • Inventory: a complete list of models, their owners, purpose, tier, validation status and known limitations. You can't manage models you don't know about.
  • Risk tiering: more material and complex models get more frequent and deeper validation (see the example below).
  • Independent validation: by people not involved in building the model, testing conceptual soundness, data quality, implementation and outcomes (for example by backtesting predictions against what actually happened, and benchmarking against alternative models).
  • Effective challenge: critical analysis by people with the competence, independence and authority to have their concerns acted on.
  • Monitoring and change control: ongoing checks against performance thresholds, and controlled, validated changes.

Example: a simple model tiering approach

Illustrative only. Organisations set their own criteria.
TierTypical characteristicsTypical validation
Tier 1 (high)Drives capital, pricing, member or customer outcomes or financial reporting; complex; large exposures.Full independent validation before use and at least annually or every few years; ongoing monitoring reported to a model risk committee.
Tier 2 (medium)Material but less complex, or used for internal decisions.Independent review on a longer cycle; periodic performance monitoring.
Tier 3 (low)Limited impact, simple, easy to check.Owner review and inventory registration; spot checks.

AI and machine learning models

AI models are models, and the same principles apply, but they bring extra challenges: they are harder to explain, can learn biases from data, may change as they are retrained, and are often supplied by third parties whose inner workings can't be inspected. Validation needs to cover fairness, robustness and explainability, and monitoring needs to be more frequent. See AI governance.

Regulatory expectations

SourceRelevance
APRAAPRA has no single model risk standard. Model requirements sit within specific standards, such as the capital standards for banks approved to use internal models and the actuarial requirements for insurers, and within CPS 220 risk management and CPS 230 operational risk. APRA's April 2026 letter on AI set specific expectations for AI-related risk.
United StatesThe long-standing SR 11-7 guidance, which popularised "effective challenge", was replaced by revised interagency guidance on model risk management in April 2026.
United KingdomThe PRA's SS1/23, in effect since May 2024, sets five principles for banks: model identification and risk classification, governance, development and use, independent validation, and risk mitigants.
CanadaOSFI's revised Guideline E-23, taking effect in May 2027, expressly covers AI and machine learning models alongside traditional ones.

International guidance is often used as a reference point by Australian institutions, particularly those with overseas operations.
