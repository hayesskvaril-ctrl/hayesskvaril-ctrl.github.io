Climate risk: the research

What finance research says about whether markets price climate risk, how it could spread through the financial system, and how investors respond.

Advanced About 21 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Climate risk reaches finance through physical risk (damage from climate events) and transition risk (the move to a low-carbon economy).
  • Some evidence suggests markets price carbon risk: higher-emitting firms have earned higher stock returns, consistent with investors demanding compensation.
  • Physical risk appears in some asset prices, such as a discount on homes exposed to sea level rise.
  • Network studies show climate losses could be amplified through links between financial institutions.
  • Many institutional investors believe climate risk matters financially, and many prefer engagement to divestment.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish physical and transition risk and explain how each reaches financial institutions.
  2. Evaluate evidence on whether asset prices reflect carbon and climate risk.
  3. Explain the logic and limits of climate stress testing, including network effects.
  4. Critically assess the debates about measurement, time horizons and investor responses.

The idea in plain English

Climate change creates financial risk in two main ways. Physical risk is damage from events such as floods, fires and rising seas. Transition risk comes from the shift to a low-carbon economy: new policy, technology and preferences can cut the value of emissions-heavy assets. APRA's guidance on climate change financial risks and Australia's mandatory climate reporting both assume these risks matter. Finance research asks harder questions: do markets already price them, how could they spread, and how do investors respond? This page builds on climate risk and climate-related financial disclosures.

Theory and research

Is climate risk priced?

If markets see climate risk, it should show up in prices. Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021) found that US firms with higher carbon emissions earned higher stock returns, after controlling for other factors. They interpret this as investors demanding compensation for exposure to carbon (transition) risk. For physical risk, Bernstein et al. (2019) found US coastal homes exposed to projected sea level rise sold for about 7% less than comparable unexposed homes, with the discount driven mainly by more sophisticated buyers. Giglio et al. (2021) review this growing literature and conclude that evidence of pricing is mixed across markets and that measuring exposure well remains a central challenge.

Stress testing and contagion

Battiston et al. (2017) mapped financial institutions' exposures to climate-policy-relevant sectors, such as fossil fuels, utilities and energy-intensive industry, and showed how losses could be amplified through the web of exposures between institutions. Their point is that direct exposures understate the risk: second-round effects matter. This thinking underpins supervisory climate scenario analysis, including APRA's climate vulnerability assessment of large banks (see scenario analysis and stress testing).

How investors respond

Krueger et al. (2020) surveyed institutional investors and found most believe climate risks have financial implications and have already begun to materialise, particularly regulatory risk. Many preferred to engage with companies rather than divest. This is relevant to super trustees, who must weigh climate risk as part of their duty to act in members' best financial interests (see superannuation).

Applying the research: implications for a super trustee

How the evidence might inform an investment governance framework:

FindingPossible implication
Markets appear to price some carbon riskTransition risk may already be partly reflected in prices, but can change quickly with policy.
Physical risk appears in some asset pricesProperty and infrastructure holdings in exposed areas need specific assessment.
Losses can be amplified through financial linksSystem-wide exercises, such as APRA's insurance climate vulnerability assessment, complement fund-level analysis.
Many investors prefer engagement to divestmentThe trustee's stewardship approach should be consistent with members' best financial interests.

An interpretive application for discussion, not a finding of fact about any organisation.

Frequently asked questions

Is the evidence settled?

No. Findings depend on the time period, data and methods, and some results are contested. That is why scenario analysis, not single forecasts, is the main tool.

Critical perspectives

  • Mispriced or priced? Higher returns for emitters could reflect a risk premium, or could mean markets have not yet fully priced the risk. The two stories have very different implications, and researchers disagree.
  • Horizon mismatch. Climate risk plays out over decades; most risk models, business plans and board terms are much shorter.
  • Scenario uncertainty. Stress tests depend on scenarios about policy and technology that are deeply uncertain, so results are best read as ranges, not forecasts.
  • Data and greenwashing. Emissions and exposure data are incomplete and sometimes overstated or understated, which can mislead both investors and supervisors (Delmas & Burbano, 2011).

Seminar questions

  1. Bolton and Kacperczyk find higher returns for higher emitters. Is this evidence that markets price carbon risk, or that they misprice it? What evidence would help decide?
  2. Using Battiston and colleagues' network view, explain why a super fund with little direct fossil-fuel exposure could still face climate-related losses.
  3. Krueger, Sautner and Starks find many investors prefer engagement to divestment. What are the arguments for each, from a trustee's perspective?
  4. How should a board deal with the mismatch between the time horizon of climate risk and its own planning horizon?

    Reviews research on how climate risks affect asset prices and how to discount long-run climate costs, and sets out open questions for the field.
  • Bolton, P., & Kacperczyk, M. (2021). Do investors care about carbon risk? Journal of Financial Economics, 142(2), 517–549. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.05.008 Peer-reviewed
    Finds that US firms with higher carbon emissions earn higher stock returns, consistent with investors demanding compensation for exposure to carbon risk.

    Maps financial institutions' exposures to climate-policy-relevant sectors and shows how losses could be amplified through interconnections between institutions.
  • Krueger, P., Sautner, Z., & Starks, L. T. (2020). The importance of climate risks for institutional investors. The Review of Financial Studies, 33(3), 1067–1111. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz137 Peer-reviewed
    A survey of institutional investors finding they believe climate risks matter financially and have begun to materialise, and that many prefer engagement to divestment.
