Quantitative operational risk

How risk teams put numbers on operational losses: how often losses happen, how big they get, and how bad a really bad year could be.

Advanced About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Quantitative models split operational losses into frequency (how many loss events a year) and severity (how big each one is), then combine them into a distribution of possible annual losses.
  • Operational loss severities are heavy-tailed: most losses are small, but a rare few are enormous and dominate the total. Averages alone badly understate the risk.
  • Expected loss is the average year. Value-at-risk (VaR) is a bad year at a chosen confidence level. Expected shortfall is the average of the years worse than that.
  • Banks no longer use their own models to set operational risk capital in Australia. Since 1 January 2023, APRA's APS 115 uses the Basel III standardised measurement approach. Quantitative methods are still widely used for scenario analysis, risk appetite and understanding exposures.
  • Every model is only as good as its data and assumptions. Treat the outputs as a way to structure judgement, not as precise truth.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the loss distribution approach: frequency, severity and the aggregate loss distribution.
  2. Compare value at risk and expected shortfall using the theory of coherent risk measures.
  3. Summarise what empirical research shows about the size, drivers and heavy tails of operational losses.
  4. Critically evaluate why model-based operational risk capital was abandoned for regulatory minimums, and what role models still play.

Why quantify operational risk?

Most organisations rate operational risks on a heat map: likelihood from 1 to 5, consequence from 1 to 5. That is a good start, but it has limits. It can't add risks together, it hides the difference between "often small" and "rarely catastrophic", and it can't answer the questions boards increasingly ask: How much could we lose in a bad year? Is our capital or reserve enough? Which risks drive the tail?

Quantitative methods try to answer those questions with numbers. This page explains the core ideas in plain terms. It assumes you know the basics on the Operational risk and Risk assessment methodologies pages.

Frequency, severity and the aggregate loss

The classic approach is the loss distribution approach. It treats a year of operational losses as the result of two separate random processes:

  • Frequency: how many loss events happen in a year. This is usually modelled with a Poisson distribution, which needs one number: the average count per year.
  • Severity: how big each loss is. This is modelled with a skewed distribution (commonly lognormal, or heavier-tailed choices such as Pareto-type distributions) fitted to past losses.

Combining the two gives the aggregate loss distribution: the range of possible total losses in a year and how likely each is. There is rarely a neat formula for it, so it is usually built by Monte Carlo simulation. A computer simulates thousands of years: for each year it draws a number of events, draws a size for each, and adds them up. The pile of simulated annual totals is the distribution.

Heavy tails: why the average misleads

Operational losses are extremely uneven. Many small errors, such as a mistyped payment or a minor system outage, sit alongside rare events, such as a major fraud, a large remediation program or a regulatory penalty, that can be thousands of times bigger. Analysis of the loss data the Basel Committee collected from banks found that severity distributions were very heavy-tailed, particularly for some event types (Moscadelli, 2004).

This has practical consequences:

  • A few events drive the total. In a heavy-tailed world, the worst year is usually bad because of one or two very large events, not because of many small ones.
  • History understates the tail. Ten years of internal data may contain no truly extreme event, even though one is plausible. That is why models also use external loss data and scenario analysis.
  • Estimates are unstable. Small changes in the assumed tail shape can change a 1-in-1,000 year estimate many times over. The simulation below shows this.

Measuring the risk: expected loss, VaR and expected shortfall

MeasurePlain-English meaningStrengths and weaknesses
Expected loss (EL)The average annual loss over many years.Useful for pricing and budgeting. Says nothing about how bad a bad year can be.
Value-at-risk (VaR) at, say, 99.9%The annual loss that is exceeded in only 0.1% of years (a "1-in-1,000 year").Simple and widely understood. Says nothing about how bad the years beyond it are, and has some mathematical drawbacks when adding risks together (it is not always "sub-additive").
Expected shortfall (ES) at, say, 99%The average loss in the worst 1% of years.Looks into the tail and behaves well mathematically (it is a "coherent" risk measure). Needs even more data to estimate reliably.
Unexpected lossVaR minus expected loss: the part of a bad year not covered by the normal budget.The traditional basis for holding capital against losses.

The idea of "coherent" risk measures comes from Artzner and colleagues (1999), and Acerbi and Tasche (2002) showed why expected shortfall meets those conditions where VaR can fail. Basel III moved market risk capital from VaR to expected shortfall for this reason.

Worked example: reading the numbers

Suppose a fund administrator's model gives: expected annual operational loss $1.2 million, 99% VaR $6 million, 99% expected shortfall $11 million (illustrative figures).

  • In a typical year, the budget should absorb about $1.2 million of losses.
  • About one year in a hundred, losses would exceed $6 million. That is $4.8 million of unexpected loss beyond the average.
  • When such a year happens, the average loss is around $11 million, nearly double the VaR figure. This gap is the heavy tail at work.

Where the data comes from

Under the former Basel II advanced measurement approach, banks had to combine four data sources. The same four remain good practice for any serious quantification:

SourceWhat it addsWatch out for
Internal loss dataYour own history of loss events (from the incident register), the best guide to frequency and to smaller losses.Under-reporting, inconsistent capture of amounts and dates, and few large events.
External loss dataIndustry loss databases and public cases, which show large events that haven't (yet) happened to you.Scaling: a loss at a much bigger or different business may not translate.
Scenario analysisStructured expert estimates of severe but plausible events, filling the tail. See Operational risk.Anchoring, optimism and group-think in workshops.
Business environment and internal control factorsCurrent indicators, such as KRIs, control test results and audit findings, that adjust a backward-looking model to today.Hard to translate into numbers without false precision.

The regulatory context in Australia

  • Bank capital: Basel II allowed banks to use their own models (the advanced measurement approach, with a 99.9% one-year confidence level) to set operational risk capital. The Basel Committee's 2017 Basel III reforms replaced all the previous approaches with a single standardised measurement approach, largely because internal models were complex and produced results that were hard to compare. APRA implemented this in APS 115, and ADIs have used it since 1 January 2023 at the latest. It is based on a bank's business size, scaled by its own history of losses.
  • Superannuation: RSE licensees must hold financial resources to address losses from operational risk under SPS 114 Operational Risk Financial Requirement. Trustees set their own target amount, and quantitative analysis of potential losses is one way to support it.
  • CPS 230: APRA's operational risk standard does not require statistical models. It does require entities to understand their operational risk profile, use scenario analysis and set tolerances for disruption, and quantitative thinking helps with all three.

Limits and model risk

  • Data scarcity: the events that matter most are the ones you have least data on.
  • Assumption sensitivity: the choice of severity distribution and how the tail is fitted can change results more than the data does.
  • Dependence: models often assume loss types are independent, but a single cause (such as a cyber attack) can trigger several at once. See Risk aggregation and correlation.
  • False precision: a figure of "$43.7 million at 99.9%" implies a certainty that doesn't exist. Good practice reports ranges, tests sensitivities and explains the assumptions to decision-makers.

Theory and research

What makes a good risk measure?

Artzner et al. (1999) set out four properties a risk measure should have to be called coherent: monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity and subadditivity. Subadditivity is the one that matters most in practice: the risk of two activities combined should be no more than the sum of their separate risks, because combining them can only help or do nothing. Value at risk can fail this test for heavy-tailed or lumpy losses, which are exactly what operational risk produces. Acerbi and Tasche (2002) showed that a properly defined expected shortfall, the average loss beyond the VaR point, is coherent for any loss distribution. This is the theoretical reason why expected shortfall has increasingly replaced VaR in regulation and internal models.

What the data shows about operational losses

Research using databases of publicly reported losses has shaped the field. de Fontnouvelle et al. (2006) used external data on large bank losses to estimate how heavy the tail of operational losses is and what this implies for capital. Chernobai et al. (2011) analysed US financial institutions' reported losses from 1980 to 2005. Most losses could be traced to breakdowns of internal control, and losses were more likely at firms that were younger, more complex and riskier on credit, and where the CEO had stronger option-based incentives. That last finding links operational risk to governance and remuneration, not only to processes.

The same data also shows the limits of measurement. Cope et al. (2009) examined whether banks could credibly estimate a 1-in-1,000-year loss (the 99.9% quantile used for regulatory capital) from the loss data they actually hold. Estimates were driven by a handful of extreme events and by modelling choices such as the severity distribution and the threshold for recording losses, and they were very hard to validate.

From internal models to a formula

These problems help explain the Basel Committee's decision to replace internal models (the advanced measurement approaches) with a standardised formula for minimum capital, which APRA implemented as APS 115. The change was contested. Peters et al. (2016) stress-tested the proposed standardised approach and argued that it can be unstable, insensitive to risk and super-additive, meaning it may not reward diversification. Their critique and the regulators' concerns about model variability are two sides of the same trade-off between risk sensitivity and comparability.

Critical perspectives

  • The tail is estimated from very few points. A 99.9% annual quantile is a 1-in-1,000-year loss. No organisation has that much history, so the answer depends on external data, scenarios and distribution choices that are hard to test.
  • Public loss data is biased. Databases of reported losses over-represent large, newsworthy events and US institutions. Findings about drivers may not transfer to Australian super funds or insurers.
  • Correlation or causation? Studies linking losses to firm characteristics such as incentives are observational. They show associations, not that changing pay would reduce losses.
  • Is a formula better than a flawed model? A formula based on size is comparable and hard to game, but it ignores the quality of controls. A model is risk-sensitive but can be tuned. There is no consensus on which is worse.
  • Numbers can crowd out judgement. A precise-looking capital figure can distract boards from the qualitative question of whether operational risk is actually well managed.

Seminar questions

  1. Explain, with a simple numerical example, how value at risk can fail subadditivity. Why does this matter more for operational risk than for many market risks?
  2. Chernobai, Jorion and Yu found that operational losses were linked to CEO option-based pay. Propose a mechanism that could explain this, and a study that could test whether it is causal.
  3. Using the simulator on this page, change the severity distribution's tail and observe the 99.9% loss. What does this tell you about model risk?
  4. Was the Basel Committee right to replace internal models with a standardised formula? Use Cope et al. and Peters et al. to argue both sides.
  5. If minimum capital is now set by formula, what is the remaining value of loss distribution modelling for an Australian bank or super fund?

Further reading

  • Artzner, P., Delbaen, F., Eber, J.-M., & Heath, D. (1999). Coherent measures of risk. Mathematical Finance, 9(3), 203–228. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9965.00068 Peer-reviewed
    Proposes four properties a sensible risk measure should have (the 'coherence' axioms) and shows that value at risk can fail one of them, subadditivity, so it can penalise diversification.
  • Chernobai, A., Jorion, P., & Yu, F. (2011). The determinants of operational risk in U.S. financial institutions. Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 46(6), 1683–1725. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109011000500 Peer-reviewed
    Using publicly reported losses from 1980 to 2005, finds most operational losses trace to breakdowns of internal control, and that losses are more likely at younger, more complex firms with higher credit risk and stronger CEO option-based pay.
  • Cope, E. W., Mignola, G., Antonini, G., & Ugoccioni, R. (2009). Challenges and pitfalls in measuring operational risk from loss data. Journal of Operational Risk, 4(4), 3–27. https://doi.org/10.21314/JOP.2009.069 Peer-reviewed
    Shows how hard it is to estimate operational risk at very high quantiles such as 99.9% from the loss data banks actually have, and how unstable model results and validation can be.
