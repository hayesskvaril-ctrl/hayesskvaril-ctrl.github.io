Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability

The big ideas from psychology, sociology and safety science about how risk is perceived and why organisations fail, and what they mean for risk management in financial services.

Advanced About 25 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Risk is not only probability times impact: people judge risks by qualities such as dread and unfamiliarity, which is why public and expert views differ.
  • Social amplification explains why some incidents cause reactions far beyond their direct harm, through media, institutions and trust.
  • Normal accident theory argues that complex, tightly coupled systems will eventually fail in unexpected ways.
  • High reliability research studies organisations that operate hazardous systems with few failures, and the practices that help them.
  • Systems approaches see accidents as failures of control across the whole system, not just broken parts.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the psychometric paradigm of risk perception and the social amplification of risk.
  2. Compare normal accident theory and high reliability theory, and explain how they can be reconciled.
  3. Apply systems thinking about accidents to operational risk in financial services.
  4. Critically assess what these theories can and cannot tell a risk manager.

The idea in plain English

Risk management frameworks treat risk as likelihood multiplied by consequence. That is useful, but it leaves out much of what researchers have learned about risk: why people fear some risks more than others, why one incident becomes a national scandal while a worse one is ignored, and why complex organisations keep having accidents despite careful controls. This page introduces the theories that answer those questions. It builds on What is risk management? and root cause analysis.

Theory and research

How people perceive risk

Slovic (1987) summarised research in the "psychometric paradigm" of risk perception. When people judge the riskiness of hazards, they don't simply estimate expected deaths or losses. Two qualities matter most: dread (catastrophic, uncontrollable, involuntary, unfair) and unknown (new, unobservable, delayed). Nuclear power scored high on both; driving scored low, despite far more deaths. For financial services, this helps explain why data breaches and scams involving vulnerable people provoke strong reactions: they are involuntary, feel uncontrollable and affect people who didn't choose the risk.

The social amplification of risk

Kasperson et al. (1988) proposed that risk events pass through "amplification stations" (the media, regulators, advocacy groups, social networks) that can intensify or dampen the public response. Amplified events create ripple effects: loss of trust, regulatory change, litigation and damage to other firms in the same industry. The Optus and Medibank data breaches, which contributed to momentum for cyber security and privacy law reform affecting many organisations that had no breach, are an example of amplification in practice. Understanding amplification helps risk functions assess reputational and regulatory consequences that a simple impact scale misses.

Normal accidents

Perrow (1984) argued that some systems are both interactively complex (parts interact in unexpected ways) and tightly coupled (there is little time or slack to stop a failure spreading). In such systems, multiple small failures will eventually combine in ways no one anticipated, so accidents are "normal", an inherent property of the system. Adding more safety devices can even make things worse by adding complexity. Modern financial systems, with interlinked platforms, automated processes and real-time payments, share some of these features.

High reliability organisations

A more optimistic research programme studied organisations that operate hazardous technology with very few failures, such as air traffic control and aircraft carriers. La Porte and Consolini (1991) set the agenda for this "high reliability" research, describing organisations that combine hierarchy for routine work with flexible, expertise-based decision-making in a crisis, and that constantly look for signs of failure. Later high reliability research summarised practices such as a preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify and deference to expertise, which have been widely adopted in healthcare and increasingly in operational resilience thinking.

Reconciling the two, and moving beyond

Normal accident theory says failure is inevitable; high reliability theory says it can be avoided. Shrivastava et al. (2009) argued that the two look at accidents at different points in time and can be reconciled, although both are hard to falsify. Leveson et al. (2009) argued that both miss a key point from safety engineering: accidents arise from inadequate control of the whole system, including management decisions and feedback, which is the systems approach described on the root cause analysis page (Leveson, 2004). Reason (2000)'s Swiss cheese model sits between them: layered defences reduce failures, but latent conditions will eventually line up.

Critical perspectives

  • Are financial systems like nuclear plants? The theories were developed for physical hazards. Financial failures are often slower and involve human decisions and incentives. The analogy is useful but imperfect.
  • Hard to test. Both normal accident and high reliability theories are difficult to falsify, as Shrivastava and colleagues note. They are better treated as lenses than as predictive models.
  • Perception is not error. The psychometric paradigm is sometimes used to dismiss public concern as irrational. Slovic's work suggests public judgements reflect legitimate values, such as fairness and consent, that expert risk measures leave out.
  • Amplification can be deliberate. Firms and interest groups can also seek to attenuate risks. Who controls the "amplification stations" is a question of power as well as psychology.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Slovic's dread and unknown dimensions, explain why a data breach at a health insurer provoked a stronger public reaction than a larger financial loss at another firm.
  2. Trace the social amplification of a recent Australian financial services incident. Which amplification stations mattered most?
  3. Is a large super fund's administration system "complex and tightly coupled" in Perrow's sense? What would reduce coupling?
  4. Which high reliability practices could an operations team at a bank or super fund adopt, and what would get in the way?
  5. Normal accident theory says some accidents are inevitable. What does this mean for CPS 230's focus on tolerance levels and recovery?

Further reading

  • Slovic, P. (1987). Perception of risk. Science, 236(4799), 280–285. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3563507 Peer-reviewed
    Shows that lay people judge risk on qualitative dimensions, especially 'dread' and 'unknown', not just expected deaths, which explains gaps between expert and public views of risk.
  • Kasperson, R. E., Renn, O., Slovic, P., Brown, H. S., Emel, J., Goble, R., Kasperson, J. X., & Ratick, S. (1988). The social amplification of risk: A conceptual framework. Risk Analysis, 8(2), 177–187. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x Peer-reviewed
    Proposes that risk events interact with psychological, social, institutional and cultural processes that amplify or attenuate public responses, producing 'ripple effects' far beyond the direct harm.
  • La Porte, T. R., & Consolini, P. M. (1991). Working in practice but not in theory: Theoretical challenges of "high-reliability organizations". Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, 1(1), 19–48. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.jpart.a037070 Peer-reviewed
    Launched high reliability organisation research by studying organisations such as air traffic control that operate hazardous systems with remarkably few failures, and asking how they do it.
  • Leveson, N., Dulac, N., Marais, K., & Carroll, J. (2009). Moving beyond normal accidents and high reliability organizations: A systems approach to safety in complex systems. Organization Studies, 30(2–3), 227–249. https://doi.org/10.1177/0170840608101478 Peer-reviewed
    Critiques both normal accident theory and high reliability research, arguing that a systems approach from safety engineering better explains and prevents accidents in complex organisations.
