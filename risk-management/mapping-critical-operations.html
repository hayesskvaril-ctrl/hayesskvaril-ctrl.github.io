Mapping critical operations end to end

A practical method for tracing a critical operation from start to finish, recording what it depends on, and finding the single points of failure, with a worked super fund example.

Advanced About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • CPS 230 requires entities to document the processes and resources behind each critical operation: people, technology, information, facilities and service providers, and how they depend on each other.
  • Define operations by the outcome for the member or customer, not by team, so the map is truly end to end.
  • Mapping resources against process steps exposes single points of failure and concentrations.
  • For every serious failure, check the workaround's capacity against the tolerance level's minimum service levels.
  • Maps are only useful if they drive tolerance setting, scenario testing and change assessments, and are kept up to date.

The idea in plain English

You can't protect an operation you don't understand. Mapping a critical operation means tracing it end to end, from the moment a member or customer starts something to the moment they get the outcome, and recording every person, system, piece of data, building and outside provider it depends on. The map then shows where a single failure would stop everything, which is what business continuity planning and tolerance setting need to know.

CPS 230 requires APRA-regulated entities to identify and document the processes and resources needed to deliver critical operations, including people, technology, information, facilities and service providers, the interdependencies across them, and the associated risks, obligations, key data and controls. This page sets out a practical method. It builds on CPS 230 and business continuity.

Which operations are critical?

A critical operation is a process that, if disrupted beyond tolerance, would have a material adverse impact on customers, members or policyholders, or on the entity's role in the financial system. CPS 230 lists operations that are critical by default unless an entity can justify otherwise. For RSE licensees these include investment management and fund administration; for all entities they include customer enquiries and the systems and infrastructure needed to support critical operations. Banks and insurers have their own defaults (for example payments and claims processing). Entities then add others from their own assessment.

How broadly to define each operation is a judgement. "Fund administration" is too big to map usefully in one go, so many funds break it into member-facing outcomes such as paying benefits, processing rollovers, allocating contributions and answering enquiries. Defining operations by outcome for the member, rather than by team, keeps the map end to end.

A seven-step method

  1. Define the outcome and boundary. What does the member or customer receive? Where does the operation start and end?
  2. Map the process steps. Start at a high level (six to ten steps), and only go deeper where risk or complexity demands it.
  3. Map resources against each step. People (roles, key persons, skills), technology (applications, infrastructure), information (data sets, records), facilities (sites, equipment) and service providers, including important fourth parties.
  4. Overlay obligations, key data and controls. Which legal deadlines and requirements apply at each step? Which controls operate there?
  5. Find single points of failure and concentrations. Resources used by many steps or many operations, with no alternative.
  6. Link to tolerance levels and plans. For each serious failure, what is the workaround, how much capacity does it have, and does that keep the operation within tolerance?
  7. Test and maintain. Use the map to design scenario tests, and update it whenever processes, systems or providers change.

    Common pitfalls

    • Mapping the org chart, not the operation. Each team maps its own part; nobody maps the hand-offs, which is where many failures happen.
    • Too much detail too early. Hundred-step maps no one maintains. Start high level and deepen where risk sits.
    • Stopping at the service provider. The administrator may rely on one cloud region or a single print house. Material fourth parties belong on the map.
    • Workarounds that don't scale. Plans that assume manual processing without checking volumes against minimum service levels.
    • Maps that sit on a shelf. A map is only useful if it drives tolerance levels, scenario tests, change assessments and investment decisions.

    Check your understanding

    1. Under CPS 230, which of these are critical operations by default for RSE licensees?

    CPS 230 lists investment management and fund administration for RSE licensees, and customer enquiries and supporting systems for all entities.

    2. Why map resources against process steps?

    Seeing which resources support many steps without alternatives shows where resilience investment matters.

    3. A workaround exists but handles only 10% of normal volume. What should you check?

    Tolerance levels include minimum service levels under alternative arrangements.
