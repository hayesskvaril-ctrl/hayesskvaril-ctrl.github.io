Service provider concentration, fourth parties and exit planning

How dependence on service providers builds up, why fourth parties and system concentration matter, and how to plan an exit that would actually work, with a substitutability scorer.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 7 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Concentration works at three levels: one provider running many operations, several providers relying on the same fourth party, and many institutions relying on the same few providers.
  • CPS 230 requires entities to manage risks from fourth parties that material service providers rely on, including location and concentration risks.
  • Substitutability, meaning how easily a provider could be replaced, should drive how detailed the exit plan is.
  • Plan for both a planned exit and a stressed exit, where the provider may not cooperate and time is short.
  • For deeply embedded providers, be honest that fast exit may be impossible, and focus on contingency and early warning.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the layers of service provider concentration, including fourth parties and shared infrastructure.
  2. Assess a provider's substitutability and the difference between a planned and a stressed exit.
  3. Use transaction cost economics and outsourcing research to explain why switching providers is hard.
  4. Critically evaluate what makes an exit plan credible, and the limits of exit as a risk control.

The idea in plain English

Relying on a service provider is normal. The risk is in how dependent you become: on one provider, on the providers behind it, and on being able to leave if things go wrong. An exit plan written the week a provider fails is too late. The time to understand how hard it would be to leave is before you sign, and every time you renew.

This page goes beyond the basics in third-party risk to look at concentration, fourth parties, substitutability and exit planning in depth.

What CPS 230 expects (summary)

  • A service provider management policy that covers identifying material service providers, managing the risks of those arrangements, and managing risks from the fourth parties that material service providers rely on to deliver critical operations.
  • Assessment of the risks of relying on a provider, including risks from geographic location and from concentration, both of the provider and of the parties it relies on.
  • A formal, legally binding agreement for each material arrangement, including termination rights and provisions supporting an orderly exit. Existing arrangements had until the earlier of their next renewal or 1 July 2026 to meet the contract requirements.
  • The ability to keep critical operations within tolerance if a provider fails, which means exit and contingency arrangements that actually work.

Paraphrased. Read CPS 230 and CPG 230 for the precise requirements, including the April 2026 targeted amendments.

Three layers of concentration

LayerExampleWhy it matters
Entity concentrationOne administrator runs every critical member process.A single failure stops many operations at once.
Fourth-party concentrationSeveral of your "different" providers all run on the same cloud region or software vendor.Diversification on paper disappears in practice. It only shows up if you map fourth parties.
System concentrationMany funds or banks depend on the same few administrators, cloud providers or security vendors.One event can hit many institutions simultaneously. The July 2024 global IT outage caused by a faulty CrowdStrike security update showed how quickly a single vendor's failure can spread.

Regulators overseas have responded with regimes for critical third parties, such as the European Union's Digital Operational Resilience Act and the United Kingdom's critical third parties regime. In Australia, system-level concentration is currently addressed mainly through entity-level requirements such as CPS 230.

Assessing substitutability

Substitutability is how easily a provider could be replaced. It drives how detailed the exit plan needs to be. Try rating a provider:

Planned exit versus stressed exit

AspectPlanned (non-stressed) exitStressed exit
TriggerStrategy change, poor value, end of contractProvider insolvency, severe breach, sanctions, prolonged outage
Provider cooperationUsually available, under the contractMay be absent or unreliable
TimeframeMonths to years; parallel running possibleDays to weeks; critical operations must stay within tolerance
Key questionWhat is the best alternative, and how do we migrate safely?How do we keep critical operations running while we find an alternative?
What makes it workTransition assistance clauses, data portability, a migration planContingency arrangements, data escrow or regular data copies, step-in or bridging options, pre-identified alternatives

What a credible exit plan contains

  1. Triggers and decision rights: the events that start exit planning, and who decides.
  2. Options: alternative providers or bringing the service in-house, with an assessment of each.
  3. Data: what data is held, in what format, how and how often you can get it back, and how its integrity is checked.
  4. People and knowledge: who understands the service well enough to specify and oversee a replacement.
  5. Timeline and cost: realistic estimates, including dual running, and whether funding is available in a stressed scenario.
  6. Customer and regulator communication: including APRA notification where required.
  7. Testing: at least a tabletop exercise that walks through a stressed exit, with lessons fed back into contracts and plans.

The honest answer is sometimes "we can't exit quickly"

For deeply embedded providers, such as a fund administrator, a stressed exit may take far longer than any tolerance level allows. That doesn't make the plan pointless. It shifts the focus to keeping critical operations running through the provider's own resilience, contingency arrangements and early warning of provider distress, and it should be visible to the board as a residual risk.

Worked example: an exit plan on a page (illustrative)

This is an illustrative example for learning only.

ElementExample: member administration provider
Trigger scenariosPlanned exit at contract end; stressed exit after repeated serious service failures; sudden loss if the provider fails.
AlternativesTwo other administrators assessed as able to take the service; bringing parts in-house is not realistic within two years.
Time and costPlanned exit 18 to 24 months; stressed exit at least 9 months with interim support; one-off cost estimated and provisioned.
DataContract gives the right to all member data in an agreed format within 30 days of request; a test extraction was done last year.
Keeping within toleranceDuring transition, urgent payments run on a manual process (see the business continuity plan).
GovernanceExit plan owner named; reviewed annually; key assumptions tested.

Common mistakes

  • Exit plans that assume a cooperative provider, when the hardest case is a provider that has failed or is in dispute.
  • Untested data assumptions: nobody has checked how long it really takes to extract and load the data.
  • Ignoring concentration across the industry: if many firms need the same alternative provider at once, capacity may not exist.
  • Plans written once and filed, not updated when the service or market changes.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Which of our providers could we not replace within our tolerance levels?
  2. When did we last test an assumption in an exit plan, such as extracting our data?
  3. Where do several of our critical operations depend on the same provider or platform?

Theory and research

Why switching is hard: transaction cost economics

Williamson (1979) explained how the characteristics of a transaction shape how it should be governed. When a relationship requires asset-specific investments, such as data migrated into a provider's platform, custom integrations or staff who only know one system, both sides become locked in. The customer can't easily switch, and the provider knows it. This is the economic root of the substitutability problem on this page. An exit plan is, in effect, an attempt to keep a credible outside option in a relationship that naturally erodes it.

What outsourcing research shows

A review of 191 studies of IT outsourcing by Lacity et al. (2009) found that outcomes vary widely and depend heavily on contract design and on the customer keeping enough in-house capability to manage the provider. Aron et al. (2005) set out a taxonomy of outsourcing risks, including opportunistic behaviour once the customer is dependent, and argued that dividing work between providers and redesigning processes can reduce them, a research basis for multi-provider strategies. Switching does happen. Whitten and Leidner (2006) surveyed 160 IT managers and compared organisations that switched provider, brought services back in-house or stayed, and the factors behind each choice, including the costs of switching. The lesson for CPS 230 exit planning is that exit is possible, but it is a project with its own costs and risks, not a clause in a contract.

Critical perspectives

  • Exit may be the wrong control for the biggest providers. For a dominant cloud provider or core administrator, a stressed exit within any short timeframe may not be realistic. Resilience within the relationship (multi-region design, data escrow, step-in rights) may matter more than exit.
  • Concentration is systemic. When many funds use the same administrator or cloud provider, the risk is not only to each fund but to the industry. Individual exit plans don't address a failure that affects everyone at once, which is why the Financial Stability Board has focused on concentration across the financial system.
  • Evidence is mostly from IT outsourcing. The research base concerns IT services in the US and Europe. Outsourcing whole business processes, such as super administration, involves member data, regulatory obligations and specialised skills, and has been studied less.
  • Plans age quickly. An exit plan written at contract signing may be irrelevant five years later after integrations and data volumes have grown. Asset specificity usually increases over the life of a relationship.

Seminar questions

  1. Use Williamson's idea of asset specificity to explain why exiting a super fund administrator is harder after ten years than after one.
  2. Score a provider you know with the exit difficulty scorer. What would most improve substitutability, and at what cost?
  3. Aron and colleagues suggest splitting work between providers. What are the risks of doing this for a critical operation?
  4. If many funds rely on the same provider, whose job is it to manage that concentration: each trustee, the industry or APRA? Justify your answer.

Check your understanding

1. Why map fourth parties?

Several providers can share the same cloud region or software vendor, concentrating risk.

2. What most distinguishes a stressed exit from a planned exit?

Stressed exits happen when the provider fails or can't be relied on, so contingency matters more than migration planning.

3. A provider is deeply customised, has no alternatives and holds data in a proprietary format. What follows?

Low substitutability calls for more detailed planning and, at renewal, better portability and transition terms.
