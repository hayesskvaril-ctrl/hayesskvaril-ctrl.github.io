Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls

How to find causes the organisation can actually fix, comparing 5 whys, fishbone, barrier analysis, bow-tie and fault trees, with a worked example and a fault tree calculator.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • "Human error" is rarely a root cause. Ask why the error was possible and why controls didn't catch it.
  • Most incidents need several failures to line up. Reason's Swiss cheese model points to latent conditions built into systems.
  • Use techniques together: a timeline first, then 5 whys, fishbone, barrier analysis, bow-tie or fault trees as the incident demands.
  • In a fault tree, probabilities multiply through AND gates, so a reliable detective control can matter more than preventing the cause.
  • Strong actions change systems (remove the hazard, automate, add independent checks). Reminders and retraining are weak on their own.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the difference between the person approach and the system approach to error, and why root cause analysis aims for the second.
  2. Apply a timeline, 5 whys, barrier analysis and a fault tree to an incident, and explain what each technique assumes.
  3. Judge the strength of corrective actions using a hierarchy of effectiveness.
  4. Critically assess the research on whether root cause analysis improves outcomes, and how hindsight and outcome bias distort investigations.

The idea in plain English

Every incident review ends with a statement of "the root cause". Too often it says "human error" or "the process wasn't followed", the person involved is retrained, and the same thing happens again six months later somewhere else. Good root cause analysis keeps asking why until it reaches causes the organisation can actually fix: gaps in design, controls, systems, incentives or oversight.

This page compares the main techniques, works through one incident with several of them, and shows how a fault tree can put numbers on which control matters most. It builds on Incident and breach management.

The Swiss cheese model

Psychologist James Reason described organisational defences as slices of Swiss cheese (Reason, 2000). Each layer (design, controls, supervision, people) has holes, some from active failures by people at the front line and some from latent conditions built into systems and decisions made long before. An incident happens when holes in several layers line up. The practical lesson: look for the latent conditions, because they will line up again.

Worked example: the wrong fee

A super fund discovers that, after a product change, an administration fee was charged at the wrong rate for eight months. Here is the same incident seen through three techniques.

5 whys

  1. Why were members overcharged? The fee rate in the administration system was set incorrectly.
  2. Why was it set incorrectly? The configuration was keyed from a draft product specification, not the final approved one.
  3. Why was the draft used? There was no single controlled source of approved fee rates; documents were emailed between teams.
  4. Why wasn't the error caught? The monthly fee reconciliation compared fees charged with the system's own rate table, so it confirmed the wrong rate.
  5. Why did the reconciliation work that way? It was designed to test calculation accuracy, not whether the rate matched the PDS. No one had asked what risk it was meant to address.

Root causes: no controlled source of approved fee rates, and a detective control designed against the wrong reference point. "The analyst keyed the wrong number" is true, but it is not a root cause.

Bow-tie

Fault tree, with numbers

A fault tree works down from the event. Here, members are charged the wrong fee if either path happens (an OR gate), and each path needs both a cause and a failed control (an AND gate). Probabilities multiply through AND gates, which is why a reliable detective control can matter more than preventing the cause itself. What a good root cause statement looks like

  • Causal: it explains how the event happened, backed by evidence from the timeline.
  • Systemic: it names a condition in the system (design, control, resourcing, incentive, oversight), not just an individual's act.
  • Fixable: the organisation can change it.
  • Tested for reach: it asks where else the same cause could produce the same failure (other products, providers or processes).
  • Linked to action: each root cause has an action, owner and date, and the fix is validated after it's done.

Regulators increasingly look at root causes, not just individual incidents. Recurring themes across incidents, breaches and complaints are a strong signal of weak controls or culture, and they belong in board reporting.

Theory and research

From the person approach to the system approach

Reason (2000) distinguishes two ways of responding to error. The person approach treats errors as the product of forgetfulness, inattention or carelessness, and responds with naming, retraining and discipline. The system approach accepts that people are fallible and asks why the system allowed an error to cause harm. The Swiss cheese model comes from the second view. Defences have holes created by active failures (unsafe acts at the front line, whose effects are immediate) and latent conditions (decisions by designers, managers and procedure writers that lie dormant until they combine with active failures). In theory, root cause analysis is a tool of the system approach. In practice it often slides back into the person approach.

Chains or systems?

Most root cause techniques assume an incident is a chain of events that leads back to a first cause. Leveson (2004) argued that this model breaks down in complex, software-intensive organisations, where accidents come from unsafe interactions between parts that are each working as designed. Her systems-theoretic model, STAMP, treats safety as a control problem. The question is not "which component failed?" but "why did the controls, including management oversight and feedback, fail to keep the system within safe limits?" For a financial services incident, that means asking how change management, reporting lines, incentives and the risk framework allowed the error, not only which step broke. Card (2017) makes a similar criticism of the "5 whys": it forces a single, linear path through what is usually a web of contributing factors, and there is little evidence that it works for serious incidents.

What the evidence says

Most empirical research on root cause analysis comes from healthcare, where formal analysis of serious incidents has been standard practice for many years. The findings are sobering. A review of 302 root cause analyses at a US hospital system found that many of the proposed solutions were weak: mostly training, process changes and enforcing existing policy (Kellogg et al., 2017). An Australian study of 227 analyses of sentinel events in Victoria's public health system classified the 1,137 recommendations as 8% strong, 44% medium and 48% weak, and about 15% of the analyses made only weak recommendations (Hibbert et al., 2018). Peerally et al. (2017) argue that the technique was borrowed from other industries without enough attention to what makes it work there, and that its name wrongly suggests a single cause.

These studies measure the strength of recommended actions, as a proxy for whether they will work. The hierarchy behind them is the one used on this page: strong actions remove or engineer out the hazard, while weak ones rely on people remembering to behave differently.

Biases that distort investigations

Investigators know how the story ended, and that knowledge changes their judgement. Fischhoff (1975) showed that people who are told an outcome judge it to have been more likely, and more foreseeable, than people who are not: hindsight bias. Baron and Hershey (1988) found an outcome bias: the same decision is rated as better reasoned, and its maker as more competent, when it happens to turn out well. Together these make investigations prone to concluding that people "should have seen it coming". Good practice reconstructs what people knew and faced at the time, and asks why their actions made sense to them then. Research on just culture adds that how an organisation treats the people involved, including fair process and repairing relationships, affects whether anyone reports the next near miss (Dekker & Breakey, 2016; Dekker, 2016).

Critical perspectives

  • The evidence is mostly from healthcare. The problems found there (weak actions, poor follow-up and single-cause thinking) are the ones this page warns about in financial services, but we found little published research on root cause analysis in banks, insurers or super funds. Treat the healthcare figures as a warning, not a measurement.
  • Strength is only a proxy. Classifying an action as strong doesn't show it worked. Serious incidents are rare, so recurrence is hard to measure, and a strong action that is badly implemented can still fail.
  • Is "root cause" the wrong goal? Systems thinkers argue there is rarely a single root, only interacting conditions. Others reply that organisations need clear, fixable findings with owners, and that the label matters less than the habit of looking past the individual.
  • Accountability or learning? Regulators increasingly expect individual accountability (see the Financial Accountability Regime and consequence management), while safety research warns that blame drives errors underground. How to hold people to account without discouraging reporting remains an open question.
  • False precision. Fault trees look exact, but their probabilities are usually judgements. Assuming independence can badly understate risk when one cause, such as a system change, both creates an error and disables the control that should catch it.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Reason's distinction between active failures and latent conditions, analyse a publicly reported incident at an Australian financial institution, for example from a regulator's enforcement action or an inquiry report. Which latent conditions were identified, and which do you think were missed?
  2. Hibbert and colleagues found that only 8% of recommendations were strong. Why might organisations prefer weak actions even when stronger ones exist? Consider cost, ownership, time pressure and the incentives of the people doing the investigation.
  3. Leveson argues that accidents are control problems rather than chains of failures. Redraw the "wrong fee" example on this page as a control structure. What causes does this reveal that the 5 whys did not?
  4. Design two practical steps to guard against hindsight and outcome bias in an incident review. How would you know whether they worked?
  5. Can a just culture coexist with the Financial Accountability Regime's emphasis on individual accountability? Argue both sides.

Further reading

  • Reason, J. (2000). Human error: Models and management. BMJ, 320(7237), 768–770. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.320.7237.768 Peer-reviewed
    Contrasts the 'person approach' to error (blame, retraining) with the 'system approach', and introduces the Swiss cheese model of layered defences with active failures and latent conditions.
  • Leveson, N. (2004). A new accident model for engineering safer systems. Safety Science, 42(4), 237–270. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-7535(03)00047-X Peer-reviewed
    Proposes STAMP, an accident model based on systems theory: accidents arise from inadequate control of interactions in a system, not just chains of failed components, so analysis must include management and organisational control.
  • Peerally, M. F., Carr, S., Waring, J., & Dixon-Woods, M. (2017). The problem with root cause analysis. BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(5), 417–422. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005511 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out problems with root cause analysis as applied in healthcare, including a misleading search for a single cause and weak, poorly followed-up fixes. Argues it was borrowed from other industries without enough attention to what makes it work there.
  • Dekker, S. W. A., & Breakey, H. (2016). 'Just culture:' Improving safety by achieving substantive, procedural and restorative justice. Safety Science, 85, 187–193. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925753516000321 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that a just culture needs more than rules about when to punish: organisations should also attend to fair process and to restoring relationships after an incident, so people keep reporting.
