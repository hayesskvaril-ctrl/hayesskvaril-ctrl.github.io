The Optus and Medibank data breaches (2022)

Two of Australia's largest data breaches, weeks apart, that changed how boards, regulators and the public think about cyber and privacy risk.

Beginner About 9 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026 Checked against sources
What
Cyber attacks and theft of customer personal information
When
September and October 2022, with regulatory action continuing into 2026
Who
Optus, Medibank, the OAIC, APRA and other regulators
Outcome
Higher privacy penalties, OAIC civil penalty proceedings, and a $250 million APRA capital adjustment for Medibank

Key takeaways

  • In late 2022, attacks on Optus and Medibank exposed the personal information of millions of Australians, including identity document numbers and health information.
  • The Information Commissioner started civil penalty proceedings against both companies, alleging they failed to take reasonable steps to protect personal information.
  • APRA increased Medibank's capital requirement by $250 million, and Parliament raised the maximum privacy penalties.
  • Key lessons: basic security controls, holding less data, and preparing the response before an attack.
  1. 22 September 2022Optus announces a cyber attack. The personal information of about 9.5 million current and former customers is affected, including identity document numbers for many.
  2. October 2022Medibank discloses a cyber incident. Criminals stole personal and health claims information of about 9.7 million current and former customers, and later published some of it online after Medibank refused to pay a ransom.
  3. Late 2022Parliament increases the maximum penalties for serious or repeated privacy breaches.
  4. 2023APRA increases Medibank's capital requirement by $250 million, from 1 July 2023, until an agreed remediation program is completed.
  5. 2024The Australian Information Commissioner starts civil penalty proceedings against Medibank.
  6. August 2025The Australian Information Commissioner starts civil penalty proceedings against Optus.

What went wrong

The detailed facts are set out in the regulators' allegations, which the companies may contest, and in the companies' own reviews. At a high level:

FeatureOptus (2022)Medibank (2022)
Data affectedNames, dates of birth, contact details and, for many, passport, driver licence and Medicare numbers.Personal details and health claims information, including sensitive diagnosis and treatment information.
Regulators' allegationsThe Information Commissioner alleges Optus failed to take reasonable steps to protect personal information over several years, affecting about 9.5 million Australians.The Information Commissioner alleges Medibank seriously interfered with the privacy of 9.7 million Australians by failing to take reasonable steps to protect their information. Weaknesses in basic controls, including multi-factor authentication for remote access, have been reported.
Data heldIdentity documents of former customers were retained, which added to the harm.Large volumes of sensitive health data were accessible once the attacker gained access.

The regulatory response

  • Higher privacy penalties: since late 2022, the maximum penalty for a company for serious interference with privacy is the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover.
  • OAIC enforcement: civil penalty proceedings against Medibank (2024) and Optus (2025). As at September 2026 we understand these were continuing; check the OAIC website for outcomes.
  • Prudential action: APRA applied a $250 million capital adjustment to Medibank, a private health insurer it regulates, reflecting weaknesses in its information security environment.
  • Wider reforms: the breaches contributed to momentum for the Government's cyber security strategy, the Cyber Security Act 2024 (including ransomware payment reporting) and privacy law reform.

Lessons for risk and compliance

  • Basic controls matter most. Multi-factor authentication, access management and patching prevent a large share of attacks. See the Essential Eight.
  • Don't keep data you don't need. Retaining old identity documents multiplied the harm. Data minimisation and destruction are risk controls, and legal obligations under the Australian Privacy Principles.
  • Plan the response before it happens: customer communications, identity document replacement, regulator notifications and the ransom decision. See Cyber risk management.
  • Several regimes apply at once: the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, CPS 234 for APRA-regulated entities, sector regulators and continuous disclosure. See Breach and incident reporting obligations.
  • Boards are accountable for overseeing cyber risk as a core business risk.

What would you have done?

Your fund still holds scanned identity documents of members who left ten years ago. What should happen?

Assess whether there is still a legal need to keep them. If not, destroy or de-identify them securely under a documented retention schedule. Under the Australian Privacy Principles, personal information no longer needed must be destroyed or de-identified, subject to record-keeping laws.

Criminals claim to have stolen your members' data and demand a ransom. What decisions must be made in the first 24 hours?

Activate the incident response plan; contain the attack; verify the claim; notify APRA (if applicable) and consider the Notifiable Data Breaches assessment; engage the Australian Cyber Security Centre and police; prepare member communications; and make a documented board-level decision on the ransom, bearing in mind the legal and ethical issues and the ransomware payment reporting obligation.

Check your understanding

1. Roughly how many people were affected by the Medibank breach?

About 9.7 million current and former customers were affected.

2. Which lesson relates to keeping old identity documents?

Holding unnecessary data increases the harm when a breach happens.

3. What action did APRA take against Medibank?

APRA increased Medibank's capital requirement by $250 million until remediation was complete.
