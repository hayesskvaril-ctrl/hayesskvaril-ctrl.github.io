The Hayne Royal Commission (2019)

The inquiry that exposed widespread misconduct in Australian financial services and reshaped regulation for the following decade.

Beginner About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026
What
Royal Commission into Misconduct in the Banking, Superannuation and Financial Services Industry
When
December 2017 to February 2019
Who
Banks, financial advisers, super trustees, insurers and the regulators ASIC and APRA
Outcome
76 recommendations; major reforms legislated from 2019 to 2021

Key takeaways

  • The Royal Commission exposed misconduct across banking, advice, super and insurance, including charging fees for no service and selling unsuitable products.
  • It linked much of the misconduct to incentives and to putting profit ahead of basic honesty and fairness.
  • Its 76 recommendations led to major reforms, including the Financial Accountability Regime, stronger breach reporting, the hawking ban and enforceable complaints standards.
  • It changed expectations of boards, culture and regulators, who became more willing to go to court.
  1. 14 December 2017The Royal Commission into Misconduct in the Banking, Superannuation and Financial Services Industry is established, with Commissioner Kenneth Hayne.
  2. 2018Public hearings across consumer lending, financial advice, small business lending, farming finance, super, insurance and regulators. Senior executives are questioned in public.
  3. 28 September 2018Interim report published.
  4. 4 February 2019Final report released with 76 recommendations, together with the Government's response, Restoring trust in Australia's financial system.
  5. 2019 to 2021Most recommendations requiring legislation are implemented through a series of reform acts.

What went wrong

The Commission found misconduct across the industry. In its final report, Commissioner Hayne said much of it could be traced to entities putting the pursuit of profit ahead of basic standards of honesty, and to remuneration and incentives that rewarded selling rather than serving customers. Key themes:

ThemeWhat happened
Fees for no serviceCustomers were charged ongoing advice fees for services they never received, in some cases including customers who had died.
Conflicted advice and incentivesCommissions, sales targets and vertical integration encouraged advice and sales that favoured the firm over the client.
Unsuitable products and salesAdd-on insurance and other products sold to people who didn't need them or couldn't claim on them, including through unsolicited selling.
Super trusteesTrustees that didn't put members' interests first, including over fees and insurance.
Insurance claimsPoor claims handling that treated claimants unfairly.
RegulatorsThe Commission criticised ASIC for too often preferring negotiation to enforcement, and asked the question that became a slogan: "Why not litigate?"

The regulatory response

The Government committed to act on all 76 recommendations. Most of the legislative changes were made between 2019 and 2021. Some of the most significant reforms that followed:

ReformLearn more
Financial Accountability Regime, extending accountability for executives to banks, insurers and super trusteesFinancial Accountability Regime
Stronger breach reporting (the reportable situations regime, from October 2021)RG 78
Hawking ban and the deferred sales model for add-on insuranceConsumer protection
Claims handling becomes a licensed financial serviceInsurance
End of grandfathered commissions and annual renewal of ongoing fee arrangementsFinancial advice regulation
Enforceable complaints handling standardsRG 271
Stronger remuneration standardCPS 511
A compensation scheme of last resortFinancial advice regulation

Lessons for risk and compliance

  • Culture and incentives drive conduct. Controls can't compensate for incentives that reward the wrong behaviour. See Culture and conduct.
  • "Can we?" is not enough. The Commission emphasised asking "should we?": doing what is fair, not just what is technically allowed.
  • Remediation must be prompt and complete. Slow, reluctant remediation compounded the harm. See RG 277.
  • Boards need to challenge. Directors must seek out the information they need, particularly about non-financial risk.
  • Regulators will enforce. The post-Commission period saw more litigation and larger penalties.

What would you have done?

You are the compliance manager at an advice licensee. A data review shows thousands of clients paying ongoing fees with no record of a review meeting in the past year. What do you do first?

Treat it as a potential significant breach and start the breach assessment immediately (the reporting clock may already be running). Stop the fees for affected clients while you investigate, preserve records, escalate to senior management and the board, and plan remediation that assumes in clients' favour where records are missing.

You sit on a trustee board. Management proposes renewing a group insurance contract with a related insurer without a market test. What questions do you ask?

Is this in members' best financial interests? How was the conflict identified and managed? Would an independent trustee make the same decision? What evidence shows the terms are competitive, and what do claims outcomes and member complaints show?

Check your understanding

2. "Fees for no service" refers to:

3. Which reform extended executive accountability to insurers and super trustees?
