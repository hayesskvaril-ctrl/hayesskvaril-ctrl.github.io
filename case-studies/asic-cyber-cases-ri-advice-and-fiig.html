ASIC v RI Advice and ASIC v FIIG: cyber security as a licensee obligation

How two Federal Court cases turned cyber security into an enforceable part of every AFS licensee's general obligations, and what that means for risk management across a network.

Advanced About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026 Checked against sources
What
Court cases about inadequate cyber security risk management by AFS licensees
When
Incidents 2014 to 2023; judgments in May 2022 (RI Advice) and February 2026 (FIIG)
Who
RI Advice Group, FIIG Securities, ASIC, the Federal Court
Outcome
Contraventions of s 912A in both cases; expert-led remediation; a $2.5 million penalty for FIIG

Key takeaways

  • ASIC used the general licensee obligations in s 912A, not a cyber-specific law, to hold licensees to account for weak cyber security.
  • In RI Advice (2022), the licensee was responsible for cyber risk across its authorised representatives' practices, and a pattern of incidents showed inadequate systems.
  • In FIIG (2026), the court imposed a $2.5 million penalty, the first civil penalty for cyber failures under these obligations.
  • What counts as "adequate" depends on the risks the business faces, such as the sensitivity of client data.
  • Lessons reach beyond cyber: principles-based obligations are given concrete content by courts.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain how ASIC used the general licensee obligations in s 912A to enforce cyber security standards.
  2. Analyse what the courts looked for in RI Advice and what FIIG added.
  3. Use the economics of information security to explain why firms underinvest in cyber security.
  4. Critically assess the evidence on the costs of cyber incidents and the role of regulation.
  1. 2014 to 2020Nine cyber incidents occur at practices of RI Advice's authorised representatives, including ransomware, compromised email accounts and a server accessed remotely by an unknown party.
  2. Late 2017 to early 2018At one practice, an attacker spends more than 155 hours logged in to a server over several months before it is detected. Up to 8,104 people's information is potentially exposed.
  3. August 2020ASIC starts proceedings, its first case about cyber security under the general licensee obligations.
  4. 5 May 2022The Federal Court (Rofe J) finds RI Advice breached s 912A by failing to have adequate cyber security risk management across its network of authorised representatives, and orders an expert-led review and remediation.
  5. May 2023A cyber attack on FIIG Securities compromises about 385GB of data, affecting about 18,000 clients.
  6. February 2026The Federal Court orders FIIG to pay $2.5 million in penalties, the first civil penalty for cyber security failures under the general licensee obligations, and requires an expert-led compliance uplift.

The legal question

Neither company was charged under a cyber-specific law. ASIC relied on the general obligations every AFS licensee has under s 912A of the Corporations Act, including to provide financial services efficiently, honestly and fairly, to have adequate resources, and to have adequate risk management systems. The question was whether weak cyber security could breach those broad, principles-based duties. In both cases, the answer was yes.

That matters well beyond cyber. It shows how open-textured obligations such as "adequate risk management systems" are given concrete content by courts, based on the risks the business actually faces. See interpreting legislation.

What the court looked at in RI Advice

IssueWhat it showed
Risk across the networkThe licensee was responsible for managing cyber risk at its authorised representatives' practices, not only in its own head office.
A pattern of incidentsSeveral incidents over time, viewed together, pointed to inadequate systems, even though each practice was small.
Adequacy depends on riskPractices held sensitive client information and dealt with clients' money, so the standard of cyber risk management needed was high. The court did not set a universal technical baseline.
RemediationOrders required an independent expert to identify further measures, and RI Advice to implement them.

The RI Advice orders were made largely on terms agreed between the parties, so the judgment is less detailed than a fully contested decision. Read it for the precise findings.

What FIIG added

FIIG admitted the contraventions. Unlike RI Advice, it resulted in a pecuniary penalty: $2.5 million, plus $500,000 in costs and an expert-led uplift program. The failures ran over about four years and concerned cyber security controls and resourcing that were not adequate for the size and nature of FIIG's business. ASIC's media release and the judgment set out the specific failings. The case moved cyber risk from "declarations and remediation" into civil penalty territory for AFS licensees.

Lessons for risk and compliance

  • General obligations reach cyber. Licensees don't need a cyber-specific rule to be exposed. APRA-regulated entities also have CPS 234.
  • Networks count. Licensees with authorised representatives, franchises or distributors must manage risk across the network, with monitoring and assurance, not just policies.
  • Patterns are evidence. A series of "small" incidents is a signal about systems. Incident trend analysis belongs in board reporting.
  • Baseline controls are expected. Measures like multi-factor authentication, patching, backups and monitoring are widely recognised baselines, for example in the Essential Eight.
  • Resourcing is an obligation. Under-investment in security can itself breach the requirement to have adequate technological and human resources.

What would you have done?

You are Head of Compliance at a licensee with 200 authorised representative practices. How would you get assurance over their cyber security?

A risk-based approach: minimum standards in the authorised representative agreement (for example multi-factor authentication, patching, backups and incident notification), an attestation program backed by sample-based technical verification, tiering practices by data held and incident history, support such as approved tools and training, and consequences for non-compliance. Report the results and incident trends to the board.

A practice reports its third email compromise in two years. What do you do beyond fixing the immediate issue?

Treat it as a pattern: run a root cause analysis across all three incidents, check whether similar weaknesses exist at other practices, assess breach reporting (including the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme and a possible reportable situation to ASIC), and escalate the trend. The RI Advice case shows that repeated incidents can be evidence of inadequate systems.

Theory and research

Why firms underinvest in security

Anderson and Moore (2006) argued that many security failures are failures of incentives rather than technology: the party that could prevent harm is often not the one that bears it. In the RI Advice case, the harm from compromised adviser systems fell largely on clients whose information was exposed. When costs fall on others, firms tend to invest less than is socially optimal, which is one justification for regulatory standards and enforcement.

How much should a firm invest? The model by Gordon and Loeb (2002) shows that, under its assumptions, the optimal investment to protect information never exceeds about 37% of the expected loss from a breach, and that the most vulnerable information is not always the best place to invest. The model is a simplification, but it frames security as an economic decision under uncertainty.

What incidents actually cost

Evidence on costs is contested. Romanosky (2016) analysed a large dataset of cyber incidents and found typical costs smaller than often claimed, which raises questions about firms' incentives to invest. But Kamiya et al. (2021) found that successful attacks exposing personal financial information caused shareholder losses far larger than the direct costs, and that affected firms tended to strengthen risk management afterwards. Both findings support the logic of the ASIC cases: private incentives alone may not produce adequate security, and the consequences of failure include regulatory action and reputational damage.

Critical perspectives

  • Principles-based obligations create uncertainty. Section 912A doesn't specify security controls, so what is "adequate" is being defined through cases like these. This is an example of the principles-based regulation debate on the interpreting legislation page.
  • Cost data is unreliable. Many incidents are never disclosed and many costs are hard to measure, so estimates in both directions should be treated with caution.
  • Models assume known probabilities. The Gordon-Loeb model assumes breach probabilities can be estimated. Cyber threats change quickly, and attackers adapt to defences.
  • Evidence is mostly US. The cost studies use US data. Australian notifiable data breach statistics describe incidents but not their full costs.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Anderson and Moore's argument about incentives, explain why ASIC chose to enforce cyber security through licensee obligations.
  2. Romanosky and Kamiya and colleagues reach different conclusions about the costs of cyber incidents. How can both be right?
  3. Apply the Gordon-Loeb logic to a small financial advice licensee. What would a reasonable security budget look like, and what are the model's limits?
  4. What would you expect a licensee to be able to show ASIC to demonstrate adequate cyber risk management after RI Advice and FIIG?
