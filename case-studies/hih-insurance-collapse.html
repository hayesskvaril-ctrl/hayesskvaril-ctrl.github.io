The HIH Insurance collapse (2001)

Australia's largest corporate collapse at the time, and how it reshaped insurance regulation, corporate governance and APRA itself.

Beginner About 13 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources
What
Collapse of the HIH Insurance group
When
Provisional liquidation on 15 March 2001; Royal Commission report April 2003
Who
HIH Insurance, its directors and auditors, and APRA
Outcome
Losses estimated at up to $5.3 billion; 61 recommendations; criminal convictions; major prudential and corporate law reforms

Key takeaways

  • HIH collapsed in 2001 with estimated losses of up to $5.3 billion, largely because it had not reserved enough for future claims.
  • The HIH Royal Commission found failures of governance, culture, decision-making and financial reporting, and criticised APRA's supervision.
  • Its 61 recommendations led to stronger insurance prudential standards, a more sceptical APRA and corporate law reforms including auditor independence.
  • The lessons about challenge, culture and independent scrutiny of judgement-based numbers remain relevant today.
  1. 1998 to 1999HIH acquires FAI Insurance, an acquisition later found to have been made without adequate due diligence and to have weakened the group.
  2. 15 March 2001The major HIH companies are placed in provisional liquidation. Liquidators later estimate the losses at up to $5.3 billion, making it the largest corporate collapse in Australian history at the time.
  3. 2001The Government establishes the HIH Royal Commission, led by Justice Neville Owen, and a scheme to support affected policyholders.
  4. 4 April 2003The Royal Commission report is delivered, with 61 policy recommendations on corporate governance, financial reporting, insurance regulation and APRA.
  5. 2002 to 2004General insurance prudential standards are strengthened, APRA's governance is restructured, and corporate law reforms (CLERP 9) strengthen auditor independence and financial reporting.
  6. April 2005HIH founder Ray Williams is sentenced to imprisonment after pleading guilty to charges including misleading shareholders about HIH's financial position.

What went wrong

Justice Owen found that HIH failed because of mismanagement and poor governance, not a single event. Key themes:

ThemeWhat happened (summarised)
Under-reservingHIH did not set aside enough to pay future claims, so its reported financial position was far better than reality.
Culture and leadershipA culture of deference to senior leadership, with too little questioning and scepticism from the board and management.
Board oversightThe board lacked the information, independence and challenge needed to oversee management and risk.
Poor decisionsAcquisitions such as FAI, and expansion into markets the group did not understand well, without proper analysis.
Financial reporting and auditAccounting treatments, including some reinsurance arrangements, that made the position look healthier; questions about auditor independence.
RegulationThe Commission found APRA's failings did not cause the collapse, but that APRA fell short of what the community was entitled to expect of an insurance regulator.

The regulatory response

  • Stronger prudential standards for general insurers, including higher capital requirements and independent actuarial valuation of claims liabilities.
  • A more questioning APRA: APRA's governance was restructured, and it adopted a more proactive, sceptical supervisory approach. The episode shaped modern Australian prudential supervision.
  • Corporate law reform: the CLERP 9 reforms strengthened auditor independence, financial reporting and disclosure.
  • Policyholder protection: a government-funded support scheme helped affected policyholders; a permanent Financial Claims Scheme for insurers and deposits was later introduced.

Lessons for risk and compliance

  • Question the numbers. Reserving, valuations and other judgement-based figures need independent challenge. See Model risk management.
  • Culture beats structure. A board can look fine on paper and still fail if no one challenges a dominant leader. See Assessing and changing risk culture.
  • Growth by acquisition needs real due diligence and a clear view of the risks being taken on.
  • Independence of the second and third lines and the external auditor is essential. See Internal audit.
  • Regulators learn from failure, and today's prudential standards reflect lessons from HIH.

What changed because of HIH

The collapse led to major changes in insurance regulation and corporate governance:

ReformWhat it did
General insurance prudential reforms (from July 2002)Stronger capital, risk management, fit and proper and actuarial requirements for general insurers, supervised by APRA.
CLERP 9 (2004)Strengthened auditor independence, including rotation of audit partners, and improved financial reporting and disclosure.
Financial Claims Scheme for insurance (2008)Protects many policyholders if a general insurer fails.

Frequently asked questions

Could an HIH-style collapse happen today?

The rules are much stronger, but no regulation removes risk entirely. Weak governance, under-reserving and a dominant chief executive are still warning signs regulators watch for.

What would you have done?

You are the appointed actuary and believe claims reserves are too low, but management wants to keep them unchanged ahead of results. What do you do?

State your professional view clearly in writing, escalate to the board or audit committee, and do not sign off on figures you believe are inadequate. Actuaries and auditors have obligations to report certain matters to APRA.

As a director, you are asked to approve a large acquisition with a short board paper and little time. What do you ask for?

Independent due diligence, a clear analysis of risks and how they would be managed, the effect on capital, alternatives considered, and enough time to consider it properly. If these are missing, defer the decision.

Check your understanding

1. What was a central financial problem at HIH?

HIH did not hold enough in reserves to pay future claims.

2. Which theme did the Royal Commission emphasise about culture?

The Commission highlighted a culture that discouraged challenge.

3. Which reform strengthened auditor independence?

The CLERP 9 corporate law reforms strengthened auditor independence and financial reporting.
