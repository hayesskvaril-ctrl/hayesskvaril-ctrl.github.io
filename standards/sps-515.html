SPS 515 Strategic Planning and Member Outcomes

APRA's standard requiring super trustees to plan around member outcomes, justify every dollar they spend, check every year whether members are getting good results, and be ready to transfer members to another fund if they aren't.

Intermediate About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Expert reviewed October 2026
Issuer
Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)
Status
Binding prudential standard. The current, strengthened version was released on 4 July 2024 and has applied since 1 July 2025. The first version applied from 1 January 2020.
Applies to
All RSE licensees (trustees of APRA-regulated super funds). It does not apply to self-managed super funds, which the ATO regulates.
Where it sits
APRA describes it as the core standard in the business operations pillar of the super prudential framework.
Guidance
Prudential practice guide SPG 515 Strategic Planning and Member Outcomes (2024), which explains APRA's expectations and better practice. Guidance is not binding, but APRA uses it when supervising.
Main related law
Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 (SIS Act): the best financial interests duty, the outcomes assessment and the retirement income covenant (s 52), the sole purpose test (s 62) and the performance test (Part 6A).

Key takeaways

  • SPS 515 makes a super trustee's plans answer one question: what outcomes are we trying to achieve for members, and are we achieving them?
  • The board must approve specific, measurable strategic objectives and a written rolling business plan of at least three years covering the whole business.
  • Since 1 July 2025, all spending is in scope, not just "significant" spending. Each decision must be backed by evidence that it is in members' best financial interests, with measures to check it delivered.
  • Trustees must track progress with key performance indicators and set triggers for action. At a minimum, failing or expecting to fail the annual performance test must be a trigger.
  • Every year the trustee reviews its business performance against internal and external benchmarks, for different groups of members, and makes the outcomes assessment the SIS Act requires.
  • Trustees must be prepared, in good time, to transfer members out of (or into) their fund when outcomes can't be fixed.
  • APRA has stepped up its scrutiny of super fund spending since late 2024, and imposed licence conditions on a trustee over related-party spending in November 2025.

SPS 515 in plain English

A super fund's trustee manages other people's retirement savings, and the law requires it to act in their best financial interests. SPS 515 turns that duty into a management system. The trustee has to decide what good outcomes for its members look like, write a plan to deliver them, spend money only where it helps achieve them, measure whether it is working, and act when it isn't. If a fund can't deliver good outcomes, the honest answer may be to merge with a fund that can, so SPS 515 also requires trustees to plan for that possibility before it becomes urgent.

Think of it as a loop: objectives → plan → spending → monitoring → annual review → better objectives, with an exit route if the loop shows the fund can't deliver.

Why APRA strengthened it

The first SPS 515 (2020) introduced strategic planning and an annual business performance review. Since then, the law has added the best financial interests duty, the annual performance test and a more detailed outcomes assessment (the 2021 "Your Future, Your Super" reforms), and the retirement income covenant (2022). APRA consulted from 2022 on lifting the standard to match. Its stated aim was to put members' interests "front and centre" in trustees' strategic and business planning, financial resource management, retirement income work and fund transfers. The final version also moved expectations that had been guidance into binding requirements, particularly on transfer planning.

The requirements, one by one

This section explains each part of the standard in plain English. It summarises the standard rather than quoting it; read the standard itself for the exact wording and paragraph numbers.

1. Strategic objectives

The board must approve strategic objectives that support the outcomes the trustee seeks for its members and the sound and prudent management of the business. The objectives must be specific and measurable, and the standard lists factors the trustee must take into account in setting them. When it sets or reviews the objectives, the board must consider the outcomes it wants for members and the results of the latest business performance review.

In practice: "deliver strong outcomes for members" is not measurable. "Keep our MySuper product's net return in the top half of comparable products over rolling 7-year periods, while reducing total fees for a $50,000 balance to below a stated level by 2028" is specific and measurable.

2. The business plan

The trustee must have a written, rolling business plan of at least three years that covers the whole of its business operations and sets out how it will achieve its strategic objectives. The board approves it, and it is reviewed at least once a year. APRA's guidance says the detail for each key initiative should be proportionate to its size, complexity, length, budget and importance.

A good plan shows, for each objective: the initiatives that deliver it, who is accountable, the budget and resources, the key risks (linked to the risk management framework), the measures of success and the timeline.

3. Financial resources and expenditure

The trustee needs a robust approach to managing the financial resources available to it, so they support member outcomes and the sound and prudent management of the business. The biggest change in 2025 was removing the word "significant": all expenditure must support the strategic objectives and be in members' best financial interests, and every expenditure decision must be supported by sound analysis and meaningful evidence. Spending decisions must also be consistent with the trustee's other legal duties, including the sole purpose test.

APRA's guidance and its 2025 letter describe what that looks like:

  • A business case for each spending decision, proportionate to its size: the purpose, clear objectives, and specific, measurable expected outcomes for members, linked to a strategic objective. Better business cases also assess what happens if the money is not spent.
  • An expenditure management framework with clearly defined spending types and categories (with practical examples), set financial thresholds and documented approval requirements.
  • Monitoring after the decision: the metrics that show whether the expected outcomes were delivered, the circumstances that would trigger a review, and timely action if they aren't.
  • Reporting supported by evidence that links spending to its impact on members.
  • Attestation: APRA's view is that better practice is for accountable senior executives to attest to how expenditure is managed.

4. Monitoring: key performance indicators and triggers

The trustee must monitor progress against its strategic objectives and business plan using key performance indicators (KPIs) and triggers. Triggers are pre-set points that tell the trustee it must start acting, either to improve the outcomes expected for members or to start preparing for a transfer of members to another fund. At a minimum, the triggers must include failing, or expecting to fail, the annual performance test in the SIS Act. APRA's guidance expects transfer-planning triggers to work across a range of plausible scenarios and to activate while there is still time and resource to carry out a successful transfer.

5. The annual business performance review

Every year the trustee must review its performance in achieving its strategic objectives (the business performance review) and use the results to improve its business operations. At a minimum, the review must assess:

  • the outcomes achieved for members, against objective internal and external benchmarks; and
  • the outcomes for different cohorts (groups) of members, so that every member is covered, including members who are retired or approaching retirement.

The review draws on the outcomes assessment, including its comparisons with other trustees' products. Splitting members into meaningful cohorts (for example by age, balance, phase, insurance cover or employer plan) shows where average results hide groups that are doing badly.

6. The outcomes assessment (a SIS Act requirement)

Separately from SPS 515, section 52 of the SIS Act requires every trustee of a fund with more than six members to make an annual outcomes assessment: a determination of whether the financial interests of the members who hold each MySuper and choice product are being promoted. SPS 515 sets out how APRA expects it to be done, and requires the trustee to act on what it finds.

What the outcomes assessment looks at (summary of SIS Act s 52(9) to (11))
PartWhat it involves
ComparisonsFees and costs, returns (after fees, costs and taxes) and the level of investment risk, compared with other products. MySuper and certain choice products must also be compared with the performance test benchmarks.
Factors to assessIncluding the options, benefits and facilities offered; the investment strategy, including its risk and return target; the insurance strategy and fees; whether members are disadvantaged by a lack of scale; operating costs; and the basis for setting fees.
MethodSPS 515 requires the trustee to document its methodology, including how it made the comparisons and applied the factors in reaching each product's determination.
PublicationThe determination, with a summary of the assessments and comparisons behind it, must be published on the fund's website within 28 days after it is made, and stay there until the next one.
ActionUnder SPS 515 the trustee must act on what the assessment finds, so the outcomes it seeks for members can be improved.

The outcomes assessment is the trustee's own judgment. The annual performance test is run by APRA, and a product that fails two years in a row cannot take on new members. See Superannuation trustee governance for how the two compare.

7. Retirement income

Since 1 July 2022 the SIS Act has required trustees to have a retirement income strategy for members who are retired or approaching retirement (s 52(8A)). The revised SPS 515 adds that the strategy must be reviewed for its appropriateness, effectiveness and adequacy at least every three years, and the business performance review must consider outcomes for retired and near-retired members.

8. Transfer planning

The trustee must take appropriate and timely steps to prepare for circumstances that may require a transfer of members out of, or into, its fund, including when a trigger has been met. APRA's guidance expects trustees to use the trigger framework to decide when to start more formal transfer planning, and to be able to show that the fund's governing rules give it the power to transfer members' benefits to a successor fund. There are specific requirements where APRA tells a trustee that its authority to offer a MySuper product may be cancelled: the trustee must document, within the time APRA specifies, a plan showing it is prepared to transfer the MySuper assets to another MySuper product. The standard also includes requirements to notify APRA about transfer plans; check the current standard for the exact triggers and timing.

Transfer planning sits beside, not instead of, CPS 190 Recovery and Exit Planning, which has applied to RSE licensees since 1 January 2025. For how a merger actually works, see Fund mergers and successor fund transfers.

9. The board's role

The board approves the strategic objectives and the business plan, considers the outcomes sought for members and the latest business performance review when it does so, and oversees spending, the business performance review, the outcomes assessment and transfer planning. Under the Financial Accountability Regime, which has applied to super trustees since 15 March 2025, individual executives are also accountable for the areas they run.

What APRA has found in practice

Intensified supervision of spending (October 2024)

On 22 October 2024 APRA wrote to all RSE licensees saying it would prioritise supervision of fund spending where the benefit to members is not obvious or may not be reasonably justified. It started with discretionary categories such as travel, entertainment and conferences, and with funds whose spending is an outlier in relative or absolute terms. APRA also began publishing fund-level expenditure data, including categories such as advertising, sponsorship and payments to industry bodies, and said it would follow up trustees with outlying discretionary spending.

"Expenditure outcomes" letter (June 2025)

On 24 June 2025 APRA published findings from information it gathered from 14 trustees of different sizes and business models, chosen because their spending was comparatively high or its member benefit was not immediately clear. It reviewed thousands of documents on discretionary, marketing and connected-entity spending.

Better practice APRA sawAreas for improvement
Business cases with data, measurable objectives and a clear link from strategic objectives to expected financial outcomes for members, including the impact if the spending didn't happen.A lack of rigour when entering into agreements.
Defined spending categories with practical examples, pre-set thresholds and documented approvals.Decisions that did not show a clear rationale or a link to measurable member benefits.
Regular, evidence-based monitoring with member-focused success measures, showing how spending led to real improvements for members.Emphasis on industry positioning or what peers were doing, rather than outcomes for members.

Licence conditions over related-party spending (November 2025)

On 27 November 2025 APRA imposed additional licence conditions on Australian Ethical Superannuation Pty Ltd, after reviewing its related-party spending, particularly investment management agreements with its parent company. APRA said the trustee had not shown it had adequate processes to scrutinise and justify how the fees it paid its parent were consistent with members' best financial interests. The conditions require an independent review of those outsourcing decisions and implementation of its recommendations. It is a reminder that related-party and connected-entity arrangements get particular attention.

Common mistakes

  • Objectives that can't be measured, so the business performance review has nothing to test against.
  • An outcomes assessment done for compliance only: the determination is published, but nothing changes in the plan, fees or products.
  • Averages that hide problems: a product looks fine overall while a cohort, such as members with low balances or older members with insurance, does badly.
  • Spending justified by peer activity or brand, without a measurable link to member outcomes.
  • No follow-up after spending is approved, so nobody checks whether the expected benefits arrived.
  • Related-party arrangements that are not tested as hard as arrangements with outside providers.
  • Triggers set too late, so by the time they fire there isn't enough time or money left for an orderly merger.
  • A business plan that isn't connected to the risk management framework, the budget or the investment strategy.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Which of our strategic objectives are measurable, and how are we tracking against each one?
  2. Which member cohorts are getting the worst outcomes, and what are we doing about it?
  3. What did last year's outcomes assessment conclude, and what changed as a result?
  4. What are our triggers, and how close are we to any of them, including the performance test?
  5. How do we know our discretionary and related-party spending is in members' best financial interests? What have we stopped spending on?
  6. If we had to merge, how long would it take, and what would need to be true for it to go well?
  7. When was our retirement income strategy last reviewed, and what did the review find?

Practical points

  • Start from member outcomes, then work back. Define the outcomes for each cohort, then set objectives, then initiatives and budget.
  • Make every dollar traceable. Each spending line should link to a strategic objective and a measure of member benefit.
  • Use the outcomes assessment to make decisions, not just to report. Poor results should lead to action: fee changes, product changes or a merger.
  • Test triggers against scenarios, such as a performance test failure or falling membership, to check they fire early enough.
  • Link to risk management. Business plan risks should connect to the risk management framework under SPS 220 and to CPS 230.

Frequently asked questions

Does SPS 515 apply to self-managed super funds?

No. It applies to RSE licensees, the trustees of APRA-regulated funds. Self-managed super funds are regulated by the ATO.

Is the business performance review the same as the outcomes assessment?

No. The outcomes assessment is a SIS Act determination for each MySuper and choice product, which must be published. The business performance review is an SPS 515 review of the whole business against its strategic objectives, and it draws on the outcomes assessment.

Does "all expenditure" mean every purchase needs a full business case?

The requirement covers all spending, but APRA's guidance is about proportionality: the depth of analysis should match the size and nature of the spending. A framework with categories and thresholds is how trustees make that work in practice.

Does failing the performance test mean the fund must merge?

Not automatically. Failing, or expecting to fail, must be a trigger for action under SPS 515. The law sets separate consequences for a failed product, such as notifying members and, after two consecutive fails, not taking on new members. Whether to fix the product or transfer members is the trustee's decision, made in members' best financial interests.

Is SPG 515 binding?

No. Prudential practice guides explain APRA's view of how to comply and what better practice looks like. Trustees can comply in other ways, but APRA uses the guide when it supervises.
