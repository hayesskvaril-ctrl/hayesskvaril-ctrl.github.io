APS 115 Operational risk capital

How APRA sets the capital banks must hold for operational risk: the business indicator, the tiered formula, a calculator and the debates behind the design.

Advanced About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • APS 115 sets operational risk capital for ADIs using a formula based on the size of the business, not the bank's own models.
  • Capital is 12% of the business indicator up to $1.5 billion, 15% of the part up to $45 billion and 18% above that. Multiply by 12.5 for risk-weighted assets.
  • The business indicator combines interest, services and financial components, averaged over three years.
  • The approach is simple and comparable, but not sensitive to the quality of risk management. Supervision and Pillar 2 capital fill that gap.
  • Quantitative models are still used for scenario analysis, stress testing and the ICAAP.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Calculate operational risk capital under APS 115 from an ADI's business indicator.
  2. Explain the policy reasons for replacing internal models with a size-based formula.
  3. Evaluate the academic critique of the standardised approach: risk insensitivity, instability and incentives.
  4. Discuss how Pillar 2 supervision and internal modelling fill the gaps a formula leaves.

The idea in plain English

Banks hold capital so they can absorb unexpected losses and keep operating. Some of those losses come from operational risk: fraud, system failures, processing errors, legal claims. APS 115 sets how much capital an authorised deposit-taking institution (ADI) must hold for operational risk.

Rather than letting each bank model its own losses, APS 115 uses a formula based on the size of the bank's business, measured through its income. Bigger businesses hold proportionately more. This page explains the formula, lets you try it, and discusses the trade-offs behind the design. For modelling approaches, see quantitative operational risk.

The formula

APS 115 starts with the business indicator (BI), a measure of the size of the ADI's business built from its financial statements, averaged over three years. The capital requirement is then calculated in tiers, like marginal tax rates:

Operational risk capital under APS 115
Part of the business indicatorCapital rate on that part
Up to $1.5 billion12%
Above $1.5 billion, up to $45 billion15% (12% plus an extra 3%)
Above $45 billion18% (12% plus a further 3%)

The capital amount is converted into operational risk-weighted assets by multiplying by 12.5, so it can be combined with credit and market risk in the capital ratios under APS 110.

What goes into the business indicator

ComponentWhat it captures (simplified)
Interest, leases and dividend component (ILDC)Net interest and lease income, limited by a cap linked to the size of interest-earning assets (2.25% of them), plus dividend income.
Services component (SC)Fee and commission income or expense, and other operating income or expense.
Financial component (FC)Net profit or loss on the trading book and the banking book.

The standard defines each item precisely, including which accounting lines are included and how the three-year averages work. This table is a simplified guide.

Try it: operational risk capital for an illustrative ADI

Illustrative. Real calculations use the standard's definitions and three-year averages, and APRA can require more capital.

Worked example

An ADI has a business indicator of $17 billion ($12 billion ILDC, $4 billion services, $1 billion financial).

  • First $1.5 billion at 12% = $0.18 billion
  • Next $15.5 billion (from $1.5 billion to $17 billion) at 15% = $2.325 billion
  • Operational risk capital = $2.505 billion, an effective rate of about 14.7% of the BI
  • Operational risk-weighted assets = $2.505 billion × 12.5 = about $31.3 billion

If the ADI's fee income grew by $1 billion, its capital would rise by $150 million, whether or not its controls improved at the same time. That is the central trade-off in this design.

Why a formula and not models?

Before 2023, some large ADIs used their own models (the advanced measurement approaches) to calculate operational risk capital. Internationally, the Basel Committee concluded that model-based results varied too much between banks with similar risks to be comparable, and replaced them with a standardised approach in its 2017 Basel III reforms. APRA implemented its version, APS 115, from 1 January 2023.

The Basel framework also allows capital to be scaled up or down using a bank's own loss history (an internal loss multiplier). APRA's formula in APS 115 is based on the business indicator alone.

Trade-offs in a size-based approach
StrengthsLimitations
Simple, transparent and comparable across ADIs.Not risk-sensitive: two ADIs of the same size hold the same capital, however different their controls.
Hard to game through modelling choices.Capital follows income. A business line with high fees attracts more capital even if its operational risk is low.
Stable and predictable for planning.Doesn't directly reward better risk management, so the incentive to invest in controls has to come from elsewhere (supervision, Pillar 2, culture).

Beyond the minimum: Pillar 2 and supervision

APS 115 sets a floor, not the whole answer. APRA can require an ADI to hold more capital where it has concerns about risk management, governance or particular exposures. The best-known example was the additional $1 billion of operational risk capital APRA required of CBA after its 2018 prudential inquiry (see the CBA case study). ADIs also assess their own capital needs, including for operational risk, in their ICAAP.

This is why quantitative methods remain useful even though they no longer set the minimum: scenario analysis, loss distribution modelling and stress testing help an ADI understand whether the formula's answer is enough for its actual risk profile. See scenario analysis and stress testing.

Where APS 115 sits in 2026

The version of APS 115 in force has applied since 1 January 2023, and APRA's 2026 capital and liquidity roadmap doesn't propose to change the operational risk formula. The changes around it still matter:

  • Removing AT1 capital from 1 January 2027 changes the mix of capital banks hold against their total risk-weighted assets, including operational risk-weighted assets.
  • Lower standardised credit risk weights (proposed start 1 April 2027) would shrink credit risk-weighted assets for some lending, making operational risk a slightly larger share of the total.
  • CPS 230 (from 1 July 2025) raised the bar for operational risk management. APS 115 sets the capital; CPS 230 sets how the risk is managed. Weak CPS 230 practices can still lead APRA to require more capital through Pillar 2.

Frequently asked questions

Does better operational risk management reduce APS 115 capital?

Not directly. The formula depends only on the business indicator. Better risk management can matter through supervision, for example if APRA removes an extra capital requirement it had imposed.

Do insurers use APS 115?

No. APS 115 applies to ADIs. Insurers have their own capital standards, including an operational risk charge in the general and life insurance capital standards.

Theory and research

Why regulators gave up on internal models

The old advanced measurement approaches let large banks calculate operational risk capital with their own loss distribution models. Research on those models explains why regulators lost confidence in them. The 99.9% annual loss they had to estimate is a 1-in-1,000-year event, and Cope et al. (2009) showed how estimates at that level depend on a few extreme losses and on modelling choices, and how hard they are to validate. Two banks with similar risks could report very different capital. Studies of external loss data also showed that the tail of operational losses is very heavy, so a single extreme loss can dominate the result (de Fontnouvelle et al., 2006).

The case against the formula

The standardised approach solves comparability but gives up risk sensitivity. Peters et al. (2016) stress-tested the Basel Committee's proposal and argued that it can be unstable, insensitive to the risk actually run, and super-additive, so that combining two businesses can increase required capital. Because capital follows the business indicator, an ADI that improves its controls holds the same capital as one that does not. That is the trade-off in the table above, stated formally.

What does predict operational losses?

If size is only a rough proxy, what else matters? Chernobai et al. (2011) found that most losses at US financial institutions traced to breakdowns of internal control, and that losses were more likely at younger, more complex firms with higher credit risk and stronger CEO option-based pay. This supports APRA's approach of treating the formula as a floor and using supervision to add capital where governance and controls are weak, as it did with CBA in 2018.

Critical perspectives

  • Comparability or accuracy? Regulators prioritised consistency between banks. Critics argue that capital which ignores risk management weakens incentives to improve it. Both are legitimate goals, and no formula achieves both.
  • Income is an odd measure of risk. The business indicator grows with fee and interest income, which is only loosely related to operational risk. A high-margin, well-controlled business may hold more capital than a low-margin, poorly controlled one.
  • Discretion moves to supervisors. With less risk sensitivity in Pillar 1, more depends on Pillar 2 judgements, which are less transparent to outsiders.
  • The research is mostly international. Most studies use US or European data, and published analysis of how the standardised approach has affected Australian ADIs is limited.

Seminar questions

  1. Using the worked example, show how two ADIs with the same business indicator but very different loss histories end up with the same capital. Is that a problem?
  2. Peters and colleagues argue the standardised approach can be super-additive. Explain what this means and why it matters for bank mergers.
  3. APRA required CBA to hold additional operational risk capital after its prudential inquiry. What does this say about the relationship between Pillar 1 and Pillar 2?
  4. If you were designing a capital rule that rewarded good operational risk management without being easy to game, what would it include?

    Stress tests the Basel Committee's proposed standardised approach and argues it can be unstable, insensitive to risk and super-additive, and proposes alternatives.
  • Cope, E. W., Mignola, G., Antonini, G., & Ugoccioni, R. (2009). Challenges and pitfalls in measuring operational risk from loss data. Journal of Operational Risk, 4(4), 3–27. https://doi.org/10.21314/JOP.2009.069 Peer-reviewed
    Shows how hard it is to estimate operational risk at very high quantiles such as 99.9% from the loss data banks actually have, and how unstable model results and validation can be.
  • Chernobai, A., Jorion, P., & Yu, F. (2011). The determinants of operational risk in U.S. financial institutions. Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 46(6), 1683–1725. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109011000500 Peer-reviewed

12% up to $1.5 billion, 15% from $1.5 billion to $45 billion, 18% above $45 billion.

2. How is operational risk capital converted to risk-weighted assets?

Capital × 12.5 gives operational risk-weighted assets.
