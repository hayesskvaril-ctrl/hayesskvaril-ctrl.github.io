CPS 190 Recovery and Exit Planning and CPS 900 Resolution Planning

Two APRA standards that make financial institutions plan ahead for a severe crisis: how they would recover, how they would leave the market in an orderly way, and how APRA could step in if needed.

Intermediate About 14 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)
Status
Binding prudential standards. CPS 190 applied to banks and insurers from 1 January 2024 and to RSE licensees from 1 January 2025. CPS 900 commenced on 1 January 2024 and is being implemented entity by entity.
Applies to
CPS 190: ADIs, insurers and RSE licensees, with fuller requirements for significant financial institutions. CPS 900: all APRA-regulated entities, with resolution plans prepared by APRA for SFIs and some others that provide critical functions.
Guidance
CPG 190 Recovery and Exit Planning, and APRA guidance on resolution planning.

Key takeaways

  • Recovery means restoring financial strength after severe stress. Exit means leaving the market in an orderly way if recovery isn't possible. Resolution is when APRA steps in to manage a failing institution.
  • CPS 190 requires entities to have a recovery and exit plan. Significant financial institutions must include at least two severe stress scenarios (one systemic, one specific to the entity) and assess their recovery capacity.
  • CPS 900 requires entities to support APRA in preparing resolution plans, and to be ready to execute them.
  • Plans must be credible and tested, not documents that sit on a shelf.
  • For super trustees, "exit" planning often means being ready for an orderly transfer of members to another fund.

Recovery, exit and resolution

What CPS 190 requires

ElementIn plain English
Recovery and exit planA board-approved plan setting out how the entity would respond to severe financial stress and, if necessary, exit the market in an orderly way.
Indicators and triggersEarly warning indicators and trigger points that prompt escalation and use of the plan.
Recovery optionsCredible actions to restore capital and liquidity, such as raising capital, selling assets or businesses, or cutting costs, with an assessment of how quickly and reliably they would work.
Scenario analysis (SFIs)At least two scenarios severe enough to threaten viability: one systemic and one idiosyncratic.
Recovery capacity (SFIs)A quantitative assessment of how much capital and liquidity the recovery actions could rebuild, and a board view on whether it is enough.
Testing and reviewRegular review and testing of the plan, and for SFIs a comprehensive review at least every three years.

What CPS 900 requires

Resolution plans are prepared by APRA, not the entity. CPS 900 requires entities to support APRA in developing and maintaining those plans, for example by providing information and being able to support actions such as transferring business, and to take pre-positioning steps where APRA requires them so that resolution could be carried out in an orderly way.

What APRA found in super

  • In the first half of 2024 APRA reviewed how ready 16 trustees, managing about 42% of APRA-regulated super assets, were for CPS 190. It published its findings in a letter on 28 June 2024.
  • Some were well advanced, but there were common gaps in three areas: early-warning indicators and triggers that didn't fit each trustee's business and risk profile; recovery and exit options that needed more preparation to be workable; and communication strategies that needed to be proactive so plans could be carried out under stress.
  • APRA asked trustees to strengthen their plans within about six months, before the standard applied to them from 1 January 2025.

Worked example: a trustee's indicators and triggers

A mid-sized super trustee pays its running costs from its own reserves and a fee on members' accounts. Its plan sets indicators that tell it early when its own viability is under pressure:

  1. Early warning: net outflows of members above a set level for two quarters in a row; trustee reserves falling below a target; a key administrator in financial difficulty.
  2. Trigger: reserves falling below the level needed to fund the next 12 months of costs; a sustained fall in membership that makes the fund unviable at current fees.
  3. Response: once a trigger is hit, the board meets within days to choose from pre-assessed options, such as cost cuts, a capital injection from the trustee's owner, or a planned merger with a larger fund (a successor fund transfer).
  4. Exit: if recovery isn't credible, the plan sets out how members would be moved to another fund in an orderly way, and how members and APRA would be told.

Illustrative only. The indicators and options in a real plan depend on the trustee's structure and risks.

Common mistakes

  • Recovery options that rely on a buyer or market that may not exist in a crisis.
  • Triggers set so late that there is little time left to act.
  • A plan that is too long to use under pressure.
  • Never testing the plan with the people who would run it.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Which indicators would warn us first, and how early?
  2. Which of our recovery options would still be available in a market-wide crisis?
  3. When did the board last rehearse using the plan?

Frequently asked questions

Is recovery planning only about capital?

No. For banks and insurers capital and liquidity are central, but plans also cover operational capability and communication. For super trustees the focus is on threats to the trustee's own viability, not investment losses borne by members.

Does an entity write its own resolution plan?

No. APRA prepares resolution plans. Under CPS 900, the entity must help APRA prepare them and be able to support resolution actions.

Practical points

  • Make it usable. A plan should be quick to navigate in a crisis, with clear decision-makers and communication steps.
  • Test it, for example through a simulation exercise with the board and executives.
  • Connect it to the ICAAP, liquidity planning, stress testing and business continuity under CPS 230.
  • Be realistic about recovery options that depend on buyers or markets being available in a crisis.
