ASIC RG 181 AFS licensing: Managing conflicts of interest

ASIC's guidance on how financial services licensees must identify and manage conflicts of interest. Reissued in December 2025 with higher expectations.

Intermediate About 8 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026
Issuer
Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)
Status
Guidance on the law. The obligation is in s 912A(1)(aa) of the Corporations Act: licensees must have adequate arrangements to manage conflicts of interest.
Applies to
AFS licensees (including super trustees and responsible entities).
Current version
Reissued 16 December 2025, replacing the 2004 version.

Key takeaways

  • AFS licensees must have adequate arrangements to manage conflicts of interest arising in their financial services business.
  • The 2025 guide takes an objective approach: a conflict exists where there is a real and sensible possibility that it could adversely sway someone's judgement or actions.
  • Conflicts are managed by controlling, avoiding or disclosing them. Disclosure alone is often not enough; it usually supplements controls.
  • Licensees are expected to keep a conflicts register, have a tailored policy, train staff, and give the board and senior management oversight.
  • Responses must be matched to the nature and risk of each conflict. A generic, one-size-fits-all approach won't meet ASIC's expectations.

The three tools: control, avoid, disclose

ToolWhen it fitsExamples
AvoidThe conflict is so serious it can't be adequately controlled.Declining a mandate; not offering a product; removing an incentive entirely.
ControlMost conflicts: reduce the risk that the conflict affects decisions.Information barriers; independent decision-makers; separate reporting lines; approval by people without the conflict; monitoring of outcomes.
DiscloseAs a supplement to controls, so clients understand the conflict.Clear, specific, timely disclosure of relationships and benefits. Generic or buried disclosure doesn't help.

What adequate arrangements include

  • A conflicts management policy tailored to the business.
  • A conflicts register recording identified conflicts, how each is managed and who is responsible.
  • Processes to identify new conflicts, for example when launching products, entering relationships or restructuring.
  • Training so staff recognise and escalate conflicts.
  • Monitoring and review of whether controls work, and reporting to senior management and the board.
  • Records that show compliance, including decisions and disclosures.

Common conflict areas in financial services include related-party arrangements (such as a super trustee using an associated administrator or investment manager), remuneration and incentives, gifts and hospitality, and personal trading. For super trustees, SPS 521 and the best financial interests duty also apply. See Conflicts of interest for the wider topic.
