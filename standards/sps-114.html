SPS 114 Operational Risk Financial Requirement

APRA's standard requiring super trustees to set money aside so members are protected from losses caused by operational risk: how much to hold, what it can be used for and how to rebuild it.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 10 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)
Status
Binding prudential standard. The current version (F2024L01319) has applied since 1 July 2025, replacing the version in force since 1 July 2013. APRA's practice guide SPG 114 explains how APRA expects it to be applied; the guide is not itself enforceable.
Applies to
All RSE licensees (trustees of APRA-regulated super funds).
Main related law
Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 (SIS Act): the covenant to maintain and manage financial resources to cover operational risk, as trustee capital, a fund reserve or both (s 52(8)(b)), and the limits on a trustee paying itself back from fund assets for operational risk costs (s 56(2A)).

Key takeaways

  • Every super trustee must hold financial resources to protect members from losses caused by operational risk: the operational risk financial requirement, or ORFR.
  • The Board approves an ORFR strategy that sets a target amount and a lower tolerance limit, and explains how the money is held, invested, used, rebuilt and reviewed.
  • APRA's guidance expects a target of at least 0.175%, 0.20% or 0.25% of funds under management, depending on the trustee's size. The trustee's own risk profile can justify more.
  • Since July 2025 the money can be used to fix losses to members, to prevent and manage incidents under CPS 230, and to reduce a material surplus. Routine projects, insurance premiums and penalties are generally not appropriate uses.
  • If the resources fall below the tolerance limit, the Board must approve a replenishment plan before it is put in place. APRA must be told before any material change to the target amount.

SPS 114 in plain English

Things go wrong in running a super fund: a unit price is calculated incorrectly, contributions are allocated to the wrong accounts, an administrator's system fails or a fraud gets through. When members lose money because of an operational failure, someone has to pay to put it right. If the trustee had no money set aside, it might have to take the cost from other members' balances, which would be unfair, or delay fixing the problem.

The SIS Act therefore requires every trustee to keep financial resources to cover operational risk (s 52(8)(b)). SPS 114 sets the rules: the trustee decides how much it needs (the target amount), how far the balance can fall before it must be rebuilt (the tolerance limit), where the money is held, and what it can and can't be spent on. The money can sit as a reserve inside the fund, as the trustee's own capital, or a mix of both.

How big should the target be?

SPS 114 doesn't set a number. The trustee must work out a prudent amount from its own operational risks and the uses it expects to need. APRA's practice guide sets out the minimum it expects from a soundly run trustee with an effective risk management framework:

APRA guidance on the minimum ORFR target amount (SPG 114, from 1 July 2025)
Funds under management (all funds the trustee runs)Guidance minimum target
More than $165 billion0.175% of funds under management
$30 billion to $165 billion0.20% of funds under management
Less than $30 billion0.25% of funds under management

APRA's table describes both the middle and lowest bands as including $30 billion. For a defined benefit fund, the guide says the percentage may instead be applied to liabilities for vested benefits.

The guide adds some specific points:

  • Investments in other funds: where a fund invests through a pooled superannuation trust run by the same trustee, or another entity with its own operational risk requirement, APRA expects a target of at least 0.10% of that entity's funds under management.
  • New or very small funds, including funds winding down or transferring members, should set a target above the guidance minimum.
  • Service providers: APRA expects it will be hard for a trustee to show it can rely on a material service provider to pay for every incident in its service, so the contract's coverage and the provider's financial strength need to be considered.
  • Attribution: a trustee may attribute the target and resources to different funds, sub-funds or critical operations. Hybrid funds (with both defined benefit and accumulation members) should pay particular attention to fairness between the two groups.

Before July 2025, APRA's guidance expected at least 0.25% of funds under management for every trustee. The lower percentages for larger trustees are guidance on the minimum, not a cap; a trustee's own risk assessment can point to a higher figure.

What the money can and can't be used for

Examples from APRA's guidance (SPG 114)
APRA considers it appropriate to use the ORFR forAPRA considers it generally not appropriate for
Remediation of losses or missed gains caused by an operational risk incidentRoutine and business-as-usual costs, including building, maintaining or improving the operational risk framework
Fixing a vulnerability or weakness that is a material risk to critical operations, including addressing the root cause of an eventNew systems, processes or technology that are not a response to, or to prevent, an incident, near miss or identified material weakness
Preventing losses and disruption where an identified material weakness, if not addressed, could cause members a lossInsurance premiums, levies, and losses from investment underperformance
Carrying out a business continuity plan, a recovery and exit plan, or an orderly exit from a service provider in response to an operational risk incidentFinancial penalties incurred by the trustee or its directors, or any use that breaches the limits on a trustee being indemnified from the reserve

APRA expects trustees to document in the ORFR strategy the kinds of circumstances in which they would use the money, and to keep records of each use and potential use, including decisions not to use it. Over time those records, together with incident and near-miss data, help test whether the target is right.

The SIS Act backs up the purpose of the reserve. A fund's governing rules can't let the trustee reimburse itself from other fund assets for operational risk costs without first using up the reserve and any other ORFR resources, or recover from the fund money it spent from its own ORFR capital (s 56(2A)).

What changed on 1 July 2025

APRA remade SPS 114 with effect from the day CPS 230 started, and the new version refers to CPS 230 throughout. SPG 114 was updated at the same time.

Although the old notification triggers have gone from SPS 114, a material operational risk incident still has to be notified to APRA under CPS 230, within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, and may also be a reportable situation for ASIC (see breach and incident reporting).

Holding, investing and rebuilding the money

  • Reserve or capital. An operational risk reserve sits inside the fund and is funded by members; trustee capital belongs to the trustee. Either way, the money must be available quickly, including when the trustee is under stress. APRA expects trustee capital to be quarantined for the ORFR and monitored against the trustee's other liabilities.
  • Investment. A trustee may invest the reserve in line with the fund's own investment strategy, provided liquidity and the risk profile suit the reserve's purpose. APRA expects the reserve's investment earnings to be credited to the reserve.
  • Surpluses. A changed risk profile, falling membership, a successor fund transfer, a wind-up or strong returns can leave more money than needed. APRA expects independent advice, a fair and transparent response, and engagement with APRA before a material reduction. In a successor fund transfer, part of the reserve may move to the receiving trustee, or be allocated to member accounts before the transfer.
  • Forward-looking triggers. APRA expects trustees to set triggers and reporting that alert the Board early to issues with the funding, investment or use of the ORFR, or the adequacy of the target itself.

Worked example: a unit pricing error and a replenishment plan

A trustee runs one fund with $50 billion in funds under management. The guidance minimum for its size is 0.20%, or $100 million. Because it is midway through moving to a new administration platform, its risk assessment supports a higher target of $110 million, with a tolerance limit of $99 million. The reserve holds $110 million.

  1. A system change at the administrator causes unit prices to be understated for two weeks. Remediating affected members, including lost earnings, costs $14 million. This is a loss caused by an operational risk incident, so it is a permitted use (para 15(a)).
  2. The trustee also assesses the incident under CPS 230 and notifies APRA if it is material, and considers whether it is a reportable situation for ASIC.
  3. After the payment the reserve holds $96 million, below the $99 million tolerance limit, so a replenishment plan is required (para 18).
  4. The plan explains how the shortfall arose, the amount for the fund, how the $14 million will be rebuilt and from what source, and the expected date for reaching the target again. It can take into account a recovery from the administrator under the service agreement if the trustee believes it can reasonably rely on it (SPG 114). The Board approves the plan before it starts (para 19).
  5. Because this was a material operational risk incident, the trustee also reviews whether $110 million is still the right target, and reports the findings to the Board (para 21). If it decides on a materially different target, it tells APRA first (para 24).

Illustrative figures and steps for learning only, not advice about any trustee's obligations. Real decisions about funding sources raise questions of fairness between current and future members that need specialist input.

Common mistakes

  • Treating APRA's guidance minimum as the answer, instead of building the target from the trustee's own operational risks and expected uses (para 13).
  • Spending the reserve on routine projects or system upgrades that aren't a response to an incident, a near miss or an identified material weakness.
  • A reserve that isn't separately identifiable, or is invested so that it can't be reached quickly when needed (para 16).
  • Not reviewing the target after a material incident, a merger or a big change in membership (para 21).
  • Changing the target materially, for example after a successor fund transfer, without telling APRA first (para 24).
  • Confusing the ORFR tolerance limit with CPS 230 tolerance levels, which are about how long a critical operation can be disrupted, not about money.

How SPS 114 fits with other rules

  • CPS 230: defines operational risk, sets the incident notification rules, and shapes the permitted uses of the ORFR.
  • SPS 515: APRA treats SPS 114 as supporting SPS 515, which covers the prudent management of financial resources and reserves, and asks trustees to consider their operational risk resources in setting strategic objectives.
  • CPS 190: carrying out a recovery and exit plan in response to an operational risk incident is one of the uses APRA's guide lists.
  • SPS 310 and SPS 510: the external and internal audit requirements that SPS 114 relies on.
  • Remediation: the ORFR is often the source of funds for paying members back after an error, so other members don't bear the cost.

Questions a board can ask

  • How did we arrive at our target amount, and how does it compare with APRA's guidance minimum for our size?
  • What would a severe but plausible operational risk incident cost us, and could the ORFR cover it?
  • What did we use the ORFR for in the past year, and what did we decide not to use it for, and why?
  • How quickly could we get the money if we needed it, and in what form is it held?
  • If we fell below the tolerance limit, how would we rebuild the money, and who would bear the cost?

Frequently asked questions

Is 0.25% of funds under management still the rule?

It was never in the standard itself: it was APRA's guidance on the minimum target. Since 1 July 2025 the guidance minimum depends on the trustee's size (0.175%, 0.20% or 0.25%), and the trustee must still work out a prudent amount for its own risks.

Can the ORFR pay for a new administration system?

Generally not, if it is ordinary investment in systems. APRA's guide says it can be appropriate where the work responds to, or prevents, an operational risk incident or near miss, or fixes an identified material weakness that could cause members a loss.

Does APRA have to be told when the ORFR is used?

SPS 114 now only requires notice before a material change to the target amount. The earlier list of five notification triggers was removed in 2025. Material operational risk incidents must still be notified to APRA under CPS 230.
