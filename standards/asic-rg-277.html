ASIC RG 277 Consumer remediation

ASIC's guidance on how licensees should put things right when their conduct harms consumers: promptly, fairly, and without making people ask.

Intermediate About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 7 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)
Status
Guidance, underpinned by the obligation to provide services efficiently, honestly and fairly. Applies to remediations started on or after 27 September 2022 (replacing RG 256).
Applies to
AFS licensees (including super trustees) and Australian credit licensees.

Key takeaways

  • Remediation should start promptly and proactively once a licensee identifies that its conduct may have caused consumer loss, without waiting for complaints or regulator action.
  • Everyone who has or may have been affected should be included in scope, automatically where possible, and remediation should be free for consumers.
  • Where information is missing, licensees should use assumptions that are beneficial to consumers.
  • Compensation should put consumers back in the position they would have been in, including for the time value of money.
  • For former customers owed $5 or less, and money that can't be returned, RG 277 sets out options for residual payments.

Put it into practice: Playbook: Run a consumer remediation

The remediation process

Key expectations

AreaWhat RG 277 expects (summary)
When to startInitiate remediation when there are reasonable grounds to suspect that conduct may have caused consumer loss; don't wait for complaints, AFCA or ASIC.
ScopeInclude all consumers who have or may have been affected; don't narrow scope artificially.
Beneficial assumptionsWhere data is incomplete, use assumptions that favour consumers, so people don't fall out of scope or get underpaid.
CompensationReturn consumers to the position they would have been in, including foregone returns or interest.
Free and easyNo cost to consumers; minimise what consumers need to do; clear communication.
Returning moneyPay current customers regardless of amount. For former customers owed $5 or less (after interest), a residual remediation payment may be made without reasonable endeavours to locate them.
Residual fundsWhere money can't be returned and lodging it as unclaimed money isn't feasible, licensees (other than those holding pooled funds) may pay it to registered charities or not-for-profits, within set timeframes.
GovernanceBoard and senior management oversight, clear accountability, record keeping and quality assurance.

Super-specific points

  • Remediation often means correcting account balances and restoring lost investment earnings, not just refunding a fee.
  • Members may have rolled over to another fund, retired or died; tracing and paying them (or their estates) needs careful processes.
  • Where remediation is funded, trustees need to consider who bears the cost (for example, the trustee's own resources rather than other members) and document that decision.

Calculating what people are owed

Foregone returns or interest

Refunding the amount alone isn't enough: people also lost the use of their money. RG 277 allows three approaches:

  • Actual returns: what the money would really have earned. For super and managed funds, that usually means the actual returns of the investment option, using its unit prices for the period.
  • Beneficial assumptions: where actual figures can't reasonably be worked out, assumptions that favour consumers.
  • A combination of both.

Where a rate has to be assumed, ASIC's guidance gives the RBA cash rate plus 6% as an example of a rate that may be fair and reasonable if justified in the circumstances, and notes that a rate prescribed by legislation may sometimes be relevant. Because the cash rate changes, the rate should reflect the cash rate over the remediation period, not a single current figure. Industry submissions argued the example is too high for some products, such as fixed income funds, so the justification matters.

Review period and scope

The review period should go back to when the problem started, not to an arbitrary cut-off. ASIC's 2023 review found some licensees' policies could narrow review periods inappropriately, for example by requiring extra approvals to look back beyond a set number of years.

Finding people and paying them

  • Current customers are paid regardless of the amount, ideally automatically into their account.
  • Former customers require reasonable endeavours to find and pay them. ASIC has warned that rigid rules, such as a fixed number of contact attempts, may not be enough.
  • Small amounts: where a former customer is owed $5 or less (including interest), a licensee may make a residual remediation payment instead of trying to locate them.
  • Money that can't be returned goes to unclaimed money arrangements where possible, or, if that isn't feasible, may be paid to a registered charity or not-for-profit under RG 277's conditions (not available to licensees holding pooled funds such as super).

Worked example (illustrative)

This is an illustrative example for learning only; the figures are made up.

A super fund overcharged an administration fee of $60 a year for three years. One member's account was in the Balanced option, which returned 8%, 2% and 9% over those years. The member is still in the fund.

YearOverchargeGrown by the option's later returnsValue today
Year 1$60× 1.02 × 1.09$66.71
Year 2$60× 1.09$65.40
Year 3$60No later returns yet$60.00
Total credited$180$192.11

This simplified example assumes each fee was deducted at the end of its year. Real calculations work at the transaction date, use daily unit prices, and handle switches between options, tax and members who have left. See Remediation calculations in practice for the methods and a calculator.

What ASIC has found

  • Remediation takes too long. ASIC's 2018 review of significant breaches found it took on average more than five years from when an incident occurred until customers were remediated. Its 2023 breach reporting data showed 8% of reports involving compensation (247 reports) had taken, or were expected to take, more than a year to finalise compensation.
  • Policies that don't meet RG 277 (September 2023 review of large institutions): review periods narrowed by approval hurdles, beneficial assumptions not considered, pre-set rates for foregone returns without adequate review, and rigid "reasonable endeavours" rules.
  • Licence conditions for slow remediation. In February 2026 ASIC imposed additional licence conditions on Cambridge Mercantile (Australia), a Corpay subsidiary, after compliance failures including retail clients misclassified as wholesale and remediation that wasn't prompt. The conditions require a comprehensive remediation plan and an independent expert to review it and its effectiveness.
  • Super member services. In November 2025 the Federal Court ordered Cbus's trustee to pay a $23.5 million penalty over claims handling delays, alongside about $32 million in compensation to 7,402 people. See Superannuation trustee governance.

Common mistakes

  • Waiting for complaints or for the regulator before starting.
  • Narrow scope: limiting the review period or the affected group to what's easy to calculate.
  • Assumptions that favour the licensee, such as dropping people with incomplete data.
  • Refunding the fee but not the lost earnings.
  • Making people claim when the licensee could pay them automatically.
  • No quality assurance over calculations and payments, leading to a second remediation.
  • Fixing the customers but not the cause, so the problem recurs.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. How many remediation programs are open, how old are they, and how much is still to be paid?
  2. How do we decide the review period and the rate for lost earnings, and who approves departures from policy?
  3. What proportion of former customers have we actually paid, and what happens to money we can't return?
  4. What assurance do we have that the calculations and payments are right?
  5. Have the root causes been fixed?

Frequently asked questions

Is RG 277 binding?

It is ASIC's guidance, not law. But it sits on top of binding obligations, such as providing services efficiently, honestly and fairly and, for advice licensees, the notify, investigate and remediate obligations, and ASIC uses it when deciding whether to take action.

Does a licensee need ASIC's approval to run a remediation?

No. ASIC generally doesn't oversee individual remediation programs, but it will consider action where licensees fail to deliver fair and timely remediation.

Who pays for a super fund remediation?

Usually not the other members. Trustees typically fund it from their own resources (including, where appropriate, the operational risk financial reserve) or recover it from a responsible service provider, and should document the decision.
