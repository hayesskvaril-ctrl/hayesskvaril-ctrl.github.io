CPS 230 Operational Risk Management

APRA's cross-industry standard requiring banks, insurers and super trustees to manage operational risk, stay resilient through severe disruption, and control the risks of relying on service providers.

Intermediate About 25 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)
Status
Binding prudential standard. In force since 1 July 2025; targeted amendments effective 1 July 2026.
Applies to
Authorised deposit-taking institutions, general insurers, life insurers, private health insurers and RSE licensees (super trustees), and relevant groups.
Replaced
CPS 231 and SPS 231 (outsourcing), HPS 231, and CPS 232 and SPS 232 (business continuity).
Guidance
Prudential Practice Guide CPG 230 (finalised June 2024; amended 2026).

Key takeaways

  • CPS 230 has three pillars: operational risk management, business continuity, and management of service providers. The board is ultimately accountable for all three.
  • Entities must identify critical operations, set board-approved tolerance levels, and be able to stay within them through severe but plausible disruptions.
  • Material service providers need due diligence, formal agreements, monitoring, a register submitted to APRA each year, and APRA notification within 20 business days of entering into or materially changing an agreement for a service it relies on to undertake a critical operation.
  • Serious events must be notified to APRA within 72 hours (material operational risk incidents) or 24 hours (critical operations disrupted outside tolerance).
  • Requirements apply in proportion to size and complexity. Smaller entities (non-SFIs) had until 1 July 2026 for some business continuity and scenario analysis requirements; since then the full standard applies to every APRA-regulated entity.

Put it into practice: Playbook: Onboard a material service provider · Board briefing

Why CPS 230 exists

Before CPS 230, APRA's operational requirements were spread across separate standards on outsourcing and business continuity, written years earlier. Meanwhile, entities had become far more dependent on technology and service providers, and a series of incidents showed weaknesses in how operational risk was managed. CPS 230 brings everything into one standard with a sharper focus on resilience: not just preventing disruption, but continuing to deliver critical services to customers when disruption happens.

Significant financial institutions (SFIs)

CPS 230 applies to all APRA-regulated entities, in proportion to their size and complexity. When it started on 1 July 2025, smaller entities (non-SFIs) were given until 1 July 2026 for some business continuity and scenario analysis requirements. The version of CPS 230 in force from that date applies the same requirements to every entity and no longer refers to SFIs at all.

SFI status still matters under other standards, such as CPS 511 (remuneration) and CPS 190 (recovery and exit planning). The definitions sit in APRA's CPS 001 Defined terms and, for super trustees, in the standards that use them. An entity is an SFI if its total assets exceed:

Entity typeTotal assets threshold
Authorised deposit-taking institution$30 billion, once it has been above it for 12 months (raised from $20 billion on 1 July 2026). Banks above $300 billion form a third tier of "most significant financial institutions".
General insurer or life company$10 billion
Private health insurer$3 billion
RSE licensee$30 billion (one RSE, or combined across the RSEs it operates)

APRA can also determine an entity to be an SFI, for example because of its complexity or group membership.

Correction (8 October 2026): this section previously said the SFI thresholds were set under CPS 230, that some CPS 230 requirements stay lighter for smaller entities, and that the bank threshold was $20 billion. Since 1 July 2026 CPS 230 applies in full to all entities, the definitions sit in CPS 001 and other standards, and the bank threshold is $30 billion.

The 2026 amendments

On 30 April 2026, APRA finalised targeted amendments to CPS 230, CPG 230 and the material service provider register template, effective 1 July 2026. They provide limited exemptions from some contractual requirements for material arrangements with certain categories of "non-traditional" service providers, such as central banks and clearing and settlement facilities, where standard contractual terms are not practicable. APRA keeps a list of the exempt provider types in an attachment to the standard.

In more detail (check the attachment for the exact wording):

  • Who: the exempt categories include government agencies, regulators, central banks, financial market exchanges, operators of clearing and settlement facilities, operators of payment systems and schemes, and financial messaging infrastructure. APRA chose to exempt by type of provider, and can add others by written notice.
  • When: the arrangement must rely on the provider's standard terms or not be documented in a formal agreement at all.
  • What: only the specified contractual requirements are relaxed. Everything else still applies, including the register, risk management and business continuity planning, and APRA expects entities to keep actively managing the risk of relying on these providers.
  • Register: the material service provider register template now lets entities flag exempt arrangements.
  • Direction: APRA has said it expects the scope of exemptions to narrow over time, not expand.

Minimum critical operations

CPS 230 sets a floor: some operations must be classified as critical unless the entity can justify otherwise. In summary:

Entity typeMinimum critical operations (summary)
Authorised deposit-taking institutionsPayments, deposit-taking and management, custody, settlements and clearing.
General, life and private health insurersClaims processing.
RSE licensees (super trustees)Investment management and fund administration.
All entitiesCommentary also lists customer enquiries and the systems and infrastructure needed to support critical operations. Check the standard's current wording, as paragraph numbers changed with the 2026 amendments.

The list is a minimum, not the whole answer. Most entities identify further critical operations based on the harm to customers and the financial system if they were disrupted. See Mapping critical operations end to end.

How APRA supervises CPS 230

  • Notifications and the register: event notifications and the annual material service provider register give APRA a picture of each entity and of concentration across the system.
  • Targeted attention: APRA has indicated heightened supervision where an entity is an outlier in its service provider data or has a material event.
  • Top-down beats bottom-up: during implementation, APRA observed that entities taking a top-down approach (starting from critical operations and customer harm) gained better insight and made faster progress than those building up from detailed process inventories.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Which of our operations are critical, and how confident are we that the list is complete?
  2. Could we actually stay within our tolerance levels in a severe but plausible disruption, including the failure of a key provider?
  3. What did our last business continuity exercise show, and what has been fixed?
  4. Which material service providers have no credible, tested exit plan?
  5. How quickly would we know, and tell APRA, if a critical operation went outside tolerance?

A printable two-page version is in the CPS 230 board briefing.

Frequently asked questions

Does CPS 230 apply to small super funds and insurers?

Yes. It applies to all APRA-regulated entities, proportionately. Smaller entities that aren't significant financial institutions had until 1 July 2026 for some business continuity and scenario analysis requirements, and can scale their approach to their size and complexity.

Is a material service provider the same as an outsourcing arrangement?

Not exactly. CPS 230 looks at reliance: a provider is material if the entity relies on it for a critical operation or it exposes the entity to material operational risk. That can include providers that the old outsourcing standards didn't treat as outsourcing.

Do tolerance levels have to be met in every disruption?

They set the maximum disruption the board will accept. The entity must be able to stay within them through severe but plausible disruptions, and notify APRA within 24 hours if a critical operation goes outside tolerance.

Where does cyber security fit?

CPS 230 covers technology risk as part of operational risk and business continuity. Information security specifically is covered by CPS 234, which has its own notification rules.

Common gaps to watch for

Critical operations defined too narrowly or too broadly

Too narrow and important services go unprotected; too broad and resources are spread thin. The test is impact on customers and the financial system, not internal importance.

Tolerance levels that aren't really tested

A tolerance level only means something if testing shows the entity could actually stay within it in a severe scenario, including when a key provider fails.

Incomplete end-to-end mapping

Critical operations often depend on people, systems, data and providers across several business units. Gaps in mapping become gaps in the plan.

Contracts not updated

The transition for pre-existing contracts ended on 1 July 2026. Material arrangements without compliant agreements (other than exempt non-traditional providers) are a compliance gap.

Materiality assessments for incidents made too late

The 72-hour clock runs from becoming aware. Entities need a quick, documented way to judge materiality early, then update as facts emerge.
