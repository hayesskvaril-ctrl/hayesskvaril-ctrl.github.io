ASIC RG 78 Breach reporting by AFS and credit licensees

ASIC's guidance on the reportable situations regime: what licensees must report to ASIC, how to decide, and how fast.

Intermediate About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)
Status
Guidance on the law (Corporations Act ss 912D–912DAD and National Credit Act equivalents). The underlying reporting obligations are law.
Applies to
AFS licensees (including super trustees) and Australian credit licensees.
Related relief
ASIC Instrument 2024/620 (as amended by Instrument 2025/289), including extending the investigation period from 30 to 60 days from 27 June 2025.

Key takeaways

  • Licensees must report reportable situations, mainly significant breaches (or likely breaches) of core obligations, within 30 calendar days.
  • Some breaches are "deemed significant" automatically; others are judged against significance factors.
  • Long-running investigations, gross negligence and serious fraud are also reportable, and some matters about other licensees' advisers and brokers.
  • The clock starts when the licensee first knows, or is reckless about, reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen, not when certainty is reached.
  • ASIC publishes information about the reports it receives.
  • ASIC has found many breaches take far too long to identify, and in 2026 the Federal Court penalised a super trustee $10.3 million for systemic failures to report.

What RG 78 covers

TopicSummary
Core obligationsInclude the general licensee obligations (e.g. efficiently, honestly and fairly; complying with financial services laws) and specified financial services laws.
Deemed significantOffences with 12+ months' imprisonment (3+ if dishonesty); civil penalty provisions (unless excluded); misleading or deceptive conduct (s 1041H(1) Corporations Act, s 12DA(1) ASIC Act); breaches causing or likely to cause material loss or damage to clients.
Significance factorsFor other breaches: the number or frequency of similar breaches; impact on the licensee's ability to provide services; the extent to which arrangements to ensure compliance are inadequate; and actual or potential loss.
InvestigationsAn investigation into whether a significant breach has occurred becomes reportable if it continues for more than 60 days (under ASIC relief; the Act says 30).
Other reportable situationsGross negligence or serious fraud in providing services; certain conditions specified in regulations.
Reporting about othersIn specified circumstances, reporting reportable situations involving another licensee's individual advisers or mortgage brokers.
Timing and lodgementWithin 30 calendar days of first knowing, or being reckless about, reasonable grounds; using the prescribed form via the ASIC Regulatory Portal.

A step-by-step decision flow is on the Incident and breach management page, and the full regime-by-regime comparison is in Breach and incident reporting obligations.

Key ideas in the guide

"Reasonable grounds to believe"

The 30-day clock starts when the licensee first knows, or is reckless about whether, there are reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen. That is an objective test: it asks whether the information available would lead a reasonable person to believe a breach has happened, not whether the licensee is certain. ASIC's guidance is that licensees should not wait until an investigation is complete, or until legal advice confirms a breach, before reporting.

When the licensee "knows"

A licensee is a company, so the question is whose knowledge counts. ASIC's guidance indicates that information known to staff and others acting for the licensee can be attributed to it. That is why incident systems need to capture the date anyone first raised the issue, and why delays in escalation can make a report late even when the compliance team acted quickly.

"Likely" breaches

The regime covers breaches that are likely to happen, not only those that have. A licensee that becomes aware it is no longer able to comply with a core obligation, for example because a key system has failed, may already have a reportable situation.

Material loss or damage

A breach that causes, or is likely to cause, material loss or damage to clients or members is deemed significant. ASIC's guidance indicates that loss or damage can include non-financial harm, and that whether it is material depends on the circumstances, including the position of the people affected: a small amount can be material to someone on a low income.

What a report contains

Reports are lodged through the ASIC Regulatory Portal using the prescribed form. The form asks for, among other things, a description of the situation, how it was identified, when it started and ended, the number of clients affected, the financial impact, rectification and remediation steps, and root cause. ASIC expects licensees to update their report as the facts develop.

Worked example: applying the tests

This is an illustrative example for learning only. It is simplified and is not a view on any real situation.

A super trustee's monthly reconciliation shows that an administration fee was set up wrongly after a product change. About 1,200 members were overcharged around $40 each over eight months, about $48,000 in total. The reconciliation was flagged on 3 March; finance confirmed the pattern on 10 March; legal review finished on 20 April.

QuestionAnalysis
Is a core obligation involved?Likely yes: charging fees that aren't authorised or disclosed engages the general licensee obligations, such as providing services efficiently, honestly and fairly.
Is it deemed significant?Check each test. Material loss is arguable: $40 may be material to some members, and the total is substantial. A civil penalty provision may also be engaged, depending on the facts.
If not deemed, is it significant on the factors?A control failed after a change and wasn't detected for eight months, which points to inadequate compliance arrangements. Many members were affected over a long period.
When did the clock start?Probably around 10 March, when there were reasonable grounds to believe members had been overcharged, not 20 April when legal review finished. The report would be due within 30 days of that date.
Other regimes?The trustee also assesses APRA reporting under SIS Act s 29JA (a single report to APRA with the information APRA asks for can count as lodged with ASIC), and remediation under RG 277.

Common mistakes

  • Waiting for legal advice or a completed investigation before starting the clock.
  • Looking only at dollar loss per person, not total impact, vulnerability or the control failure behind it.
  • Missing the deemed significance tests, especially civil penalty provisions.
  • Escalation delays in the business that use up the 30 days before compliance hears about the issue.
  • Lodging and forgetting: not updating ASIC when the scope or cause changes.
  • Not reporting long investigations once they pass 60 days.

Frequently asked questions

Does RG 78 apply to super trustees?

Yes. Super trustees hold an AFS licence, so they must report reportable situations to ASIC. They also have APRA breach reporting obligations under SIS Act s 29JA.

Is RG 78 itself law?

No. RG 78 is ASIC's guidance on how it interprets and administers the law. The obligations are in the Corporations Act and the National Consumer Credit Protection Act, and the relief is in ASIC's legislative instrument.

Can we report "just in case"?

Licensees can report a matter they believe may be reportable. ASIC's data shows reports vary a lot between licensees; what matters is a consistent, documented approach.

Practical points

  • Record the awareness date. Capture when anyone in the business first had information pointing to a possible breach; ASIC may attribute staff knowledge to the licensee.
  • Don't wait for the investigation to finish. Report once there are reasonable grounds, and update ASIC as facts emerge.
  • Document "not reportable" decisions with reasons, so the assessment can be reviewed later.
  • Look for patterns. Several minor breaches with the same cause can together be significant.
  • Coordinate across regimes. The same event may also need to be reported to APRA, the OAIC or clients (see s 912EA for advice licensees).
