ASIC RG 97 Disclosing fees and costs in PDSs and periodic statements

ASIC's guidance on how super funds and managed funds must show their fees and costs, so people can see what a product really costs and compare it with others.

Intermediate About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)
Status
Guidance on the law. The fee disclosure requirements themselves are in the Corporations Act, Schedule 10 of the Corporations Regulations and ASIC Corporations (Disclosure of Fees and Costs) Instrument 2019/1070.
Applies to
Issuers of superannuation products and managed investment products (including notified foreign passport funds) offered to retail clients.
Current version
Reissued 28 September 2020. The updated regime applies to PDSs given on or after 30 September 2022, and to periodic statements for reporting periods starting on or after 1 July 2021.

Key takeaways

  • RG 97 sets out how fees and costs must be shown in the PDS (the "Fees and costs summary") and in each member's or investor's periodic statement.
  • Ongoing costs are grouped into a small number of headings (for super: administration, investment and transaction costs) so that products can be compared on a like-for-like basis.
  • The PDS must include a worked example of the annual cost on a $50,000 balance, and a "cost of product" figure for each investment option.
  • Indirect costs count. Costs deducted inside underlying investments, not just fees charged to the account, must be included, which makes estimation and data from investment managers critical.
  • Accurate fee disclosure depends on strong fee governance: authority to charge, accurate calculation, and disclosure that matches what is actually charged. Getting it wrong is a common cause of breaches and remediation.

What problem RG 97 solves

A fee of 1% a year sounds small, but over a working life it can reduce a super balance by tens of thousands of dollars. Before the current regime, products described similar costs in different ways, and some costs sat inside underlying investments where people could not see them. RG 97 aims to make disclosure complete (all fees and costs, direct and indirect), consistent (the same headings and method across products) and comparable (a standard worked example).

ASIC's 2018 review (Report 581) found the earlier approach was hard for consumers to use and inconsistently applied. That led to the updated guide and legislative instrument released in 2019 and 2020.

What RG 97 covers

TopicSummary
Fees and costs summaryA prescribed table near the start of the fees section of the PDS, listing each fee or cost, the amount and how and when it is paid. Wording and order follow the template.
Example of annual fees and costsA worked example for one option (for super, the MySuper or main balanced option) showing what the fees and costs would be over a year on a $50,000 balance.
Cost of productA single annual figure for each investment option, on the same $50,000 basis, so members can compare options within a fund and across funds.
Indirect costsAmounts that reduce the return but are not charged directly, such as fees inside underlying funds or costs of derivatives and other interposed vehicles. These must be included, estimated where needed, with the basis of any estimate explained.
Performance feesShown as part of investment (or management) fees and costs, generally based on an average of past years so that a single good or bad year does not distort the figure.
Transaction costsCosts of buying and selling assets, such as brokerage, stamp duty and spreads. Shown separately, net of amounts recovered through buy-sell spreads.
Periodic statementsEach member or investor must be told the fees and costs they paid over the period, including fees deducted directly from their account and fees and costs deducted from their investment, and a total.
Additional explanationExtra information about fees and costs (for example how performance fees are calculated) can go in a section after the summary, but must not undermine it.

Worked example: reading a fees and costs summary

The table below uses made-up figures for a fictional balanced option to show how the pieces add up. It is not taken from any real fund.

Illustrative example: $50,000 balance for one year
ItemRateAnnual cost
Administration fees and costs$1.50 per week plus 0.20% of balance$78 + $100 = $178
Investment fees and costs (incl. performance fees)0.60%$300
Transaction costs0.10%$50
Cost of product (ongoing annual fees and costs)about 1.06%$528
Member activity fees, e.g. switching or adviceOnly if the member uses themNot included above

The same idea drives comparison: if another fund's cost of product on the same $50,000 balance is $400, the difference of $128 a year compounds over time. Fees are only one factor, though. Net returns after fees and the services provided also matter, which is why the APRA performance test and member outcomes assessments look at them together.

Fee governance: the risk and compliance angle

For trustees and responsible entities, RG 97 is not just a documents exercise. Disclosure has to match what is actually charged, and that depends on controls across product, finance, administration and investments. Common control points include:

  • Authority to charge: check that every fee is allowed under the governing rules or constitution and disclosed in the PDS before it is deducted.
  • Data from investment managers and platforms: indirect and transaction costs rely on third-party data. Obtain it on a consistent basis, check it for reasonableness and record the estimation method.
  • Calculation accuracy: reconcile fees deducted in the administration system with the rates disclosed, especially after product changes, system migrations or fee changes.
  • Periodic statement testing: sample statements to confirm the fees shown match what members actually paid.
  • Change control: a fee change triggers updates to the PDS, website, calculators, periodic statements and, for super, APRA reporting. A checklist helps make sure none are missed.
  • Breach assessment: charging fees that were not disclosed or not permitted, or disclosing materially wrong figures, may be a significant breach under RG 78 and lead to remediation under RG 277.

Fee rules in super beyond disclosure

RG 97 is about how fees are shown. Separate rules in the SIS Act limit what super funds can charge in the first place:

RuleIn plain English
Exit fees banned (from 1 July 2019)Funds can't charge a fee for leaving or rolling over.
Fee cap for small balances (from 1 July 2019)Administration fees, investment fees and indirect costs on accounts with balances below $6,000 at the end of the income year are capped at 3% of the balance, with any excess refunded.
MySuper permitted feesMySuper products can charge only the fee types the SIS Act allows, and must charge them on the same basis to all members (with limited exceptions such as employer-negotiated administration fees).
Best financial interests duty and authority to chargeFees must be authorised by the governing rules, disclosed, and set and spent in members' best financial interests. See SPS 515.
Advice fees from superFees for personal advice deducted from a member's account must meet the conditions in the SIS Act on what advice can be paid for from super. These rules were changed in 2024–25 as part of the Delivering Better Financial Outcomes reforms, so check the current law.

For more on super fee governance, see the Superannuation page.

Common mistakes

  • Indirect costs left out or stale, because underlying managers' data wasn't requested, checked or updated.
  • Estimates without a documented basis, so nobody can show later why a figure was reasonable.
  • Performance fees shown on the wrong basis, or not averaged as required.
  • Transaction costs netted incorrectly against buy-sell spread recoveries.
  • The PDS, website, calculators and periodic statements disagreeing after a fee change.
  • Fees deducted at rates that don't match the PDS after a system migration or product change, leading to breach reports and remediation.
  • Extra explanation that contradicts or buries the summary, which RG 97 does not allow.

Questions for a fee governance review

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Can we trace every fee in the PDS to the governing rules and to the administration system's settings?
  2. When did we last reconcile fees actually deducted against disclosed rates, and what did we find?
  3. Where do our indirect and transaction cost figures come from, and how do we check them?
  4. What happens, step by step, when a fee changes? Who signs off each document?
  5. How many fee-related incidents have we had in the last two years, and what were the root causes?

Frequently asked questions

Is RG 97 law?

No. RG 97 is ASIC's guidance on how to comply. The requirements themselves are in the Corporations Act, Schedule 10 of the Corporations Regulations and ASIC's legislative instrument. ASIC has said it will not look to take action where issuers make reasonable endeavours to comply and don't mislead consumers.

Does the cost of product include insurance premiums?

No. Insurance premiums and member activity fees, such as switching or advice fees, are shown separately and are not part of the ongoing cost of product figure.

Why can two funds with the same strategy show different transaction costs?

Transaction costs depend on how much an option trades and what it buys. One-off events, such as buying property and paying stamp duty, can make one year look very different, which is why ASIC moved to average stamp duty.

Does RG 97 apply to platforms and wholesale products?

It applies to superannuation products and managed investment products offered to retail clients. Disclosure to wholesale clients is not covered by these PDS rules.

Current developments

  • Stamp duty: ASIC consulted from 28 November 2025 (consultation CS 39, closed 20 February 2026) and then announced changes to how stamp duty is shown in fees and costs summaries. Stamp duty paid in one year will be spread over the following seven years, so large one-off property or infrastructure purchases don't cause a spike in reported transaction costs. ASIC described this as an interim measure, made by amending Instrument 2019/1070. Check ASIC's announcement for the start date.
  • Portfolio holdings disclosure: in the same November 2025 release, ASIC sought feedback on super funds' portfolio holdings disclosure requirements.
  • Wider review of RG 97: ASIC has committed to a broader review of RG 97 in the 2026–27 financial year, responding to other concerns industry raised about fees and costs disclosure. The outcome is not yet known, so parts of this page may change. See ASIC's RG 97 review and consultation page for the latest.
