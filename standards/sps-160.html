SPS 160 Defined Benefit Matters

APRA's standard for super trustees that run defined benefit funds: keeping enough assets to pay the promised benefits, regular actuarial checks, and what happens when funding falls short.

Intermediate About 14 minutes Last reviewed: 12 October 2026 Checked against sources
Issuer
Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)
Status
Binding prudential standard (F2013L01247), in force since 1 July 2013.
Applies to
RSE licensees of defined benefit funds: any regulated super fund with at least one defined benefit member, including public sector schemes and defined benefit sub-funds. Some requirements apply directly to the fund's RSE actuary. It does not apply to the Commonwealth's CSS, PSS and Military Superannuation Benefits Scheme (Attachment A).
Main related law
Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 and the SIS Regulations, including the funding and solvency rules and technical insolvency in Part 9 of the Regulations.

Key takeaways

  • A defined benefit fund is in an unsatisfactory financial position when its assets, leaving out the money held for the ORFR, don't cover members' vested benefits.
  • The Board sets a shortfall limit: how far below that line the fund can go while still expecting temporary market falls to correct within a year. It must be set so the fund breaches it before it could become technically insolvent.
  • An RSE actuary carries out regular investigations, at least every three years or annually for funds paying defined benefit pensions, and an interim investigation if the shortfall limit may have been breached.
  • If the actuary finds the position unsatisfactory, a clock starts: the actuary's statement within 15 business days, a copy to APRA within 15 business days, a Board-approved restoration plan within three months, and restoration within three years.
  • Trustees that self-insure benefits must hold reserves or APRA-approved arrangements, attest each year that self-insurance is in members' best interests and keep a contingency plan.

SPS 160 in plain English

Most Australians are in accumulation funds, where the benefit is simply the account balance. In a defined benefit fund, the benefit is set by a formula, usually based on salary and years of service, or is a specified amount. The fund's assets must be big enough to pay those promises, and the employer that sponsors the fund usually contributes at rates the actuary recommends, depending on its obligations under the fund's rules. Many defined benefit funds are closed to new members, but they can still carry long-term obligations, including lifetime pensions.

SPS 160 is APRA's early warning system for these funds. It sets a test for when funding is unsatisfactory, requires regular actuarial health checks, and lays out a strict sequence of steps, with deadlines, when the fund falls short. Actuaries often express the test as a ratio of assets to vested benefits, sometimes called the vested benefits index: 100% or more is satisfactory.

Each sub-fund must be investigated, and the actuary works having regard to the professional standards of the Institute of Actuaries of Australia (now known as the Actuaries Institute), with SPS 160 prevailing if they conflict (paras 19, 20). The trustee gets the report of a regular or initial investigation within six months of the valuation date, and gives APRA a copy within 15 business days if asked (paras 21, 22). An interim report is due within three months and goes to APRA within 15 business days of receipt (paras 28, 29).

A regular or initial report must cover, at a minimum (para 23): the value of assets excluding the ORFR; a three-year projection of the financial position; the actuary's opinion on whether assets cover accrued benefits, with comment on the assumptions and methods; whether the position is unsatisfactory and whether the shortfall limit should be reviewed; liabilities for minimum benefits; funding and solvency certificate matters where the fund is used for superannuation guarantee; recommended employer contributions for the next three years; and, where defined benefit pensions are paid, whether there is a high degree of probability the fund can keep paying them. Public sector schemes that are not fully funded have a shorter list (para 24).

The restoration plan must set out, at a minimum, the trustee's view of how likely it is that the employer will make the recommended contributions, any investment strategy changes, the likely impact on benefit payments, and how the actuary and Board will monitor progress (para 33).

Self-insurance

Some defined benefit funds are permitted to self-insure benefits, such as death and disability benefits, rather than buying insurance. They must maintain reserves or APRA-approved arrangements to fund current and future self-insurance liabilities, attest each year that self-insuring remains in members' best interests, keep a contingency plan for an orderly transfer of the insurance if the Board decides it no longer is, and have the arrangement overseen through the regular actuarial investigation (paras 36, 37). SPS 250 adds the general requirements for offering insured benefits.

Worked example: a market fall breaches the shortfall limit

A corporate defined benefit sub-fund pays no pensions. Its Board has set a shortfall limit of 97% (assets, excluding the ORFR, to vested benefits). The last regular investigation, 14 months ago, found the fund in a satisfactory position.

  1. Monthly monitoring (para 13) shows the ratio has fallen to 95% after a sharp market fall. The next regular investigation is more than six months away, so the trustee appoints the RSE actuary for an interim investigation as soon as practicable (para 17(a)).
  2. The interim report, due within three months, confirms the fund is unsatisfactory and below its limit. The trustee gives APRA a copy within 15 business days (paras 27 to 29).
  3. Within 15 business days of that finding, the actuary provides a statement recommending higher employer contributions to restore the fund within three years. The trustee sends it to APRA within 15 business days and consults the employer (paras 31, 32).
  4. The Board approves a restoration plan within three months, covering the employer's capacity and willingness to pay, any investment changes and the effect on benefits, and sends it to APRA and the actuary within 15 business days (paras 32, 33).
  5. The actuary and Board monitor progress, and the Board considers whether the 97% limit is still right, which the actuary may also have commented on (para 23(d)).

Illustrative figures and steps for learning only, not advice about any trustee's obligations. The shortfall limit is each Board's own decision.

Common mistakes

  • Including the ORFR reserve in the assets when testing the funding position (para 8).
  • Monitoring that is too infrequent to detect a shortfall limit breach on a timely basis (para 13).
  • Setting the shortfall limit without checking it is breached before the fund could become technically insolvent (para 11).
  • Treating the 15-business-day and three-month deadlines as running from the regular investigation's date rather than from the actuary's finding or statement.
  • A restoration plan that assumes employer contributions without assessing whether the employer can and will pay (para 33(a)).
  • Not revisiting the self-insurance attestation and contingency plan each year (para 36).

How SPS 160 fits with other rules

  • SPS 114: money held for the ORFR is excluded when testing whether the fund's funding is satisfactory.
  • SIS Regulations Part 9: funding and solvency certificates, minimum benefits and technical insolvency, which SPS 160 builds on.
  • SPS 530: investment governance, including the investment strategy a restoration plan may change.
  • SPS 250: requirements for offering insured benefits, including self-insured ones.

Questions a board can ask

  • What is our fund's funding ratio today, how often do we measure it, and how close are we to the shortfall limit?
  • Why did we choose our shortfall limit, and when did we last test it against a severe market fall?
  • How confident are we that the employer-sponsor could meet higher contributions if needed?
  • If we self-insure, is our annual attestation backed by actuarial evidence, and is the contingency plan current?

Frequently asked questions

Does SPS 160 apply to accumulation funds?

Only if the fund has at least one defined benefit member. A fund with a defined benefit sub-fund applies SPS 160 to that sub-fund.

Is being in an unsatisfactory financial position a breach?

Not in itself. Market falls can push a fund below 100% of vested benefits. SPS 160 requires monitoring, actuarial investigation and, where triggered, a restoration plan within set deadlines. Failing to take those steps is the problem.

Can benefits be cut while the fund is in deficit?

Only if the fund's governing rules allow it. SPS 160 requires the trustee to appoint an actuary to advise on this, among other things, while the position is unsatisfactory (para 32(c)).
