ISO 31000 Risk management

The world's most widely referenced risk management guideline, with Australian roots. It describes what good risk management looks like for any organisation and any type of risk.

Beginner About 11 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources
Publisher
International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Adopted in Australia as AS ISO 31000:2018 by Standards Australia.
Current edition
ISO 31000:2018 Risk management – Guidelines. A revision is in development (at committee draft stage in 2026).
Status
Voluntary guidance. Not certifiable. Copyrighted and sold by ISO and national standards bodies.
Companion documents
ISO 31073:2022 (vocabulary, replacing ISO Guide 73:2009); IEC 31010:2019 (risk assessment techniques).

Copyright note

ISO standards are copyrighted. This page describes ISO 31000 in our own words and does not reproduce its text or figures. For the full standard, see ISO or Standards Australia.

Key takeaways

  • ISO 31000 grew out of AS/NZS 4360, first published in 1995 and described as the world's first formal risk management standard.
  • It has three parts: principles (what effective risk management looks like), a framework (how to embed it in the organisation) and a process (how to manage specific risks).
  • It defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives, positive or negative.
  • It is deliberately generic and short, which makes it adaptable but leaves detail (such as risk appetite and quantitative methods) to other sources.

A standard with Australian roots

In 1995, Standards Australia and Standards New Zealand published AS/NZS 4360 Risk management, the first formal risk management standard anywhere. It was revised in 1999 and 2004, and the 2004 version became the starting draft for an international standard. ISO 31000 was first published in 2009 and substantially revised in 2018 to be shorter, clearer and more focused on integrating risk management into how organisations are run.

The three parts

Principles

The principles describe the characteristics of effective risk management, with the overall purpose of creating and protecting value. In our words: risk management should be part of everything the organisation does; follow a consistent, systematic approach; be tailored to the organisation; involve the right stakeholders; respond to change; use the best information available (while recognising its limits); take account of how people and culture affect risk; and keep improving.

Framework

The framework is about embedding risk management into governance and operations. Leadership and commitment from the board and top management sit at the centre. Around them: integrating risk management into structures and decisions; designing the arrangements (roles, resources, communication); implementing them; evaluating whether they work; and improving them.

Process

The process is how specific risks are managed: define the scope, context and criteria; assess risks (identify, analyse, evaluate); treat them; monitor and review; communicate and consult throughout; and record and report. It is covered in more detail in What is risk management? and Risk assessment methodologies.

How it's used in Australia

  • Government: many public sector risk policies reference or align with ISO 31000.
  • Financial services: APRA's standards don't mandate any particular framework, but ISO 31000's language is commonly used in risk management frameworks and policies.
  • Other sectors: health, infrastructure, mining and not-for-profits frequently use it as the base for their frameworks.

Strengths and limitations

StrengthsLimitations
Short, generic and adaptable to any organisationLittle practical detail on how to do things such as setting risk appetite or aggregating risks
Common international language for riskNot certifiable, so there's no external check that it has been applied well
Treats risk as including upside as well as downsideEasy to adopt the words without changing decisions ("paper compliance")
Strong emphasis on integration and cultureMust be supplemented with techniques (e.g. IEC 31010) and sector rules (e.g. APRA)
