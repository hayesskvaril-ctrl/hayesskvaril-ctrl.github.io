COSO ERM and Internal Control frameworks

COSO publishes two widely used frameworks. One covers enterprise risk management and how risk connects to strategy. The other covers internal control and how an organisation makes sure its controls work.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources
Publisher
The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO), a US private-sector initiative of five professional bodies.
Frameworks
Enterprise Risk Management – Integrating with Strategy and Performance (2017), which updated the 2004 ERM framework. Internal Control – Integrated Framework (2013), which updated the 1992 original.
Status in Australia
Voluntary. No Australian law or APRA standard requires COSO, but many organisations and internal audit teams use it as a reference model.

Copyright note

The COSO frameworks are copyrighted publications. This page summarises them in our own words, and our diagram is our own drawing, not COSO's.

Key takeaways

  • COSO ERM (2017) is about strategy and performance. It treats risk as something to weigh when setting strategy, not only as something to control afterwards.
  • COSO Internal Control (2013) is about controls. It helps an organisation achieve its operations, reporting and compliance objectives with reasonable assurance.
  • Each has five components. ERM has 20 principles and Internal Control has 17.
  • Under the Internal Control framework, an effective system needs all components and relevant principles to be present and functioning, and to operate together.
  • The two frameworks complement each other. Internal control sits inside the wider ERM picture.

The two frameworks side by side

COSO ERM (2017): risk, strategy and performance

The 2017 framework moved away from treating enterprise risk management as a separate compliance exercise. Its central idea is that risk should shape how an organisation chooses its strategy and sets its objectives, and how it judges its performance. It also stresses culture: people's behaviour decides whether risk management works in practice.

ComponentWhat it's about (our summary of its principles)
Governance and culture (5)Board oversight of risk; operating structures; defining the culture the organisation wants; commitment to core values; attracting, developing and keeping capable people.
Strategy and objective-setting (4)Understanding the business context; defining risk appetite; weighing alternative strategies; setting business objectives at different levels.
Performance (5)Identifying risks; assessing how severe they are; prioritising them; choosing responses; looking at risk as a whole portfolio, not one at a time.
Review and revision (3)Watching for major changes; reviewing risk and performance; improving ERM over time.
Information, communication and reporting (3)Using information and technology; communicating risk information; reporting on risk, culture and performance.

COSO Internal Control (2013): making controls work

The Internal Control framework defines internal control as a process, run by the board, management and staff, that gives reasonable assurance that the organisation will achieve three kinds of objectives:

  • Operations: effective and efficient operations, including protecting assets.
  • Reporting: reliable internal and external reporting, both financial and non-financial.
  • Compliance: following applicable laws and regulations.
ComponentWhat it's about (our summary of its principles)
Control environment (5)Commitment to integrity and ethics; independent board oversight; structures, reporting lines and authority; commitment to competence; holding people accountable.
Risk assessment (4)Setting clear objectives; identifying and analysing risks; considering the potential for fraud; spotting significant changes.
Control activities (3)Choosing and building controls that reduce risks; general controls over technology; putting controls in place through policies and procedures.
Information and communication (3)Using relevant, good-quality information; communicating internally; communicating with external parties.
Monitoring activities (2)Ongoing and separate evaluations of whether controls work; reporting and fixing deficiencies in time.

Each principle comes with "points of focus", which are typical characteristics that help in designing and assessing controls. They are aids to judgement, not a checklist that must be met in full. In 2023 COSO also published supplementary guidance on applying the framework to sustainability reporting. The 2013 framework itself was unchanged.

How they compare

FeatureCOSO ERM (2017)COSO Internal Control (2013)
Main questionAre we taking the right risks to achieve our strategy?Are our controls reliable enough to meet our objectives?
ScopeWhole enterprise, including strategy settingOperations, reporting and compliance objectives
Structure5 components, 20 principles5 components, 17 principles, points of focus
Typical usersBoards, CROs, strategy and risk teamsManagement, control owners, internal and external auditors
RelationshipInternal control is part of ERM. COSO says the ERM framework doesn't replace the Internal Control framework, and the two are designed to work together.

Using COSO in Australia

  • APRA's standards don't prescribe a framework. CPS 220 and CPS 230 set outcomes. An entity can use COSO, ISO 31000, or its own model, provided the requirements are met.
  • Internal audit often uses the Internal Control framework to structure reviews and rate how well controls are designed and working.
  • Control testing: the "present and functioning" idea maps neatly onto testing design effectiveness and operating effectiveness. See Control design and testing.
  • Mixing frameworks is common. Many organisations take their process language from ISO 31000, their control ideas from COSO and their roles from the Three Lines model.

Worked example: testing one control with COSO's internal control lens

A fund's control: "Payments over $50,000 need approval by two authorised people." Thinking through COSO's five components:

  1. Control environment: do leaders support the control, or is it routinely bypassed when payments are urgent?
  2. Risk assessment: does the control address the real risks, such as fraud, error and payments split to stay under $50,000?
  3. Control activities: is the system set up to require two approvers, or does it rely on people remembering?
  4. Information and communication: do approvers see enough information to make a real decision?
  5. Monitoring: does anyone check exception reports, such as many payments just under the limit?

Illustrative only.

Common mistakes

  • Using COSO as a checklist of components without judging whether they work together.
  • Confusing the two frameworks: the internal control framework (2013) and the ERM framework (2017) have different purposes.

Frequently asked questions

Is COSO used in Australia?

Yes, especially in organisations with US links or listings, and by internal auditors. Many Australian organisations combine it with ISO 31000.

Is COSO a standard?

No. COSO publishes frameworks and guidance. They are widely used but not legally required in Australia.
