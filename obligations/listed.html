Obligations for listed companies outside financial services

The 8 regimes that usually apply to listed companies outside financial services, grouped by theme, with 19 plain-English obligation summaries, control objectives, evidence and notification deadlines.

8 regimes 19 obligation summaries 3 notification deadlines Last reviewed: 10 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • This page lists the regimes that typically apply to listed companies outside financial services. Whether each one applies to a particular organisation depends on its licences, products, size and thresholds.
  • Each summary groups related obligations in plain English. It is a starting point for an obligations register, not a substitute for the law: always read the official source.
  • The IDs match the obligations library and the GRC model builder, so you can download the full set or build a linked model.

Governance and accountability

Whistleblower protections

ASIC Applies to: Public companies, large proprietary companies and super trustee companies must have a whistleblower policy; the protections apply more widely.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-WB-01Have a whistleblower policy (public companies, large proprietary companies, super trustees)
4 individual obligations
  1. Public companies must have a whistleblower policy and make it available to officers and employees Corporations Act s 1317AI(1)
  2. Large proprietary companies must have one from 6 months after the end of their first financial year as a large proprietary company; proprietary companies that are trustees of registrable superannuation entities must have one Corporations Act s 1317AI(2)–(3)
  3. The policy must explain the protections available, to whom and how protected disclosures can be made, how the company will support and protect whistleblowers, how disclosures will be investigated, how people mentioned in disclosures will be treated fairly, and how the policy is made available Corporations Act s 1317AI(5)
  4. Failing to have the policy is a strict liability offence Corporations Act s 1317AI(4)
The policy is current and accessiblePolicy; communications
OB-WB-02Protect whistleblowers: confidentiality of identity, protection from detriment, and anonymous disclosures
7 individual obligations
  1. Disclosures qualify for protection when an eligible whistleblower (including current and former officers, employees, suppliers and their relatives) has reasonable grounds to suspect misconduct or an improper state of affairs, and discloses to ASIC, APRA, an eligible recipient or a lawyer; disclosers need not identify themselves Corporations Act ss 1317AA, 1317AAA
  2. Eligible recipients include officers and senior managers, auditors, actuaries, people the entity authorises to receive disclosures and, for super funds, trustees and trustee directors Corporations Act s 1317AAC
  3. Public interest and emergency disclosures to journalists or parliamentarians are protected only in limited circumstances, such as after a prior disclosure to a regulator and (for public interest disclosures) at least 90 days and written notice Corporations Act s 1317AAD
  4. Disclosures about a personal work-related grievance are generally not protected, unless they concern victimisation or are made to a lawyer Corporations Act s 1317AADA
  5. Do not disclose the whistleblower’s identity, or information likely to identify them, except as the law allows (for example to ASIC, APRA, the AFP, a lawyer, or with consent) Corporations Act s 1317AAE
  6. Do not cause or threaten detriment to anyone because of a belief or suspicion that they made, or could make, a protected disclosure Corporations Act s 1317AC
  7. A protected discloser is not subject to civil, criminal or administrative liability for making the disclosure, and contractual remedies cannot be enforced against them for it Corporations Act s 1317AB
Protections operate in practiceCase records; training

Risk, resilience and cyber

Ransomware payment reporting

Department of Home Affairs (ASD for cyber support) Applies to: Businesses with annual turnover above $3 million, and responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

Notification deadlines

  • Within 72 hours: Ransomware payment made (Australian Government)
IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-RANSOM-01Report any ransomware payment within 72 hours
5 individual obligations
  1. The duty applies when a cyber security incident affects a reporting business entity, someone makes a demand to benefit from it, and the entity (or someone on its behalf) makes a payment or gives a benefit directly related to the demand Cyber Security Act s 26(1)
  2. Reporting business entities are businesses carrying on business in Australia with annual turnover for the previous financial year above $3 million (not Commonwealth or State bodies), and responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets covered by Part 2B of the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 Cyber Security Act s 26(2)–(3); Ransomware Payment Reporting Rules s 6
  3. Report to the designated Commonwealth body within 72 hours of making the payment or becoming aware it was made Cyber Security Act s 27(1)
  4. Include what the entity knows or can find out by reasonable enquiry about who paid, the incident and its impact, the demand, the payment, and communications with the extorting entity, in the approved form Cyber Security Act s 27(2), (4); Ransomware Payment Reporting Rules s 7
  5. Failing to report is a civil penalty provision (60 penalty units) Cyber Security Act s 27(5)
Ransomware payments are reported on timeIncident records

Financial crime

Sanctions

DFAT (Australian Sanctions Office) Applies to: Everyone. For companies, breaching sanctions is a strict liability offence; the defence is reasonable precautions and due diligence.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-SANCT-01Screen customers, owners, counterparties and payments against the Consolidated List (and other relevant lists)
5 individual obligations
  1. Do not directly or indirectly make an asset available to, or for the benefit of, a designated person or entity without a permit Autonomous Sanctions Regulations reg 14
  2. Do not use or deal with a controlled asset you hold, or allow or facilitate its use, without a permit Autonomous Sanctions Regulations reg 15
  3. Under UN sanctions, do not deal with freezable assets or make assets available to proscribed persons or entities, except as authorised Charter of the United Nations Act ss 20–21
  4. DFAT maintains the Consolidated List of designated persons, entities and assets, published on its website Autonomous Sanctions Regulations reg 22
  5. AML/CTF reporting entities must establish in initial customer due diligence whether the customer, beneficial owners and people acting for them are designated for targeted financial sanctions AML/CTF Act s 28(2)(e)(ii)
Screening covers every relevant party and paymentScreening configuration; testing
OB-SANCT-02Handle alerts carefully and keep evidence of reasonable precautions and due diligence
2 individual obligations
  1. For companies, breaching a sanction law is a strict liability offence; the defence is to prove reasonable precautions and due diligence to avoid the contravention Autonomous Sanctions Act s 16(5)–(8); Charter of the United Nations Act s 21(2C)–(2E)
  2. Penalties for companies reach the greater of 10,000 penalty units or three times the value of the transaction Autonomous Sanctions Act s 16(9)
Alerts are resolved and documentedAlert records
OB-SANCT-03A sanctions match may also require an AUSTRAC report
2 individual obligations
  1. If you hold an asset and form the opinion it is a controlled asset (or no longer is), tell the Australian Federal Police as soon as practicable, with what you know about it and why Autonomous Sanctions Regulations reg 24
  2. AML/CTF risk assessments and controls must cover proliferation financing, which includes breaches of sanctions addressing weapons of mass destruction AML/CTF Act s 5 (definition of proliferation financing), ss 26C, 26F
Sanctions and AML processes are connectedEscalation procedure

Anti-bribery and corruption

Commonwealth and state law Applies to: Companies, including the offence of failing to prevent foreign bribery by associates (since 8 September 2024).

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-ABC-01Have adequate procedures to prevent bribery by associates, including employees, agents and contractors
5 individual obligations
  1. A company commits an offence if an associate bribes a foreign public official (or does so overseas) for the company’s profit or gain, even if the associate is not convicted Criminal Code s 70.5A(1)–(3)
  2. The only defence is for the company to prove it had adequate procedures designed to prevent its associates from bribing foreign public officials Criminal Code s 70.5A(5)
  3. The Minister must publish guidance on the steps a company can take to prevent bribery by associates; it is guidance, not law Criminal Code s 70.5B
  4. Maximum fine is the greatest of 100,000 penalty units, three times the benefit obtained, or 10% of annual turnover (including related bodies corporate) Criminal Code ss 70.5A(6), 70.5C
  5. Providing, offering or promising a benefit to anyone, intending to improperly influence a foreign public official to obtain or keep business or an advantage, is itself an offence (the business or advantage need not actually be obtained) Criminal Code s 70.2(1)–(2)
Procedures are adequate and evidencedPolicy; training; risk assessment
OB-ABC-02Carry out due diligence on and monitor higher-risk third parties
1 individual obligation
  1. Associates include officers, employees, agents, contractors, subsidiaries, controlled entities and anyone else who performs services for or on behalf of the company, so third parties’ conduct can make the company liable Criminal Code s 70.1 (definition of associate)
Third parties are vetted and monitoredDue diligence records

Privacy and data

Privacy Act and notifiable data breaches

OAIC Applies to: Australian Government agencies and most businesses with annual turnover over $3 million, plus some smaller ones such as health service providers.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

Notification deadlines

  • Assess quickly, generally within 30 days: Suspected eligible data breach (OAIC)
  • Notify the OAIC and affected individuals as soon as practicable: Eligible data breach (OAIC)
IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-PRIV-01Handle personal information in line with the 13 Australian Privacy Principles across its whole life
6 individual obligations
  1. Most businesses with annual turnover of $3 million or less are not covered (small business operators), with exceptions such as health service providers Privacy Act s 6D
  2. APP entities must not breach the Australian Privacy Principles Privacy Act s 15
  3. Take reasonable steps to put in place practices, procedures and systems that ensure compliance with the APPs and allow privacy inquiries and complaints to be handled Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 1.2
  4. Have a clearly expressed, up-to-date privacy policy covering the kinds of information held, how it is collected and held, its purposes, access and correction, complaints, and likely overseas disclosures, and make it available free of charge Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 1.3–1.5
  5. Collect, notify, use, disclose, send overseas, keep accurate, and give access to and correct personal information in line with APPs 3 to 13 Privacy Act Sch 1, APPs 3–13
  6. A serious interference with privacy is a civil penalty provision; for a company the maximum is the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover in the breach period Privacy Act s 13G
Personal information is collected, used and disclosed lawfullyData inventory; privacy impact assessments
OB-PRIV-02Protect personal information from misuse, loss and unauthorised access (APP 11)
3 individual obligations
  1. Take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference and loss, and from unauthorised access, modification or disclosure Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 11.1
  2. Destroy or de-identify personal information that is no longer needed (unless the law requires it to be kept) Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 11.2
  3. Reasonable steps include technical and organisational measures Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 11.3
Security controls protect personal informationSecurity controls; testing
OB-PRIV-03Assess suspected eligible data breaches quickly (generally within 30 days) and notify the OAIC and affected people as soon as practicable
5 individual obligations
  1. An eligible data breach is unauthorised access to, disclosure or loss of personal information that a reasonable person would conclude is likely to result in serious harm to any of the individuals affected Privacy Act s 26WE(2)
  2. It is not an eligible data breach if remedial action taken before serious harm results means serious harm is no longer likely Privacy Act s 26WF
  3. Where there are reasonable grounds to suspect an eligible data breach, carry out a reasonable and expeditious assessment and take all reasonable steps to complete it within 30 days Privacy Act s 26WH
  4. Where there are reasonable grounds to believe there has been an eligible data breach, prepare a statement (entity, description, kinds of information, recommended steps for individuals) and give it to the Commissioner as soon as practicable Privacy Act s 26WK
  5. Notify the individuals affected or at risk (or, if that is not practicable, publish and publicise the statement) as soon as practicable after preparing it Privacy Act s 26WL
Suspected breaches are assessed and notified on timeBreach assessments; notifications
OB-PRIV-04From 10 December 2026, explain in the privacy policy when personal information is used in substantially automated decisionsAutomated decisions are identified and disclosedDecision inventory; privacy policy

Sustainability, reporting and sector rules

Climate risk and climate-related disclosures (AASB S2)

ASIC (and APRA guidance) Applies to: Entities above the size thresholds, phased: Group 1 from years starting 1 January 2025, Group 2 from 1 July 2026, Group 3 from 1 July 2027. Super funds and registered schemes with $5 billion or more in assets are in Group 2.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-CLIMATE-01Prepare an annual sustainability report with climate statements under AASB S2 (governance, strategy, risk management, metrics and targets)
4 individual obligations
  1. From financial years starting on or after 1 July 2027, entities that prepare a financial report and meet two of $50 million revenue, $25 million gross assets or 100 employees, or are NGER reporters, or are registered schemes, super funds or retail CCIVs with $5 billion or more in assets, prepare an annual sustainability report; larger entities started earlier under the transition rules Corporations Act ss 292A(1), (3), (5)–(6), 1707B
  2. The report contains the climate statements, notes, and a directors’ declaration on whether it complies with the Act, the sustainability standards and the climate disclosure requirements Corporations Act s 296A
  3. Comply with the sustainability standards and any further requirements the Minister sets Corporations Act s 296C
  4. Keep sustainability records, and have the sustainability report audited or reviewed Corporations Act ss 286A, 301A
Climate disclosures are complete and supportedSustainability report; data records
OB-CLIMATE-02Run climate scenario analysis and disclose greenhouse gas emissions
3 individual obligations
  1. Climate statements and notes disclose material climate-related financial risks and opportunities, climate metrics and targets (including scope 1, 2 and 3 greenhouse gas emissions, with financed emissions), and governance, strategy and risk management Corporations Act s 296D(1)
  2. Any scenario analysis must use at least two scenarios: one where warming well exceeds 2°C and one where it is limited to 1.5°C (the temperature goals referred to in the Climate Change Act 2022) Corporations Act s 296D(2A)–(2B)
  3. For the first three years, statements about scope 3 emissions, scenario analysis and transition plans have limited immunity from private actions, but not from ASIC or criminal proceedings Corporations Act s 1707D
Scenario analysis and emissions data are robustScenario analysis; emissions inventory
OB-CLIMATE-03Avoid misleading sustainability claims (greenwashing) in all communications
2 individual obligations
  1. ASIC can direct an entity to correct an incorrect, incomplete or misleading statement in a sustainability report Corporations Act s 296E
  2. Sustainability and green claims in any communication must not be misleading; statements about the future need reasonable grounds Corporations Act s 1041H; ASIC Act ss 12BB, 12DA
Sustainability claims are substantiatedReview records

Modern slavery reporting

Attorney-General’s Department Applies to: Entities with annual consolidated revenue of at least $100 million. A proposed offence was announced in July 2026; check the current position.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-MSA-01Publish an annual modern slavery statement addressing the seven mandatory criteria, approved by the board
6 individual obligations
  1. Reporting entities include entities based or operating in Australia with consolidated revenue of at least $100 million for the reporting period (others may volunteer) Modern Slavery Act ss 5–6
  2. Give the Minister a modern slavery statement for each reporting period (alone or in a joint statement), in the approved form, within 6 months after the period ends Modern Slavery Act ss 13(1), 13(2)(b) and (e), 14
  3. The statement is approved by the principal governing body and signed by a responsible member Modern Slavery Act s 13(2)(c)–(d)
  4. Address the mandatory criteria: identify the entity; describe its structure, operations and supply chains, the modern slavery risks (including in entities it owns or controls), the actions taken to assess and address them (including due diligence and remediation), how effectiveness is assessed, and the consultation process; and include any other relevant information Modern Slavery Act s 16(1)
  5. Include details of the governing body’s approval Modern Slavery Act s 16(2)
  6. If an entity fails to comply, the Minister may ask for an explanation or remedial action within 28 days or more, and may publish details if the request is not met Modern Slavery Act s 16A
The statement is complete and approvedStatement; supplier assessments

Listed company obligations (continuous disclosure, ASX Principles)

ASX and ASIC Applies to: ASX-listed entities.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-ASX-01Tell the market immediately about information that would materially affect the share price, subject to limited exceptions
4 individual obligations
  1. Once aware of information that a reasonable person would expect to have a material effect on the price or value of its securities, tell ASX immediately ASX Listing Rule 3.1
  2. The exception applies only while the information is confidential, a reasonable person would not expect it to be disclosed, and it falls in a listed category (breach of law, incomplete proposal or negotiation, insufficiently definite, internal management information, or a trade secret) ASX Listing Rule 3.1A
  3. If ASX asks for information to correct or prevent a false market, give it immediately ASX Listing Rule 3.1B
  4. The rule has statutory force: failing to notify price-sensitive information that is not generally available can breach the Corporations Act Corporations Act s 674(1)–(2)
Price-sensitive information is identified and disclosedDisclosure committee records
OB-ASX-02Report against the ASX Corporate Governance Principles on an if not, why not basis
1 individual obligation
  1. Benchmark governance practices against the ASX Corporate Governance Principles and Recommendations and, where not followed, disclose that fact and why ASX Listing Rule 4.10.3
Governance practices and explanations are accurateGovernance statement
OB-ASX-03Board oversight of risk, typically through a risk committee, and remuneration reporting
1 individual obligation
  1. Listed companies include a remuneration report in the directors’ report, covering board policy on key management personnel remuneration, its link to company performance, and any performance conditions Corporations Act s 300A(1)
Risk oversight is evidencedCommittee papers
