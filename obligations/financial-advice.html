Obligations for financial advice licensees

The 10 regimes that usually apply to financial advice licensees, grouped by theme, with 32 plain-English obligation summaries, control objectives, evidence and notification deadlines.

10 regimes 32 obligation summaries 5 notification deadlines Last reviewed: 9 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • This page lists the regimes that typically apply to financial advice licensees. Whether each one applies to a particular organisation depends on its licences, products, size and thresholds.
  • Each summary groups related obligations in plain English. It is a starting point for an obligations register, not a substitute for the law: always read the official source.
  • The IDs match the obligations library and the GRC model builder, so you can download the full set or build a linked model.

Governance and accountability

Conflicts of interest (s 912A(1)(aa), RG 181; SPS 521 for super)

ASIC and APRA Applies to: AFS licensees; super trustees also under SPS 521.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-COI-01Maintain adequate arrangements to manage conflicts, matched to the nature and risk of each conflict
5 individual obligations
  1. AFS licensees must have adequate arrangements to manage conflicts of interest arising in providing financial services (a civil penalty provision) Corporations Act s 912A(1)(aa), (5A); RG 181.48
  2. Take a proportionate, risk-based approach that reflects other legal requirements, the likelihood and seriousness of each conflict and the harm it could cause, and the nature, scale and complexity of the business RG 181.50–181.59
  3. Other duties may require putting clients’ or members’ interests first or stronger arrangements, and some conflicts are prohibited outright (such as conflicted remuneration) RG 181.54–181.55
  4. Have arrangements to identify, assess and respond to conflicts, and to check that responses stay effective RG 181.60; RG 181 Table 2, steps 1–3
  5. Manage conflicts through a combination of avoiding, controlling and disclosing them; disclosure alone is often not enough RG 181.64–181.65
Conflicts are identified and managedConflicts register; management plans
OB-COI-02Keep a conflicts register and tailored policy, train staff, and give the board oversight
3 individual obligations
  1. Implement, monitor, maintain and review the arrangements, with senior management (and, where appropriate, board) approval, staff training, accountable people, compliance monitoring and disciplinary measures RG 181.61; RG 181 Table 2, step 4
  2. Be able to show the arrangements are integrated into business operations; having a policy is not enough RG 181 Table 2, step 4
  3. Document identified conflicts and actions taken (for example in a conflicts register), reports to senior management, and disclosures given to affected parties RG 181.63
Policy, register and training are currentRegister; training records; board reports
OB-COI-03Super trustees: give priority to members’ interests and keep a board-approved conflicts management framework (SPS 521)
6 individual obligations
  1. Have a Board-approved conflicts management framework, proportionate to the business, so that all potential and actual conflicts are identified and avoided or prudently managed; the Board is ultimately responsible for it SPS 521 paras 8–10, 14
  2. The framework includes a Board-approved conflicts management policy, defined roles and resources, and up-to-date registers of relevant duties and relevant interests, with a process for deciding what is relevant SPS 521 paras 15–16
  3. The policy covers identifying and monitoring conflicts, avoiding them where required, managing them so that members’ duties and interests get priority (SIS Act ss 52(2)(d) and 52A(2)(d)), escalation, and minuting each conflict and the action taken SPS 521 para 18
  4. Regularly and thoroughly look for conflicts arising from relationships with existing or prospective service providers and advisers SPS 521 para 19
  5. Make sure responsible persons and staff understand conflicts and the framework, and that incoming responsible persons disclose their relevant duties and interests before appointment SPS 521 paras 11–12
  6. Review the framework every year and report to the Board, with an operationally independent comprehensive review at least every three years SPS 521 paras 20–23
Member interests are prioritised in conflicted decisionsFramework; decision records

Whistleblower protections

ASIC Applies to: Public companies, large proprietary companies and super trustee companies must have a whistleblower policy; the protections apply more widely.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-WB-01Have a whistleblower policy (public companies, large proprietary companies, super trustees)
4 individual obligations
  1. Public companies must have a whistleblower policy and make it available to officers and employees Corporations Act s 1317AI(1)
  2. Large proprietary companies must have one from 6 months after the end of their first financial year as a large proprietary company; proprietary companies that are trustees of registrable superannuation entities must have one Corporations Act s 1317AI(2)–(3)
  3. The policy must explain the protections available, to whom and how protected disclosures can be made, how the company will support and protect whistleblowers, how disclosures will be investigated, how people mentioned in disclosures will be treated fairly, and how the policy is made available Corporations Act s 1317AI(5)
  4. Failing to have the policy is a strict liability offence Corporations Act s 1317AI(4)
The policy is current and accessiblePolicy; communications
OB-WB-02Protect whistleblowers: confidentiality of identity, protection from detriment, and anonymous disclosures
7 individual obligations
  1. Disclosures qualify for protection when an eligible whistleblower (including current and former officers, employees, suppliers and their relatives) has reasonable grounds to suspect misconduct or an improper state of affairs, and discloses to ASIC, APRA, an eligible recipient or a lawyer; disclosers need not identify themselves Corporations Act ss 1317AA, 1317AAA
  2. Eligible recipients include officers and senior managers, auditors, actuaries, people the entity authorises to receive disclosures and, for super funds, trustees and trustee directors Corporations Act s 1317AAC
  3. Public interest and emergency disclosures to journalists or parliamentarians are protected only in limited circumstances, such as after a prior disclosure to a regulator and (for public interest disclosures) at least 90 days and written notice Corporations Act s 1317AAD
  4. Disclosures about a personal work-related grievance are generally not protected, unless they concern victimisation or are made to a lawyer Corporations Act s 1317AADA
  5. Do not disclose the whistleblower’s identity, or information likely to identify them, except as the law allows (for example to ASIC, APRA, the AFP, a lawyer, or with consent) Corporations Act s 1317AAE
  6. Do not cause or threaten detriment to anyone because of a belief or suspicion that they made, or could make, a protected disclosure Corporations Act s 1317AC
  7. A protected discloser is not subject to civil, criminal or administrative liability for making the disclosure, and contractual remedies cannot be enforced against them for it Corporations Act s 1317AB
Protections operate in practiceCase records; training

Risk, resilience and cyber

Ransomware payment reporting

Department of Home Affairs (ASD for cyber support) Applies to: Businesses with annual turnover above $3 million, and responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

Notification deadlines

  • Within 72 hours: Ransomware payment made (Australian Government)
IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-RANSOM-01Report any ransomware payment within 72 hours
5 individual obligations
  1. The duty applies when a cyber security incident affects a reporting business entity, someone makes a demand to benefit from it, and the entity (or someone on its behalf) makes a payment or gives a benefit directly related to the demand Cyber Security Act s 26(1)
  2. Reporting business entities are businesses carrying on business in Australia with annual turnover for the previous financial year above $3 million (not Commonwealth or State bodies), and responsible entities for critical infrastructure assets covered by Part 2B of the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018 Cyber Security Act s 26(2)–(3); Ransomware Payment Reporting Rules s 6
  3. Report to the designated Commonwealth body within 72 hours of making the payment or becoming aware it was made Cyber Security Act s 27(1)
  4. Include what the entity knows or can find out by reasonable enquiry about who paid, the incident and its impact, the demand, the payment, and communications with the extorting entity, in the approved form Cyber Security Act s 27(2), (4); Ransomware Payment Reporting Rules s 7
  5. Failing to report is a civil penalty provision (60 penalty units) Cyber Security Act s 27(5)
Ransomware payments are reported on timeIncident records

Licensing, conduct and disclosure

AFS licence general obligations (s 912A, RG 104, RG 105)

ASIC Applies to: Australian financial services licensees.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-AFSL-01Provide financial services efficiently, honestly and fairly
6 individual obligations
  1. Do all things necessary to ensure the financial services covered by the licence are provided efficiently, honestly and fairly Corporations Act s 912A(1)(a)
  2. Comply with the conditions on the licence and with the financial services laws Corporations Act s 912A(1)(b)–(c)
  3. Take reasonable steps to ensure representatives comply with the financial services laws Corporations Act s 912A(1)(ca)
  4. Comply with the Reference Checking and Information Sharing Protocol (for representatives who give personal advice to retail clients about relevant financial products) Corporations Act s 912A(1)(cc), (3A)
  5. If providing services to retail clients, have arrangements to compensate them for loss caused by breaches by the licensee or its representatives Corporations Act s 912B
  6. Contravening most of the general obligations is a civil penalty provision Corporations Act s 912A(5A)
Services meet the efficiently, honestly and fairly standardMonitoring results; complaints and breach trends
OB-AFSL-02Maintain adequate risk management systems and compliance arrangements
3 individual obligations
  1. Have adequate risk management systems (not required of APRA-regulated bodies, other than RSE licensees that are also responsible entities of registered schemes) Corporations Act s 912A(1)(h), (5)
  2. Have adequate arrangements for managing conflicts of interest Corporations Act s 912A(1)(aa)
  3. If providing services to retail clients, have a dispute resolution system (internal dispute resolution meeting ASIC’s standards, and AFCA membership) and give ASIC the IDR information it specifies Corporations Act s 912A(1)(g), (2), (2A)
Risk and compliance systems are adequate for the businessFrameworks; monitoring plans
OB-AFSL-03Maintain organisational competence through responsible managers with appropriate knowledge and skills (RG 105)
1 individual obligation
  1. Maintain the competence to provide the financial services covered by the licence Corporations Act s 912A(1)(e)
Responsible managers are nominated, competent and currentResponsible manager records
OB-AFSL-04Have adequate resources and ensure representatives are trained and competent
2 individual obligations
  1. Have adequate financial, technological and human resources to provide the financial services and carry out supervisory arrangements (not required of APRA-regulated bodies, other than RSE licensees that are also responsible entities of registered schemes) Corporations Act s 912A(1)(d), (4)
  2. Ensure representatives are adequately trained (including meeting continuing professional development requirements) and competent to provide the financial services Corporations Act s 912A(1)(f)
Training and competence are trackedTraining records

Financial advice obligations

ASIC Applies to: Licensees and advisers giving personal advice to retail clients.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-ADVICE-01Advisers act in the client’s best interests, give appropriate advice and prioritise the client’s interests
5 individual obligations
  1. Act in the best interests of the client; the steps that show this include identifying the client’s objectives, financial situation and needs and the subject of the advice, making reasonable inquiries where information is incomplete or inaccurate, and declining to advise without the needed expertise Corporations Act s 961B
  2. Give the advice only if it would be reasonable to conclude it is appropriate to the client Corporations Act s 961G
  3. Warn the client, when the advice is given, if it is or may be based on incomplete or inaccurate information about their circumstances Corporations Act s 961H
  4. Give priority to the client’s interests where there is a conflict with the interests of the adviser, the licensee or their associates Corporations Act s 961J
  5. The licensee takes reasonable steps to ensure its representatives comply with these duties Corporations Act s 961L
Advice meets the best interests dutyAdvice file reviews
OB-ADVICE-02No conflicted remuneration, apart from limited exceptions
5 individual obligations
  1. Conflicted remuneration is any benefit (other than from the client) that could reasonably be expected to influence the products recommended or the advice given to retail clients Corporations Act s 963A
  2. Licensees must not accept conflicted remuneration and must take reasonable steps so their representatives don’t Corporations Act ss 963E–963F
  3. Authorised and other representatives must not accept it; employers, product issuers and sellers must not give it Corporations Act ss 963G–963K
  4. Volume-based benefits are presumed to be conflicted remuneration unless shown otherwise Corporations Act s 963L
  5. Exceptions are limited, such as some benefits for general insurance and (subject to conditions) life risk insurance products Corporations Act s 963B
Remuneration arrangements are reviewed for conflictsRemuneration reviews
OB-ADVICE-03Renew ongoing fee arrangements annually, with client consent for deductions
5 individual obligations
  1. Get the client’s signed, dated written consent before entering into or renewing an ongoing fee arrangement, after telling them in writing the services, fees, frequency, consent period and their right to end it Corporations Act s 962G
  2. Consent lapses unless renewed in the window from 60 days before to 150 days after each anniversary (or an earlier date set in the consent), and the arrangement then ends Corporations Act ss 962F, 962H
  3. The client may end an ongoing fee arrangement at any time Corporations Act s 962J
  4. Do not deduct, or arrange or accept deduction of, ongoing fees from a client’s account without their written consent Corporations Act ss 962R–962S
  5. Keep records that show compliance with the ongoing fee arrangement rules Corporations Act s 962X
No fees are deducted without current consentConsent records
OB-ADVICE-04Advisers are registered and meet professional standards; the licensee supervises and monitors them
3 individual obligations
  1. Relevant providers must not give personal advice to retail clients on relevant financial products unless registered with ASIC, and licensees must not keep authorising unregistered providers Corporations Act ss 921Y–921Z
  2. Relevant providers must meet the education and training standards (qualification, exam, and work and training) and comply with the Code of Ethics Corporations Act ss 921BA, 921E(3)
  3. Licensees notify ASIC when a person becomes a relevant provider, and of changes to their details, within 30 business days Corporations Act ss 922D, 922H, 922L(2)
Adviser records and supervision are currentRegistration checks; supervision records

Disclosure and misleading conduct (Corporations Act, ASIC Act)

ASIC Applies to: Financial services providers dealing with retail clients; the misleading conduct rules apply to everyone.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-DISC-01Give retail clients the right documents at the right points (FSG, SOA, PDS) and keep ongoing disclosure current
4 individual obligations
  1. Give a Financial Services Guide to a retail client when providing a financial service (a civil penalty provision) Corporations Act s 941A
  2. Give a Statement of Advice when providing personal advice to a retail client, unless an exception applies Corporations Act s 946A
  3. Give a Product Disclosure Statement when personal advice recommends a particular product, and in the situations where a product is offered or issued to a retail client Corporations Act ss 1012A–1012B
  4. Keep the PDS up to date and notify holders of material changes and significant events Corporations Act ss 1012J, 1017B
Required documents are given on timeDocument delivery records
OB-DISC-02Do not make misleading or deceptive statements in any communication, including sustainability claims
4 individual obligations
  1. Do not engage in conduct in relation to a financial product or service that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive Corporations Act s 1041H; ASIC Act s 12DA
  2. Do not make false or misleading representations about financial services, including their standard, quality or value, or testimonials ASIC Act s 12DB
  3. A statement about a future matter (such as a forecast or a net zero target) is taken to be misleading unless there were reasonable grounds for making it, and the maker must bring evidence of those grounds ASIC Act s 12BB
  4. Do not engage in conduct liable to mislead the public about the nature, characteristics or suitability of financial services (a strict liability offence) ASIC Act s 12DF
Every claim is substantiated before publicationReview records; evidence files

Internal dispute resolution (RG 271)

ASIC Applies to: AFS and credit licensees and others required to have IDR.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

Notification deadlines

  • 30 calendar days (generally); 45 calendar days for most super complaints: Complaint response (IDR) (Customer, with AFCA rights)
IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-RG271-01Accept complaints in any form, including on social media, without requiring them in writing
6 individual obligations
  1. Deal with every expression of dissatisfaction that meets the complaint definition (adopted from AS/NZS 10002:2014) through the IDR process RG 271.27–271.28
  2. Posts on the firm’s own social media channels that meet the definition are complaints, where the author is identifiable and contactable RG 271.32
  3. The IDR process must, at a minimum, deal with complaints by retail clients (including small businesses with fewer than 100 employees) and, for super funds, by members and beneficiaries eligible to go to AFCA RG 271.36–271.38; RG 271.41–271.42
  4. The process must be free, easy to understand and use (including for people with disability or language difficulties), and supported by a public complaints policy and an internal procedure RG 271.134; RG 271.141; RG 271.172
  5. Record all complaints in an effective system that tracks the progress of each one RG 271.179
  6. Firms that outsource IDR must choose providers with care, monitor them, and deal with their failures RG 271.48
Every complaint is capturedComplaints system
OB-RG271-02Resolve complaints within maximum timeframes: generally 30 calendar days, 45 days for most super complaints
7 individual obligations
  1. Give an IDR response no later than 30 calendar days after receiving a standard complaint RG 271.56; Table 2
  2. Superannuation trustee complaints (other than about death benefit distributions) and traditional trustee complaints: no later than 45 calendar days RG 271.58–271.59; Table 2; RG 271.76–271.78
  3. Death benefit distribution complaints: no later than 90 calendar days after the 28-day objection period ends; credit complaints about default notices or hardship: no later than 21 calendar days Table 2; RG 271.80–271.93
  4. Complaints about insurance in super: trustees, insurers and administrators must meet the timeframe wherever the complaint is first lodged RG 271.79
  5. A firm may exceed the timeframe only if the complaint is particularly complex or delayed by circumstances beyond its control, and it sends an IDR delay notification (reasons, AFCA rights and contact details) before the timeframe ends RG 271.64–271.66
  6. If a customer advocate review is offered, it must not be a mandatory step before AFCA, and the total time must stay within the maximum timeframe RG 271.109–271.110
  7. Resource and staff the IDR function to resolve complaints fairly within the timeframes, including during spikes, and give staff the authority to resolve complaints RG 271.142–271.143; RG 271.146–271.147
Complaints are resolved within timeframesTimeliness reports
OB-RG271-03Give a written IDR response explaining the outcome and the right to go to AFCA
3 individual obligations
  1. The IDR response is in writing and gives the final outcome (with reasons for any rejection), the right to go to AFCA and AFCA’s contact details RG 271.53–271.54
  2. No IDR response is needed if the complaint is resolved, or an explanation or apology given, by the end of the fifth business day, unless the complainant asks for one or it is about hardship, a declined claim, a claim’s value, or a super trustee decision RG 271.71; RG 271.75
  3. Implement complaint outcomes (refunds, fee waivers, corrections, compensation) in a timely manner RG 271.165
IDR responses are completeResponse samples
OB-RG271-04Identify and escalate systemic issues; report complaints data to the board and to ASIC
3 individual obligations
  1. The board sets clear accountabilities for complaints handling, including managing systemic issues identified through complaints RG 271.118
  2. Reports to the board or executive committees include complaints metrics and analysis, including systemic issues RG 271.119; RG 271.183
  3. Enable staff to escalate possible systemic issues, analyse complaints data regularly, escalate promptly for investigation, and report on outcomes RG 271.120
Systemic issues are identified and reportedBoard reports; ASIC data lodgements

Breaches and remediation

Breach reporting (reportable situations, RG 78)

ASIC Applies to: AFS and credit licensees.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

Notification deadlines

  • Within 30 calendar days of awareness: Reportable situation (significant breach or likely breach of a core obligation) (ASIC)
IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-RG78-01Report reportable situations (mainly significant breaches or likely breaches of core obligations) within 30 calendar days
7 individual obligations
  1. A reportable situation is a significant breach, or likely significant breach, of a core obligation; an investigation into whether there is one that runs for more than 30 days (60 days under ASIC relief), and its outcome if it finds there is none; or gross negligence or serious fraud in providing a financial service Corporations Act s 912D(1)–(2); National Credit Act s 50A
  2. Core obligations include the general obligations in s 912A (the duty to comply with financial services laws only for specified laws) and s 912B, and representatives’ obligations under specified financial services laws Corporations Act s 912D(3); National Credit Act s 50A(3)
  3. Where there are reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen, lodge a report with ASIC in the prescribed form Corporations Act s 912DAA(1)–(2)
  4. Lodge the report within 30 calendar days after first knowing, or being reckless about whether, there are reasonable grounds to believe the reportable situation has arisen Corporations Act s 912DAA(3); National Credit Act s 50B; RG 78.83
  5. A further reportable situation with the same or substantially similar underlying circumstances as one already reported may be reported within 90 days (ASIC relief) ASIC Instrument 2024/620 s 7; RG 78.84
  6. Failing to report is a strict liability offence and a civil penalty provision Corporations Act s 912DAA(1) note, (4) and (7)
  7. For APRA-regulated licensees, a report to APRA with all the required information counts as lodged with ASIC Corporations Act s 912DAA(5)
Reportable situations are assessed and reported on timeBreach register; ASIC lodgements
OB-RG78-02Start the clock from awareness (or recklessness), not from the end of the investigation
3 individual obligations
  1. The reporting clock starts when the licensee first knows, or is reckless about whether, there are reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen (knowledge and recklessness have their Criminal Code meanings) Corporations Act s 912DAA(3) and (8); RG 78.88–78.92
  2. Do not wait for board consideration, legal advice, rectification or (for a likely breach) the breach to happen if that would take the report past the deadline RG 78.98
  3. Investigations are reportable only once they have run for more than 60 days (ASIC relief; the Act says 30), with the report due within 30 days after that Corporations Act s 912D(1)(c); RG 78.102
The awareness date is recorded for every potential breachIncident log with awareness dates
OB-RG78-03Apply deemed significance and the significance factors consistently
4 individual obligations
  1. A breach is deemed significant if it is an offence punishable by imprisonment of 3 months or more (dishonesty) or 12 months or more (other offences), a civil penalty contravention (unless excluded by regulation), misleading or deceptive conduct, or causes or is likely to cause material loss or damage to clients or members Corporations Act s 912D(4); National Credit Act s 50A(4)
  2. Some breaches are excluded from deemed significance, including certain civil penalty provisions specified in regulations and, under ASIC relief, single breaches of RG 271’s enforceable paragraphs and some minor misleading conduct affecting one person Corporations Regulations reg 7.6.02A(2); ASIC Instrument 2024/620 ss 5–6; RG 78.37–78.38
  3. Otherwise, decide significance having regard to the number or frequency of similar breaches, the impact on the ability to provide licensed services, and the extent to which the breach shows compliance arrangements are inadequate Corporations Act s 912D(5); National Credit Act s 50A(5)
  4. Consider deemed significance first, and only then the significance factors RG 78.31–78.32
Significance assessments are consistent and documentedSignificance assessments
OB-RG78-04Licensees giving personal advice: notify, investigate and remediate affected clients; report some misconduct by other licensees’ advisers
6 individual obligations
  1. Report to ASIC significant breaches, gross negligence or serious fraud by individual advisers of other licensees who give personal advice to retail clients on relevant financial products, within 30 days, and give the other licensee a copy (unless ASIC is already aware) Corporations Act s 912DAB(1)–(5)
  2. Take reasonable steps to notify affected retail clients in writing within 30 days where they may have suffered loss they can recover from the licensee Corporations Act s 912EA(1)–(2)
  3. Start an investigation within 30 days, identify the conduct and quantify the loss, and complete it as soon as reasonably practicable Corporations Act s 912EB(1)–(4)
  4. Tell the affected client the outcome in writing within 10 days of completing the investigation Corporations Act s 912EB(5)
  5. Take reasonable steps to pay the client’s loss or damage within 30 days of completing the investigation Corporations Act s 912EB(8)
  6. Keep records that show compliance with these client notification and remediation obligations Corporations Act s 912EC
Advice-related breaches are notified, investigated and remediatedClient notifications; remediation records

Consumer remediation (RG 277)

ASIC Applies to: AFS and credit licensees remediating consumer loss.

OB-RG277-01Start remediation promptly and proactively once conduct may have caused consumer loss
5 individual obligations
  1. Have adequate systems and processes to identify misconduct or other failures when they occur, and to protect and compensate consumers for their loss RG 277.3
  2. Initiate a remediation when misconduct or other failure in providing financial services or credit activities has caused, or may have caused, consumer loss, including failures by representatives, service providers and consultants RG 277.22–277.23
  3. Misconduct or other failure includes breaches of financial services or credit laws, contractual failings, negligence or fraud, and failures to meet other regulatory requirements RG 277.25
  4. Start promptly on becoming aware, rather than waiting for complaints, AFCA, ASIC or court proceedings RG 277.24; RG 277 Table 1, principle 6

OB-RG277-02Include everyone who has or may have been affected, automatically where possible, at no cost to them
2 individual obligations
  1. Aim to identify everyone who has or may have suffered loss, by understanding the nature, extent and impact of the failure, including its root cause and when it first caused loss RG 277 Table 1, principle 2; RG 277.45–277.53
  2. Include affected consumers automatically (no opt-in), don’t exclude them for not responding or lacking evidence of loss, and keep the remediation free for consumers RG 277 Table 1, principle 7; RG 277.150–277.152

OB-RG277-03Use beneficial assumptions where information is missing, and restore consumers’ position including the time value of money
4 individual obligations
  1. Aim to return consumers as closely as possible to the position they would have been in had the failure not occurred RG 277 Table 1, principle 1; RG 277.61–277.63
  2. Account for foregone returns or interest, consider indirect financial loss, and don’t discount compensation for unrealisable benefits RG 277.70–277.74
  3. Use assumptions only if they benefit consumers and are evidence based and documented RG 277 Table 1, principle 3; RG 277.113–277.141
  4. Document and justify key decisions about scope and remedies, and keep records ASIC may ask to see RG 277 Table 1, principle 4

OB-RG277-04Handle small amounts and money that can’t be returned through the residual payment options
4 individual obligations
  1. Use reasonable endeavours to pay consumers, prioritising methods that need no action from them (such as EFT or PayID) RG 277 Table 1, principle 5; RG 277.153–277.171
  2. Pay consumers with current payment details regardless of amount; for former customers owed $5 or less (after interest) with no current payment details, the amount may go to a residual remediation payment instead (super trustees and scheme operators using fund assets may keep such amounts in the fund for its members) RG 277.160–277.163
  3. Do not profit from the failure: money that can’t be returned despite reasonable endeavours goes to an unclaimed money regime or a residual payment to an ACNC-registered charity RG 277 Table 1, principle 8; RG 277.188–277.197

Privacy and data

Privacy Act and notifiable data breaches

OAIC Applies to: Australian Government agencies and most businesses with annual turnover over $3 million, plus some smaller ones such as health service providers.

Read our guide · See it in the obligations library

Notification deadlines

  • Assess quickly, generally within 30 days: Suspected eligible data breach (OAIC)
  • Notify the OAIC and affected individuals as soon as practicable: Eligible data breach (OAIC)
IDObligation (summary)Control objectiveEvidence
OB-PRIV-01Handle personal information in line with the 13 Australian Privacy Principles across its whole life
6 individual obligations
  1. Most businesses with annual turnover of $3 million or less are not covered (small business operators), with exceptions such as health service providers Privacy Act s 6D
  2. APP entities must not breach the Australian Privacy Principles Privacy Act s 15
  3. Take reasonable steps to put in place practices, procedures and systems that ensure compliance with the APPs and allow privacy inquiries and complaints to be handled Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 1.2
  4. Have a clearly expressed, up-to-date privacy policy covering the kinds of information held, how it is collected and held, its purposes, access and correction, complaints, and likely overseas disclosures, and make it available free of charge Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 1.3–1.5
  5. Collect, notify, use, disclose, send overseas, keep accurate, and give access to and correct personal information in line with APPs 3 to 13 Privacy Act Sch 1, APPs 3–13
  6. A serious interference with privacy is a civil penalty provision; for a company the maximum is the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover in the breach period Privacy Act s 13G
Personal information is collected, used and disclosed lawfullyData inventory; privacy impact assessments
OB-PRIV-02Protect personal information from misuse, loss and unauthorised access (APP 11)
3 individual obligations
  1. Take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference and loss, and from unauthorised access, modification or disclosure Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 11.1
  2. Destroy or de-identify personal information that is no longer needed (unless the law requires it to be kept) Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 11.2
  3. Reasonable steps include technical and organisational measures Privacy Act Sch 1, APP 11.3
Security controls protect personal informationSecurity controls; testing
OB-PRIV-03Assess suspected eligible data breaches quickly (generally within 30 days) and notify the OAIC and affected people as soon as practicable
5 individual obligations
  1. An eligible data breach is unauthorised access to, disclosure or loss of personal information that a reasonable person would conclude is likely to result in serious harm to any of the individuals affected Privacy Act s 26WE(2)
  2. It is not an eligible data breach if remedial action taken before serious harm results means serious harm is no longer likely Privacy Act s 26WF
  3. Where there are reasonable grounds to suspect an eligible data breach, carry out a reasonable and expeditious assessment and take all reasonable steps to complete it within 30 days Privacy Act s 26WH
  4. Where there are reasonable grounds to believe there has been an eligible data breach, prepare a statement (entity, description, kinds of information, recommended steps for individuals) and give it to the Commissioner as soon as practicable Privacy Act s 26WK
  5. Notify the individuals affected or at risk (or, if that is not practicable, publish and publicise the statement) as soon as practicable after preparing it Privacy Act s 26WL
