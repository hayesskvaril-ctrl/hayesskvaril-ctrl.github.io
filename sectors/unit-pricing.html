Unit pricing and unit pricing errors

How super fund unit prices are calculated, how errors arise, why they move money unfairly between members, and how trustees detect, assess and fix them, with an error calculator.

Advanced About 26 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • A unit price is the option's net asset value (assets less liabilities, including fees and tax provisions) divided by units on issue.
  • Errors come from asset prices, unlisted valuations, tax provisions, fee accruals, transaction processing and corporate actions.
  • An overstated price disadvantages members investing and those who stay; an understated price disadvantages members leaving.
  • ASIC RG 94 recognises a 0.30% materiality threshold that has commonly been used. Whether relying on it fits the best financial interests duty and RG 277 is a live question.
  • Correct every affected transaction, restore the option, decide who bears the cost, and fix the control that failed.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain how a unit price is calculated and where errors enter the calculation.
  2. Calculate who gains and who loses from a mispriced unit, for joining, leaving and remaining members.
  3. Use research on stale pricing and the 'wild card option' to explain why pricing errors transfer value between investors.
  4. Critically evaluate the use of materiality thresholds against trustees' duties.

The idea in plain English

In most super funds, each investment option is divided into units. Members' balances are the number of units they hold multiplied by the unit price, which is recalculated regularly (usually daily). Every contribution, switch, rollover and benefit payment is processed at a unit price. If the price is wrong, even by a fraction of a percent, every member who transacted at that price is affected, and money moves unfairly between members who come, go and stay.

Unit pricing sits at the centre of fee governance, valuation and fairness between members. This page explains how prices are built, where errors come from, and how funds detect, assess and fix them. It builds on superannuation trustee governance and RG 97 fees and costs.

A useful daily detective control compares each option's price movement with the movement expected from its benchmark or underlying assets, and investigates differences above a tolerance before prices are released.

Who gains and who loses when a price is wrong

Try it: the effect of a pricing error

An overstated price hurts people coming in (they get fewer units) and benefits people leaving (they get paid too much), with the cost falling on members who stay. An understated price does the reverse. Correcting an error therefore means looking at every transaction in the error period, not just the headline percentage.

Materiality thresholds: a live question

ASIC and APRA's joint guide to unit pricing (ASIC RG 94, first published in 2008 and administratively updated in April 2026) recognises that a materiality threshold of 0.30% of the unit price has commonly been used to decide whether to correct past transactions and compensate, and that a small fixed-dollar minimum (up to $20) may be set before compensating exited members.

Many trustees now ask whether relying on those thresholds is consistent with their duty to act in members' best financial interests and with ASIC's later remediation guidance in RG 277, which expects people to be returned to the position they would have been in. An error below 0.30% on a large option can still add up to a substantial amount across many members. Whatever threshold a trustee uses, it should be a documented, board-approved policy, applied consistently, and reconsidered when errors are systematic rather than one-off. This is an area of judgement where practice is evolving.

Handling an error: the steps

  1. Detect and contain: stop further transactions at the wrong price if possible, and log the incident with the awareness date.
  2. Quantify: the correct price for every affected day, the error percentage and the affected transactions and members.
  3. Assess: apply the fund's error policy, and assess breach reporting obligations (ASIC and APRA).
  4. Correct and compensate: adjust current members' accounts, pay or credit exited members, and restore the option (and so remaining members) where others were overpaid.
  5. Decide who bears the cost: trustee resources, the ORFR where appropriate, or recovery from a service provider, not the remaining members.
  6. Fix the cause: strengthen the control that failed; see root cause analysis.

Worked example: quantifying an error

This is an illustrative example for learning only; the figures are made up.

An option has 100 million units on issue. For five days the price used was $2.0040 when the correct price was $2.0000: overstated by 0.20%, below the commonly used 0.30% threshold. During those days, members invested $10 million and other members redeemed 2 million units.

GroupWhat happenedEffect
Joining membersReceived 4,990,020 units ($10m ÷ $2.0040) instead of 5,000,000 ($10m ÷ $2.0000).Short 9,980 units, worth $19,960 at the correct price.
Leaving membersPaid $4,008,000 (2m × $2.0040) instead of $4,000,000.Overpaid $8,000, at the expense of the option.
Remaining membersGained the joiners' shortfall and lost the leavers' overpayment.Net gain of $11,960, which isn't theirs.

A full correction would credit the joining members with the missing units, and restore the $8,000 overpaid to leaving members to the option from a source other than the remaining members (for example the trustee or a responsible service provider), because overpayments to exited members are often impractical to recover. Real errors are more complicated: many days, many transaction types, switches between options and members who have since left. A fund applying a 0.30% threshold might not correct this error at all, which is why the threshold question matters.

Governing unit pricing

RG 94 sets out good practice principles for product providers. In our own words, they come down to:

  • A documented unit pricing policy, approved by the board, covering methods, frequency, roles, tolerances and how errors are handled.
  • Board and senior management oversight, including regular reporting on pricing incidents and near misses.
  • Sound valuation, tax and fee accrual methods, applied consistently and reviewed periodically.
  • Strong controls over outsourced pricing. Many funds rely on administrators and custodians to calculate prices. The trustee stays accountable, and under CPS 230 these providers may be material service providers and unit pricing may be a critical operation.
  • An error policy that sets out how errors are detected, quantified, corrected and compensated, applied consistently.
  • Periodic independent review of the unit pricing framework.

Unit pricing errors also raise breach reporting questions. An error that causes members material loss may be a reportable situation to ASIC (see RG 78), and, for super, may need reporting to APRA. ASIC's 2026 penalty against Mercer Super for failing to report investigations into member services issues shows the cost of getting reporting wrong (see Superannuation trustee governance).

Common mistakes

  • Treating the 0.30% threshold as a safe harbour rather than a policy choice that has to fit members' best financial interests.
  • Looking only at the error percentage, not the total dollars and the number of members affected.
  • Missing related errors: the same faulty input affecting several options or several periods.
  • Letting remaining members bear the cost of overpayments to members who left.
  • Weak oversight of the administrator's pricing, relying on reports without testing.
  • Fixing the price but not the control, so the same error recurs.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. How many pricing errors and near misses did we have this year, and what were the causes?
  2. What threshold do we use for correcting errors, and why is it consistent with members' best financial interests?
  3. Who bears the cost of correcting an error, and has that ever fallen on remaining members?
  4. How do we oversee and test the unit prices our administrator or custodian calculates?
  5. When was our unit pricing framework last independently reviewed?

Frequently asked questions

Is RG 94 binding?

No. It is a joint ASIC and APRA guide to good practice. The binding obligations come from the law and standards it sits under, such as the best financial interests duty, SPS 530 and, for managed funds, the scheme constitution and the Corporations Act.

Do members who have left get compensated?

Generally yes, if they were disadvantaged. RG 94 recognises that a provider may choose not to pay exited members amounts below a small minimum (up to $20), because the cost of paying can exceed the amount.

Are unit prices the same as the performance shown in reports?

Reported returns are usually calculated from unit prices, so a pricing error can also affect reported performance, the performance test and fee calculations.

Theory and research

Prices set by an intermediary

Most investors in a pooled fund don't trade with each other; they trade with the fund at a price the fund sets. Chalmers et al. (2001) showed the danger in this arrangement. When the price is calculated from stale underlying prices, for example overseas shares that last traded hours earlier, it becomes predictable. Anyone who can see that the "true" value has moved holds what the authors called a wild card option: the right to buy or sell at yesterday's value. Every such trade is a transfer from the fund's other investors.

Zitzewitz (2003) estimated how large these transfers could be in US mutual funds, with arbitrage profits of 35 to 70% a year available in international funds, all at the expense of long-term shareholders. Zitzewitz later gave evidence to a US congressional committee during the 2003 mutual fund trading scandal, and fair value pricing became a standard defence. For super, the same logic applies to any option whose price lags reality, including options with unlisted assets valued infrequently, where smoothed values understate volatility (Getmansky et al., 2004).

Why errors create winners and losers

A pricing error is not just an accounting problem; it moves money between members. If the price is too high, members joining or switching in get too few units and members leaving are overpaid, and the cost falls on members who remain. The research on first-mover advantage (Chen et al., 2010) adds that when members can predict a price correction, those who act first benefit most. This is why the Who gains and who loses section above, and the calculator, focus on the direction of each transfer, not only its size.

Critical perspectives

  • Threshold or principle? A fixed materiality threshold is efficient and predictable, but it means small errors are never corrected even when they affect many members. The best financial interests duty is framed around members, not error sizes, which is why the use of thresholds is a live question.
  • The research is about arbitrage, not errors. The stale pricing studies concern predictable prices exploited by traders. Unit pricing errors in super are usually operational mistakes. The value transfer mechanism is the same, but the behavioural response differs.
  • Precision has costs. Daily fair value adjustments and frequent revaluations reduce transfers but increase cost and model risk. Who should bear that cost, all members or only those trading, is a policy choice.
  • Limited Australian evidence. We found little published research on the frequency or size of unit pricing errors in Australian super. Most public information comes from regulator reports and enforcement outcomes.

Seminar questions

  1. Using the calculator, show how a 0.2% overstatement for one week affects joining, leaving and remaining members. Who should be compensated, and who should pay?
  2. Explain the mutual fund "wild card option" in your own words. Could a similar option exist for super members switching between options? What conditions would create it?
  3. Is a 0.30% materiality threshold consistent with the best financial interests duty? Argue both sides.
  4. How should a trustee balance the cost of more frequent unlisted asset valuations against the fairness benefit to members?

Further reading

    Shows that fund prices set with stale underlying prices are predictable, giving traders a 'wild card option' to buy or sell at yesterday's value at the expense of other investors.
  • Zitzewitz, E. (2003). Who cares about shareholders? Arbitrage-proofing mutual funds. The Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization, 19(2), 245–280. https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/ewg011 Peer-reviewed
    Estimates that trading on stale fund prices could earn 35–70% a year in international funds, at the expense of long-term investors, and discusses fair value pricing and other fixes.
  • Getmansky, M., Lo, A. W., & Makarov, I. (2004). An econometric model of serial correlation and illiquidity in hedge fund returns. Journal of Financial Economics, 74(3), 529–609. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.04.001 Peer-reviewed
    Shows that smooth, serially correlated returns in funds holding illiquid assets mostly reflect stale or smoothed valuations, which understate true volatility and overstate risk-adjusted performance.
