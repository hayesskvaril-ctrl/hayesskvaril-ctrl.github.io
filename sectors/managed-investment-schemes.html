Managed investment schemes

Managed funds pool investors' money and have it managed by someone else. For retail investors, the law places a single responsible entity in charge and surrounds it with compliance checks.

Intermediate About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources
Main law
Corporations Act 2001, Chapter 5C (managed investment schemes) and Chapter 7 (financial services).
Regulator
ASIC. Managed investment schemes are not APRA-regulated.
Examples
Unit trusts such as share funds, property funds, mortgage and private credit funds, and many exchange-traded funds.

Key takeaways

  • A scheme generally must be registered with ASIC if it is offered to retail investors and has more than 20 members, or is promoted by a professional promoter.
  • A registered scheme is run by a responsible entity (RE): a public company with an AFS licence that acts as trustee and manager and owes duties directly to members.
  • The RE must put members' interests first where they conflict with its own.
  • Four checks surround the RE: the scheme's constitution, a compliance plan, a compliance committee (unless most directors are external) and an annual compliance plan audit.
  • Private credit funds and the Shield and First Guardian collapses have put fund governance, conflicts and research ratings under intense scrutiny.

How a registered scheme is structured

The responsible entity's duties

The Corporations Act (s 601FC) gives the RE statutory duties, and its officers have matching duties (s 601FD). In plain English, the RE must:

  • act honestly and use the care and diligence of a reasonable person in its position
  • act in members' best interests and, if there is a conflict, give priority to members' interests
  • treat members who hold interests of the same class equally, and members of different classes fairly
  • not improperly use its position or information to gain an advantage or cause detriment to members
  • keep scheme property clearly identified and separate from its own property and other schemes' property, and value it regularly
  • comply with the constitution and the compliance plan, and report breaches to ASIC.

Payments to the RE are limited to fees and indemnities permitted by the constitution. Related-party benefits out of scheme property are restricted, with member approval required unless an exception applies.

The four checks

CheckWhat it doesKey ASIC guidance
ConstitutionLegally binding rules, including the RE's powers, fees, how units are issued and redeemed, and complaints handling.RG 134
Compliance planSets out the measures the RE applies to make sure it complies with the Act and the constitution. It is lodged with ASIC.RG 132 (updated June 2025)
Compliance committeeNeeded if fewer than half the RE's directors are external. It has at least three members, a majority of them external. It monitors compliance with the plan, reports breaches to the RE and, if not addressed, to ASIC.RG 132
Compliance plan auditA registered auditor audits compliance with the plan every year. The scheme's financial statements are audited separately.RG 132

REs must also maintain adequate risk management systems (RG 259), meet financial requirements such as net tangible assets and liquidity (RG 166), and follow custody standards (RG 133). The compliance plan and risk management framework should work together. See Designing a compliance program.

Liquidity and valuation

A scheme is "liquid" under the Act only if liquid assets make up at least 80% of its property. Illiquid schemes can only offer withdrawals through a formal withdrawal offer. Funds holding property, private credit or other hard-to-value assets need:

  • strong valuation policies, independence and frequency
  • liquidity stress testing
  • redemption terms that match the assets, so early leavers don't gain at the expense of those who stay.

Current focus areas

Private credit

ASIC's 2025 surveillance of 28 private credit funds (REP 820) found poorer practices in four areas:

  • conflicts of interest
  • fees and remuneration, including opaque interest margins
  • portfolio transparency and valuation
  • inconsistent terminology.

ASIC said it would continue this work in 2026.

Shield and First Guardian

The collapse of the Shield Master Fund and First Guardian Master Fund affected thousands of Australians who had been advised to move their super into them. ASIC made enforcement over the collapses a top priority for 2026. It has brought actions against several parties across the chain, including advice licensees, a research house and a super trustee. In 2026 the Federal Court declared that two platform trustees, Macquarie and Netwealth, had contravened the Corporations Act, and Macquarie compensated affected members of its platform. Many other matters are still before the courts, so allegations are unproven unless admitted or decided. See the Shield and First Guardian case study.

For risk and compliance professionals, the lessons cut across the whole chain:

  • due diligence on investment options offered on platforms
  • monitoring unusual inflows
  • managing conflicts in lead generation and advice
  • the limits of relying on research ratings.

Wholesale funds and alternatives

  • Unregistered (wholesale) schemes offered only to wholesale clients don't need registration, a compliance plan or a compliance committee. The trustee usually still needs an AFS licence, and ASIC's scrutiny of wholesale funds has increased.
  • Corporate collective investment vehicles (CCIVs), available since 1 July 2022, are a company-based alternative to the unit trust structure, operated by a corporate director.

Check your understanding

1. An RE's board has five directors, two of them external. What does the RE need?

Fewer than half the directors are external (two of five), so a compliance committee is required.

2. The RE wants to pay a related company from scheme property for property management services. What should it consider first?

Related-party benefits from scheme property need member approval unless an exception, such as arm's length terms, applies. The conflict must be managed in members' interests.
