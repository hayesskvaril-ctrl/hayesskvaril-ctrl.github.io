Superannuation trustee governance

The obligations that shape how an APRA-regulated super fund is run: who regulates it, the duties trustees owe members, and the main standards, tests and reporting that sit around them.

Intermediate About 25 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Checked against sources
Main laws
Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 (SIS Act) and Regulations; Corporations Act 2001; Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023.
Regulators
APRA (prudential), ASIC (conduct and disclosure), the ATO (self-managed funds and employer contributions), and AFCA for member complaints.
Licences
An RSE licence from APRA, and an Australian financial services licence from ASIC.
Scope of this page
APRA-regulated funds, not self-managed super funds.

Key takeaways

  • A super trustee holds members' retirement savings on trust, so it is regulated both as a trustee (SIS Act covenants) and as a financial services provider (Corporations Act).
  • The central duty is to act in members' best financial interests. Trustees should be able to show evidence for how decisions, including spending, meet that test.
  • APRA's prudential standards set out how the trustee must be governed and run: governance, risk, investment, strategy and member outcomes, operational resilience and more.
  • Member outcomes are tested publicly: the annual outcomes assessment by the trustee, and APRA's annual performance test.
  • Since 15 March 2025, super trustees have been covered by the Financial Accountability Regime.
  • Member services are now a major enforcement focus: courts have ordered penalties against large trustees over duplicate accounts (2025), insurance and death benefit claim delays (2025) and failures to report problems to ASIC (2026).

The big picture

In Australia most people's super sits in large APRA-regulated funds run by a corporate trustee, the RSE licensee. The trustee's board is responsible for the fund. Members can't easily judge whether their fund is well run, and much of their money is placed there by default, so the law sets strong duties and heavy oversight.

The trustee covenants

Section 52 of the SIS Act writes covenants (binding promises) into every fund's governing rules. Members can sue for loss caused by a breach, and many breaches also attract civil penalties. Section 52A sets matching covenants for each director of a corporate trustee.

CovenantIn plain English
Honesty; care, skill and diligence (s 52(2)(a)–(b))Act honestly, and with the care, skill and diligence a prudent superannuation trustee would use.
Best financial interests (s 52(2)(c))Perform duties and use powers in members' best financial interests. The courts focus on the process behind a decision, not only its result.
Conflicts (s 52(2)(d))Where a conflict arises, give priority to members' interests and manage the conflict so members aren't disadvantaged.
Investment strategy (s 52(6))Set and regularly review an investment strategy that considers risk and return, diversification, liquidity and valuation, among other factors.
Insurance strategy (s 52(7))Set an insurance strategy and offer only insurance that doesn't unduly erode members' retirement savings.
Retirement income strategy (s 52(8A))Have a strategy to help members who are retired or near retirement, in force since 1 July 2022.
Outcomes assessment (s 52(9)–(12))Assess every year whether each MySuper and choice product is promoting members' financial interests, compared with other products.

Best financial interests in practice

Since 1 July 2021 the duty has said "financial" interests, and the evidential burden is reversed: the trustee and its directors must be able to point to evidence that their decisions were in members' best financial interests. This matters especially for spending such as marketing, sponsorships, advertising and payments to related parties. Good practice is to record the decision, the options considered and the expected member benefit, and to review the results.

APRA's prudential standards for super

Super uses a mix of super-only standards ("SPS") and cross-industry standards ("CPS"). The main ones for governance and risk:

StandardWhat it covers
SPS 510 GovernanceBoard composition and independence, board committees (such as audit and remuneration), board renewal and performance assessment.
SPS 520 Fit and ProperAssessing that directors, senior managers and auditors have the competence and character for their roles.
SPS 521 Conflicts of InterestA conflicts management framework, register and policy.
SPS 220 Risk ManagementRisk management framework, risk appetite statement, risk management strategy, a designated risk management function and CRO.
SPS 515 Strategic Planning and Member OutcomesLinking strategy and business planning to member outcomes, and annual business performance reviews. A strengthened version applies from 1 July 2025.
SPS 530 Investment GovernanceInvestment governance framework, including stress testing, liquidity management and valuation.
SPS 114 Operational Risk Financial RequirementHolding financial resources to meet losses from operational risk events.
SPS 250 Insurance in SuperannuationInsurance management framework, including selecting insurers and managing insurance arrangements.
CPS 230 Operational Risk ManagementOperational risk, business continuity and service providers, in force since 1 July 2025 (replacing SPS 231 and SPS 232).
CPS 234 Information SecurityInformation security capability, controls, testing and notifying APRA of incidents.
CPS 190 Recovery and Exit PlanningPlans for responding to severe financial stress, and for an orderly exit if needed. Applies to RSE licensees from 1 January 2025.
CPS 511 RemunerationRemuneration frameworks that support sound risk management and members' interests.

Other super standards cover audit and related matters (SPS 310), defined benefit matters (SPS 160) and APRA's data collections (reporting standards, being rebuilt through APRA's Superannuation Data Transformation project). See SPS 515 and SPS 530 for detailed guides.

On 16 June 2026 APRA released a draft cross-industry CPS 510 Governance for consultation (closed 28 August 2026). It would replace five separate governance, fit and proper, and conflicts of interest standards, including SPS 510, SPS 520 and SPS 521, with one standard for banks, insurers and super trustees, and remove routine fit and proper reporting. APRA plans to release the final standard in late 2026, with new requirements expected to apply from early 2028. On the same day it consulted on strengthening SPS 530 investment governance. Check APRA's website for the current position.

Member outcomes and the performance test

FeatureOutcomes assessmentAnnual performance test
Who does itThe trusteeAPRA
ProductsMySuper and choice productsMySuper products (since 2021) and trustee-directed products (since 2023)
What it looks atWhether the product promotes members' financial interests, considering returns, fees and costs, investment risk, insurance and other factors, compared with other productsNet investment returns against a benchmark portfolio over up to 10 years, with administration fees taken into account
ConsequenceMust be published (a summary within 28 days). Feeds into business planning under SPS 515.A failing product's members must be notified. A product that fails two years in a row can't accept new members.

In 2026 Treasury consulted on changes to the performance test, including how to treat asset classes that don't have a suitable market benchmark. Treat the design details as subject to change and check APRA's current methodology.

Conduct obligations from ASIC

  • Disclosure and fees: PDSs, periodic statements and fees and costs disclosure under ASIC's RG 97. See Disclosure obligations.
  • Design and distribution: choice products need a target market determination. MySuper products are excluded. See RG 274.
  • Complaints: most super complaints must be resolved or answered within 45 calendar days, with separate timeframes for death benefit objections. See RG 271.
  • Breach reporting: reportable situations to ASIC (as an AFS licensee) and significant breaches to APRA under SIS Act s 29JA. See Breach and incident reporting obligations.
  • Remediation: where members are harmed (for example, by fee errors or delays), RG 277 applies, often with balance corrections and lost earnings.
  • Member services: ASIC has focused on how trustees handle claims (including death benefits and insurance), complaints and member communications.

Fee governance

Fees reduce members' balances every year, so fee governance is a core trustee risk. A sound approach usually covers:

  • Authority to charge: each fee and cost must be permitted by the governing rules, disclosed in the PDS and, for MySuper, be a permitted fee type.
  • Fee setting and review: a documented rationale linking fees to costs and to member value, reviewed as part of the outcomes assessment.
  • Accuracy controls: reconciliations and testing of fee calculations in the administration system, including after system or product changes.
  • Disclosure alignment: fees shown in the PDS, periodic statements and RG 97 calculations match what is actually charged.
  • Errors and remediation: fee errors are a common breach and remediation trigger. They need prompt breach assessment, root-cause fixes and member remediation.

Accountability: FAR and fitness

The Financial Accountability Regime has applied to RSE licensees since 15 March 2025. Trustees must notify APRA and ASIC of their accountable persons, keep accountability statements and maps, and apply deferral and consequence rules to variable pay. It sits alongside SPS 520's fit and proper requirements and CPS 511.

Insurance in super

Most members get default life, total and permanent disability (TPD) and sometimes income protection insurance through their fund. Premiums come out of their balance, so the law balances cover against erosion of retirement savings.

RuleIn plain English
Insurance strategy (SIS Act s 52(7))The trustee must set an insurance strategy and offer cover only where premiums don't inappropriately erode members' retirement income.
SPS 250 Insurance in SuperannuationAn insurance management framework, including selecting insurers, the terms of cover and monitoring the arrangements.
Inactive accounts (from 1 July 2019)Cover on an account that hasn't received a contribution or rollover for 16 months must stop, unless the member chooses to keep it.
Young members and low balances (from 1 April 2020)Default cover is generally opt-in for new members under 25 and for accounts with balances below $6,000, with some exceptions (for example, some dangerous occupations).
Claims handlingClaims handling is a financial service regulated by ASIC, with claims-handling complaints to AFCA. Delays in death and TPD claims have been a major ASIC focus.

ASIC's 2024 review of death benefit claims found long delays and poor communication with grieving families. Its June 2026 follow-up, Report 831, found complaints about death benefit delays had fallen by 53% since early 2024 but that some of the 45 trustees it examined still lacked urgency and maturity in fixing basic processes.

Recent enforcement

These cases show where regulators and courts have drawn the line. They are summaries; see the regulators' media releases for detail.

CaseWhat went wrongOutcome
AustralianSuper (judgment 21 February 2025)Failed to deal with members holding multiple accounts from 2013 to 2023, so about 90,000 members paid duplicate fees and insurance premiums, costing them about $69 million.$27 million penalty. The court found it "inexcusable" not to have the processes and systems to comply.
United Super, trustee of Cbus (November 2025)Unreasonable delays for more than 7,000 members and claimants in handling death benefits and TPD insurance claims.$23.5 million penalty, and about $32 million compensation to 7,402 people.

Other developments to watch

  • Payday Super (law since 6 November 2025, in force from 1 July 2026): employers must pay super with wages, with contributions due to reach the fund within 7 business days of payday, instead of quarterly. Trustees process and allocate far more, smaller contributions, and must deal with errors and returns quickly.
  • Division 296 tax on earnings for members with total super balances above $3 million applies from 1 July 2026. APRA-regulated funds must report in-scope members' earnings to the ATO from the 2026–27 income year. See the ATO's guidance for APRA funds.
  • CPS 510 governance consolidation (final standard expected late 2026, requirements from early 2028) and APRA's proposed changes to SPS 530.
  • Death benefit and claims handling, following ASIC's Report 831.
  • Performance test changes following Treasury's 2026 consultation.
  • ASIC's review of RG 97 fees and costs disclosure.

Common weak spots

  • Member administration that isn't controlled end to end: duplicate accounts, unallocated contributions, insurance not starting or stopping when it should.
  • Claims handling without end-to-end time measures, targets or ownership, especially for death benefits.
  • Breach reporting systems that don't capture investigations that run longer than the reporting window.
  • Spending and related-party arrangements without evidence of member benefit.
  • Outsourced administration that the trustee doesn't actively oversee, even though the trustee stays accountable.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. How many members hold more than one account with us, and what are we doing about it?
  2. What are our end-to-end times for death and TPD claims, and how do they compare with our targets?
  3. How much member money is unallocated, and for how long?
  4. Which incidents have we investigated in the last year, and were all reportable ones reported to ASIC and APRA on time?
  5. Can we show the member benefit of our largest discretionary and related-party spending?
  6. Are our administrator and other material service providers delivering to the standard our members need, and how do we know?

Frequently asked questions

Does this page apply to self-managed super funds?

No. Self-managed super funds (SMSFs) are regulated by the ATO and their members are their own trustees. This page covers APRA-regulated funds run by RSE licensees.

Why does a super trustee need both an RSE licence and an AFS licence?

The RSE licence from APRA covers running the fund as a trustee. Issuing interests in the fund, giving general advice and dealing with members are financial services, which need an Australian financial services licence from ASIC.

Who does a member complain to?

First to the trustee, through its internal dispute resolution process. If that doesn't resolve it, the member can go to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA), which is free for consumers.

Is the outcomes assessment the same as the performance test?

No. The trustee makes the outcomes assessment about its own products each year. APRA runs the performance test. See the comparison table above and SPS 515.

Check your understanding

1. A trustee plans a large sports sponsorship. What does the best financial interests duty mean for this decision?

Spending isn't banned, but the trustee bears the onus of showing it is in members' best financial interests. Board approval alone isn't enough without a sound, documented rationale.

2. A trustee-directed product fails APRA's performance test for the second year in a row. What happens?

After a first fail, members must be notified. After two consecutive fails, the product must be closed to new members.

3. Who makes the annual member outcomes assessment?

The trustee makes the outcomes assessment and publishes a summary. APRA runs the separate performance test.
