Banking and ADIs

How banks, building societies and credit unions (together, authorised deposit-taking institutions, or ADIs) are regulated to keep deposits safe and treat customers fairly.

Intermediate About 14 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources
Main laws
Banking Act 1959; National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009 (including the National Credit Code); Corporations Act 2001; Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023; Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006.
Regulators
APRA (prudential), ASIC (conduct, credit and financial services), AUSTRAC (financial crime), the ACCC (competition and scams), the RBA (payments system and financial stability), and AFCA for complaints.
Licences
APRA authorisation as an ADI, plus an Australian credit licence and an AFS licence for most retail banking.

Key takeaways

  • Only APRA-authorised ADIs may take deposits from the public as a business, and use of words such as "bank" is restricted.
  • Prudential rules focus on capital (absorbing losses) and liquidity (meeting cash outflows), supported by strong risk management.
  • APRA also uses macroprudential tools, such as the mortgage serviceability buffer and, from February 2026, a limit on high debt-to-income home lending.
  • If things go wrong, recovery plans, APRA's resolution powers and the Financial Claims Scheme (up to $250,000 per account holder per ADI) protect depositors.
  • On the conduct side, responsible lending, the Banking Code of Practice, AML/CTF and the new Scams Prevention Framework are the big obligations.

Layers of protection for depositors

Prudential standards for ADIs

ADIs follow banking-specific standards ("APS") and the cross-industry standards ("CPS"). The capital rules are based on the international Basel III framework, adapted by APRA.

AreaStandard(s)In brief
Capital adequacyAPS 110 and the risk-specific capital standardsMinimum capital ratios against risk-weighted assets, with buffers on top. Common Equity Tier 1 (mainly shareholders' equity) must be at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets before buffers.
LiquidityAPS 210Larger ADIs must meet the Liquidity Coverage Ratio (enough high-quality liquid assets for a 30-day stress) and the Net Stable Funding Ratio. Smaller ADIs have a simpler minimum liquidity holdings requirement.
Credit risk managementAPS 220Credit risk management framework, lending standards, and identifying and provisioning for problem loans.
Large exposuresAPS 221Limits on concentrated exposures to single counterparties or connected groups.
Interest rate risk in the banking bookAPS 117Managing and holding capital for the effect of interest rate changes on the balance sheet (for larger ADIs).
Governance, risk, resilienceCPS 510, CPS 520, CPS 220, CPS 230, CPS 234, CPS 511Board and governance, fit and proper, risk management, operational resilience, information security and remuneration.
Recovery and resolutionCPS 190, CPS 900Recovery and exit plans for severe stress (from 1 January 2024 for ADIs), and resolution planning for larger or more complex entities.

Capital buffers

On top of the minimums, ADIs hold buffers of Common Equity Tier 1 capital that can be drawn down in stress, though doing so limits dividends and bonuses. They are:

  • a capital conservation buffer for all ADIs
  • an extra 1% for the four major banks as domestic systemically important banks
  • a countercyclical capital buffer, set by APRA, with a default of 1% of risk-weighted assets in normal conditions.

APRA has decided to phase out Additional Tier 1 capital (bank "hybrids") from 1 January 2027, replacing it mainly with Common Equity Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital, with existing instruments expected to be gone by 2032.

Worked example: the Liquidity Coverage Ratio

Item (illustrative bank)Amount
High-quality liquid assets (cash, government bonds, after haircuts)$12.0 billion
Expected cash outflows over 30 days of stress (e.g. deposit run-off, wholesale funding maturing)$14.0 billion
Expected inflows over the same period (capped)$4.0 billion
Net cash outflows$10.0 billion
LCR = liquid assets ÷ net cash outflows120% (minimum 100%)

The stress assumptions (for example, how fast different kinds of deposits leave) are set by APRA. Online banking has made deposit runs faster, as the overseas bank failures of 2023 showed.

Macroprudential policy

Macroprudential tools target risks to the financial system as a whole, not a single bank. APRA's main housing tools today:

  • Serviceability buffer: lenders assess whether borrowers can afford repayments at an interest rate at least 3 percentage points above the loan rate.
  • Debt-to-income (DTI) limit: from February 2026, no more than 20% of an ADI's new mortgage lending may be at a DTI of six times or more. It is measured separately for owner-occupier and investor lending, with some exemptions, such as loans to build or buy new dwellings.

Accountability

ADIs were the first sector covered by the Financial Accountability Regime, from 15 March 2024. It replaced the Banking Executive Accountability Regime (BEAR), which had applied since 2018. FAR is jointly administered by APRA and ASIC.

Check your understanding

1. A bank has $9 billion of high-quality liquid assets and $10 billion of net cash outflows in the 30-day stress scenario. What is the position?

$9bn ÷ $10bn = 90%. The LCR must be at least 100%, so the bank would need more liquid assets or less reliance on short-term funding.

2. Which tool is macroprudential rather than aimed at a single bank's soundness?

The DTI limit targets system-wide housing credit risk. The other two are about individual ADIs.
