Private health insurance

How private health insurers are regulated: community rating, ministerial premium approval, APRA's capital framework, the Ombudsman and why ASIC's conduct regime mostly doesn't apply.

Intermediate About 19 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources
Main laws
Private Health Insurance Act 2007; Private Health Insurance (Prudential Supervision) Act 2015; Competition and Consumer Act 2010; Privacy Act 1988; Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023.
Regulators
Department of Health, Disability and Ageing (product and premium rules); APRA (prudential); ACCC (consumer law); OAIC (privacy); the Private Health Insurance Ombudsman (complaints).
Products
Hospital cover (Gold, Silver, Bronze and Basic tiers) and general treatment (extras) cover.
Industry code
Private Health Insurance Code of Conduct.

Key takeaways

  • Private health insurance is community rated: insurers can't refuse cover or charge more because of someone's health or claims history.
  • Premium changes need the Health Minister's approval, and usually take effect from 1 April each year.
  • Health insurance isn't a financial product under the Corporations Act, so ASIC's licensing and conduct rules mostly don't apply.
  • APRA supervises insurers' financial strength, with a revised capital framework since 1 July 2023.
  • Complaints go to the Private Health Insurance Ombudsman, not AFCA.

Private health insurance in plain English

Private health insurance helps pay for treatment as a private patient in hospital and for extras such as dental, physiotherapy and optical. It works differently from other insurance. Under community rating, insurers can't charge people more, or refuse them cover, because of their health, age (beyond the Lifetime Health Cover loading), claims history or where they live. The government sets the product rules and approves premium increases. And because health insurance isn't a "financial product" under the Corporations Act, ASIC's conduct regime largely doesn't apply: the rules come from health insurance law instead.

Key rules under the Private Health Insurance Act

RuleIn plain English
Community ratingInsurers must not improperly discriminate between people, for example on health, claims history or where they live, when offering or pricing complying policies.
Premium approvalChanges to premiums need the Health Minister's approval. Increases usually take effect from 1 April each year after an annual approval round.
Product tiersHospital cover is classified as Gold, Silver, Bronze or Basic based on the treatments covered (since 1 April 2019).
Lifetime Health CoverPeople who take out hospital cover later in life pay a loading on top of the base premium, to encourage people to join earlier.
Information for consumersStandard information statements let people compare policies; the government's privatehealth.gov.au site compares them.

Prudential regulation

APRA took over prudential supervision of private health insurers on 1 July 2015, from the former Private Health Insurance Administration Council. Its revised capital framework, based on the approach used for life and general insurers but adapted for health insurance, applied from 1 July 2023. The main standard is HPS 110 Capital Adequacy, with companion standards for asset risk, asset concentration risk and operational risk. Health insurers also follow cross-industry standards, including CPS 220, CPS 230, CPS 234 and the governance and fit and proper standards.

Health insurers hold large amounts of sensitive data, so cyber security and privacy are major risks. See the Optus and Medibank data breaches.

Worked example: switching insurers and waiting periods

A member with hospital cover moves to another insurer's policy with equivalent cover. Six months later, she needs knee surgery for a condition she had before joining the first insurer.

  1. Portability: waiting periods already served for equivalent benefits carry over, so she doesn't serve them again.
  2. Higher cover: if the new policy covered more than the old one, waiting periods could apply to the extra benefits.
  3. Legislated maximums: insurers can apply waiting periods of up to 12 months for pre-existing conditions and obstetrics, and up to 2 months for most other hospital treatment.
  4. Good practice: explain waiting periods clearly before the member switches, and give a transfer certificate promptly.

Illustrative only. The Private Health Insurance Act and rules set the detailed requirements.

Common weak spots

  • Communicating product changes, such as reductions in benefits or cover, clearly and early.
  • Cyber security and privacy of health information.
  • Complaints handling and how issues raised with the Ombudsman are fixed at their root.
  • Capital and pricing under pressure from rising claims costs and an ageing membership.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. What are members complaining to us and the Ombudsman about, and what have we changed as a result?
  2. How would we detect and respond to a cyber attack on member health data?
  3. How do we make sure product changes are communicated clearly and in time?
  4. How resilient is our capital position to a sustained rise in claims?

Frequently asked questions

Does ASIC regulate private health insurers?

Not for health insurance products, which are excluded from the Corporations Act's definition of a financial product. ASIC's general corporate law role still applies to them as companies.

Do private health insurance complaints go to AFCA?

No. After the insurer's own process, complaints go to the Private Health Insurance Ombudsman, a role held by the Commonwealth Ombudsman.

Can an insurer refuse cover because someone is unwell?

Not for complying policies. Community rating prevents improper discrimination on health grounds, although waiting periods can apply to pre-existing conditions.
