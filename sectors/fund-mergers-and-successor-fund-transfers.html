Fund mergers and successor fund transfers

How super funds merge without member consent, the equivalent rights and best financial interests tests, and why the biggest risks often come after the legal transfer.

Advanced About 25 minutes Last reviewed: 2 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Most super fund mergers use a successor fund transfer, which moves members without their consent if the successor fund confers equivalent rights in respect of their benefits (SIS Regulations reg 6.29).
  • Equivalent means not identical: rights are assessed as a bundle, for individual members and groups, not just on average.
  • Both trustees must act in members' best financial interests, and the transferring trustee must be able to evidence why the transfer meets that test.
  • From 1 July 2025, SPS 515 brings transfer planning into strategic planning, with APRA's SFT guidance now part of its SPS 515 guidance.
  • The highest operational risk sits in data migration, insurance continuity and integration, often for years after the transfer date.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the legal framework for successor fund transfers, including the equivalent rights test.
  2. Evidence that a merger is in members' best financial interests.
  3. Use research on scale, costs and governance in pension funds to evaluate the case for consolidation.
  4. Critically assess the limits of the scale argument and the risks mergers create.

The idea in plain English

Australian super has been consolidating for years, as funds seek scale, lower costs and better outcomes, and as the annual performance test and APRA's focus on member outcomes put pressure on smaller or underperforming funds. Most mergers move members from one fund to another without asking each member's consent, using a successor fund transfer (SFT). Because members don't choose, the law sets a protective test, and both trustees must be satisfied the move is in members' best financial interests.

This page explains the legal tests, how trustees assess and evidence them, and where merger risk really sits: often in the years after the legal transfer, when systems and products are brought together. It builds on superannuation trustee governance and SPS 515.

The legal framework (summary)

ElementIn brief
SFT rule (SIS Regulations, reg 6.29)Members' benefits can be transferred without their consent to a successor fund that confers on them equivalent rights to the rights they had in the transferring fund in respect of their benefits.
Equivalent, not equalAPRA's long-standing view is that rights must be equivalent, not identical, assessed as a bundle of rights, for individual members and groups of members, not just on average.
Best financial interestsBoth trustees must act in the best financial interests of their beneficiaries. The transferring trustee must be able to show why the transfer is in its members' best financial interests, with supporting evidence.
Transfer planning (SPS 515)From 1 July 2025, SPS 515 brings transfer planning into strategic and business planning, and APRA's guidance on SFTs is now part of its SPS 515 guidance rather than a separate guide.
Other regimesTax, insurance, member communication and disclosure, ATO reporting protocols and, for some transactions, competition law. Australia's mandatory merger notification regime has applied since 1 January 2026, but the ACCC's notification determination exempts acquisitions by super entities that relate to transferring members' benefits between super entities or a change of trustee (see the ACCC's merger reform FAQs). Tax relief is covered below.

Testing equivalent rights

The equivalent rights test is applied to rights in respect of benefits, looking at what members are legally entitled to, not just current practice. Common areas of analysis:

AreaQuestions
Benefits and entitlementsAre accrued benefits preserved? For defined benefit members, is the benefit formula or its value protected?
Investment optionsHow do old options map to new ones? Are members moved to options with a materially different risk profile?
Fees and costsFees aren't strictly "rights", but a large fee increase goes to best financial interests. Compare total cost for representative members.
InsuranceIs cover continued on equivalent terms, without new underwriting, exclusions or waiting periods for existing cover?
Reserves and accountsHow are reserves, unallocated amounts and member-specific arrangements treated?
Governing rulesRights under the trust deed, such as how the trustee can amend rules or exercise discretions.
Groups and individualsAre any groups worse off (for example pension members, members with unusual insurance, or defined benefit members) even if the average member is better off?

Evidencing best financial interests

The comparison is between the members' likely outcomes if the transfer happens and if it doesn't. Typical evidence:

  • Net returns and investment strategy compared over relevant periods, and the credibility of the successor's future strategy.
  • Fees and costs for representative member cohorts, including the effect of scale over time.
  • Insurance design, premiums and claims experience.
  • Services, digital capability and retirement income offerings.
  • Financial strength and governance of the successor trustee, including its risk management and track record with previous mergers.
  • Transition costs and who bears them.
  • Risks of the transition itself (below), and how they will be managed.

Where some cohorts would be worse off, trustees need to decide whether that can be addressed (for example through transitional arrangements) and document the reasoning. "Most members are better off" is not the same as the test being met.

Where merger risk really sits

  • Data migration: incomplete or incorrect member data leads to wrong balances, lost insurance and payment errors. Reconcile at member level, not just in totals.
  • Insurance continuity: gaps in cover around the transfer date can leave members uninsured when they claim.
  • Service disruption: call volumes spike and processing backlogs grow just as members need help.
  • Benefits realisation: scale benefits depend on consolidating administration and products. Track them against what was promised in the best financial interests case.
  • Culture and people: key-person loss and competing ways of working can weaken controls during integration.

See change, project and reputational risk and mapping critical operations for managing the transition.

How a merger runs, step by step

Every merger is different, but most follow a similar path. The legal transfer date is one point in a process that can take a year or more before it and several years after.

StageWhat happensKey risks and controls
1. Strategy and triggersUnder SPS 515, the trustee's monitoring and triggers (including the performance test) prompt it to consider whether members would be better off elsewhere.Triggers set too late; the board not seeing the case early enough.
2. Partner search and agreementShortlisting partners and signing a memorandum of understanding or heads of agreement.Decisions driven by board seats, brand or staff rather than members' interests; conflicts of interest.
3. Due diligenceEach side examines the other's governance, investments, insurance, administration, data, risk, compliance history and liabilities.Hidden data quality problems, unresolved breaches and remediation, and insurance terms that can't be matched.
4. AssessmentBest financial interests and equivalent rights assessments for every member cohort, with legal and actuarial input.Relying on averages; cohorts that are worse off not identified or addressed.
5. Approvals and documentsBoth boards approve; the trustees sign a successor fund transfer deed and engage with APRA, ASIC and the ATO (including the ATO's transfer reporting protocol).Deed terms that don't reflect the assessment; regulators engaged too late.
6. Member communicationMembers are told what is changing, including investment option mapping, fees and insurance.Unclear or late notices, and members not told about changes that affect them.
7. TransferAssets, members' accounts and insurance move to the successor fund on the transfer date.Data errors, unit pricing during the transfer, insurance gaps and service backlogs.
8. Integration and reviewSystems, products and teams are combined, and promised benefits are tracked.Benefits not delivered; errors found months later, leading to breach reports and remediation.

Tax and performance history

  • Tax relief: moving assets between funds would normally trigger capital gains tax. Division 310 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 lets merging complying super funds transfer losses and defer tax on gains and losses, subject to conditions. The relief started as a temporary measure in 2008 and was made permanent from 1 July 2020.
  • Performance test history: after a merger, the performance test needs a combined history for the successor's products. APRA has published an information paper on how it combines trustee-directed product performance histories. The merger assessment should consider how the combined products will be tested.

Common mistakes

  • Treating "most members are better off" as enough, without assessing and addressing worse-off cohorts.
  • Underestimating integration, so the scale benefits used to justify the merger arrive years late or not at all.
  • Insurance mapping done late, leaving members with gaps, new exclusions or different definitions.
  • Data reconciled only in totals, so member-level errors appear after the transfer.
  • Open breaches and remediation not resolved or priced in before the transfer, leaving the successor to deal with them.
  • No post-merger review comparing actual outcomes with what the board was told.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Which member cohorts would be worse off, by how much, and what are we doing about it?
  2. What evidence supports the fee and return benefits we expect, and when will members actually see them?
  3. How will insurance cover be mapped, and could any member be left without cover on the transfer date?
  4. What open incidents, breaches and remediation programs are we inheriting or handing over?
  5. How will we measure, after the transfer, whether the merger delivered what we promised members?

Theory and research

The case for scale

Much of the policy push for super fund mergers rests on economies of scale. International evidence supports it. Using Dutch data, Bikker and de Dreu (2009) found substantial economies of scale in pension fund operating costs, with larger funds having much lower costs per member. In Australia, Bateman and Mitchell (2004) examined how the design and structure of super funds relate to administrative expenses, an early contribution to debates about fees and consolidation.

Governance matters as much as size

Scale is not the only driver of outcomes. Coleman et al. (2006) studied 225 Australian funds over the seven years to 2002 and found that not-for-profit funds significantly outperformed for-profit funds, a difference they linked to higher agency costs in for-profit funds (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). For a merger assessment, this means the question is not only whether the combined fund will be larger, but whether its governance, investment capability and incentives will serve members better.

Critical perspectives

  • Scale has limits. Very large funds can face diseconomies, for example difficulty investing large sums in smaller, high-return opportunities, and more complex operations. The optimal size is debated.
  • Merger risk is operational. Research on scale benefits says little about the transition itself: data migration, member communications and insurance changes, which is where members can be harmed.
  • Older evidence. Some key Australian studies predate MySuper, the performance test and the best financial interests duty. The industry has changed a great deal since.
  • Average effects hide variation. A merger that benefits most members can disadvantage some, such as those losing a particular insurance cover or investment option. The equivalent rights test exists for this reason.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Bikker and de Dreu's findings, estimate how cost savings from scale might be shared between members of two merging funds. Who benefits most?
  2. Coleman and colleagues found governance mattered. What governance factors would you assess in a merger partner, beyond size?
  3. How would you evidence that a merger is in the best financial interests of a group of members who lose a valued insurance feature?
  4. Is there a size at which a super fund becomes too big? What evidence would you need to decide?

Check your understanding

1. Under the SFT rule, what must the successor fund confer on transferring members?

Rights must be equivalent, not equal, assessed as a bundle.

2. Most members would be better off after a merger, but one group of pension members would lose a feature. What follows?

The assessment covers individual members and groups, not just averages.

3. Where is operational risk in a merger usually highest?

Data migration, insurance continuity and system consolidation carry the most risk of member harm.
