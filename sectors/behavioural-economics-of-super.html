Behavioural economics of super: defaults, inertia and choice

Why most members never make an active choice, what research shows about defaults, inertia and disclosure, and what it means for trustees.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Most people accept the default: automatic enrolment, default contribution rates and default investment options shape outcomes more than choice does.
  • This makes the design of default products one of the most important decisions a trustee makes, and a key reason for MySuper and the performance test.
  • More choice is not always better, but the evidence that "too much choice" harms decisions is mixed.
  • How risk is described changes the choices people make, which matters for risk labels and investment disclosures.
  • Behavioural insights can help members, but they also raise questions about paternalism and whose interests a default serves.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain default effects, status quo bias and inertia, and their role in Australian super.
  2. Evaluate evidence on choice overload, naive diversification and commitment devices.
  3. Analyse how the presentation of investment risk affects member choices, using Australian research.
  4. Critically assess the ethics and limits of designing defaults for disengaged members.

The idea in plain English

Traditional economics assumes people choose the super fund, investment option and contribution rate that best suits them. In practice, most Australians never make an active choice: they end up in a default fund, in the default option, contributing the compulsory minimum. Behavioural economics studies why, and what follows for policy and product design. It builds on the superannuation sector page.

Theory and research

Status quo bias and the power of defaults

Samuelson and Zeckhauser (1988) showed in experiments and field data, including retirement plan choices, that people disproportionately stick with the status quo. Madrian and Shea (2001) provided the landmark evidence from retirement saving: when a large US employer switched from opt-in to automatic enrolment, participation in the retirement plan rose sharply, and many employees stayed at the default contribution rate and default investment, even though nothing else about the plan had changed. Defaults work through several channels: effort (changing takes time), procrastination, and the implicit endorsement a default seems to carry.

Australia's compulsory system already relies heavily on defaults. MySuper products were designed as simple, low-cost defaults, the annual performance test aims to remove underperforming ones, and since 1 November 2021 "stapling" means an employee's existing fund follows them to a new job unless they choose otherwise, to prevent unintended multiple accounts. Each is a policy built on the insight that most members won't act.

Commitment devices

If people won't save more today, will they commit to saving more later? Thaler and Benartzi (2004) designed "Save More Tomorrow", in which employees committed in advance to put part of future pay rises into retirement savings. Of those offered it, 78% joined, and average saving rates rose from 3.5% to 11.6% over 28 months. The design works with inertia rather than against it: once enrolled, people stay.

Choice overload and naive diversification

A famous set of experiments by Iyengar and Lepper (2000) found people were more likely to choose when offered 6 options than 24 or 30, suggesting "choice overload". Later work tempered this: a meta-analysis of 50 experiments found an average effect of about zero, with large variation, meaning overload occurs in some conditions but is not universal (Scheibehenne et al., 2010). Menus also shape choices in less obvious ways. Benartzi and Thaler (2001) found many people spread savings evenly across whatever options are offered, so adding more share-based options increases how much people hold in shares.

How risk is described

Disclosure is supposed to help members choose, but presentation matters. In an Australian choice experiment, Bateman et al. (2016) found people made choices inconsistent with basic principles of rational choice in about a quarter of cases, and the rate varied with how investment risk was described. This is directly relevant to the standard risk measure and to how funds label investment options. More broadly, research on disclosure shows that limited attention can blunt or even reverse its intended effects (Loewenstein et al., 2014).

Critical perspectives

  • Paternalism. Designing defaults means someone decides what is good for members. Supporters call this "libertarian paternalism" because people can still opt out; critics argue defaults can serve providers' interests, which is why default design needs strong governance.
  • A default is only as good as its design. Inertia means a poor default harms many people for a long time. The performance test exists because inertia kept members in underperforming products.
  • Replication and context. Some behavioural findings, such as choice overload, have proven less robust than first thought. Effects found in US employer plans may differ in a compulsory system.
  • Engagement is not always the goal. Encouraging members to switch can backfire if they switch at the wrong time, for example after a market fall. Good outcomes may matter more than active choice.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Madrian and Shea's findings, explain why the design of MySuper products matters more than the quality of choice products for most members.
  2. Design a "Save More Tomorrow" style option for an Australian super fund. What legal, operational and ethical issues would it raise?
  3. Scheibehenne and colleagues found the average choice overload effect was close to zero. What does this mean for how many investment options a fund should offer?
  4. Bateman and colleagues found the way risk was described changed choices. How would you test a new risk label before using it?
  5. Is stapling a good example of using inertia in members' interests? Who might it disadvantage?
