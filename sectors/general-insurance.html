General insurance

How general insurers (home, motor, travel, business and liability) are regulated, from capital and reinsurance to pricing, claims after disasters and the Code of Practice.

Intermediate About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources
Main laws
Insurance Act 1973; Insurance Contracts Act 1984; Corporations Act 2001; ASIC Act 2001; Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023.
Regulators
APRA (prudential) and ASIC (conduct); AFCA handles complaints.
Products
Home and contents, motor, travel, business, liability, landlord and other general insurance.
Industry code
General Insurance Code of Practice (Insurance Council of Australia).

Key takeaways

  • General insurers are regulated by APRA for financial strength and by ASIC for how they treat customers.
  • Every insurance contract carries a duty of utmost good faith, and since 1 January 2022 insurers must handle claims efficiently, honestly and fairly.
  • Post-Royal Commission reforms banned hawking, introduced a deferred sales model for add-on insurance and applied unfair contract terms laws to insurance.
  • Pricing promises and claims after disasters are ASIC's biggest concerns: IAL was penalised $40 million in 2023 over discounts it didn't deliver.
  • A redrafted Code of Practice proposes enforceable claim timeframes, with ASIC approval sought from October 2026.

General insurance in plain English

General insurance covers things that can be damaged, lost or go wrong: homes and contents, cars, travel, businesses, liability and more. Most policies last a year and are renewed. The insurer collects premiums now and pays claims later, sometimes in huge numbers at once after a flood, bushfire or cyclone. Regulation therefore has two jobs: making sure insurers can afford to pay (APRA), and making sure customers are treated fairly from sale to claim (ASIC).

Pricing promises

Many general insurers promised loyalty, multi-policy or no-claims discounts that their pricing systems didn't fully deliver. In 2023 the Federal Court ordered Insurance Australia Limited to pay $40 million, the largest penalty imposed on an insurer for breaching financial services consumer protection laws at the time, for misleading more than 600,000 NRMA customers between 2014 and 2019 about the loyalty and no-claims discounts they would receive. ASIC's 2023 report on pricing promises (Report 765) called on general insurers to improve how they manage the non-financial risks behind these systemic failures. ASIC also began separate proceedings in 2023 over loyalty discounts on SGIO, SGIC and RACV home insurance; check ASIC's website for the outcome.

Claims after disasters

Catastrophes test every part of a general insurer at once: capital and reinsurance, claims capacity, outsourced assessors and builders, and the treatment of customers in distress.

  • ASIC's 2023 review of claims handling after the 2022 floods found insurers can and should improve claims handling, and ASIC wrote to insurers in March 2024 about claims and severe weather events.
  • Parliamentary inquiry: the House of Representatives economics committee's inquiry into insurers' responses to the 2022 floods ("Not if, but when", 2024) made recommendations on claims handling, communication and the Code.
  • Enforcement: in April 2025 ASIC sued Hollard Insurance Partners, alleging it breached its duty of utmost good faith by taking nearly three and a half years to resolve a storm damage home and contents claim.
  • Priorities: ASIC's 2026 enforcement priorities include claims and complaints handling failures by insurers, and misleading pricing.
  • Climate: APRA's guide CPG 229 sets out expectations for managing climate-related financial risks, which for general insurers show up as catastrophe losses and affordability. See Climate risk management.

The General Insurance Code of Practice

The current Code (updated in February 2025) sets commitments on sales, claims, complaints, financial hardship and vulnerable customers, including timeframes for responding to claims. An independent review in 2025 found the Code's timeframes hadn't driven broad compliance and that insurers' performance against key timeframes had deteriorated. A redrafted Code went to public consultation from 24 June to 21 July 2026. The draft proposes claim decisions within four months (twelve in some cases), automatic acceptance of home and motor claims left undecided after twelve months (with exceptions), and commitments that are legally enforceable in consumer insurance contracts once ASIC approves the Code. The Insurance Council planned to seek ASIC approval in October 2026, with a transition period of about two years. These are proposals until the new Code is approved and starts.

Worked example: a pricing promise review

A home insurer's website says: "Our loyalty discount rewards you every year you stay with us." A review finds:

  1. The discount is applied to new policies, but a system error means about 3% of renewing customers haven't received it for two years.
  2. Is it a breach? Likely: customers were charged more than the insurer promised, which can be misleading and a failure to act efficiently, honestly and fairly.
  3. Report: assess whether it is a reportable situation for ASIC (within 30 days).
  4. Remediate: refund affected customers with interest, fix the system, and check other discounts for similar problems.
  5. Learn: add regular reconciliations of promised discounts against what customers are charged.

Illustrative only. ASIC has taken action against several insurers for not delivering promised discounts.

Common weak spots

  • Pricing systems that don't deliver promised discounts, often hidden in complex algorithms.
  • Claims delays, especially in catastrophes and complex building claims.
  • Weak oversight of outsourced claims managers, assessors and builders. See Third-party risk.
  • Poor treatment of vulnerable customers and those in financial hardship.
  • Product terms customers don't understand, raising unfair contract terms and misleading conduct risks.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. How do we know the discounts and prices we promise are the ones customers actually get?
  2. How long do our claims take, and how did we perform in the last catastrophe?
  3. Could we handle a sudden surge of claims without breaching the Code or treating customers unfairly?
  4. How do we oversee the assessors, builders and claims managers who act for us?
  5. Are we ready for the new Code's enforceable timeframes?

Frequently asked questions

Is a home insurance claim a financial service?

Yes. Handling and settling insurance claims has been a financial service since the Hayne reforms, so insurers need a claims handling authorisation and must handle claims efficiently, honestly and fairly.

Is the Code of Practice law?

Not by itself today. Breaches can matter for the efficiently, honestly and fairly obligation and for AFCA decisions. The redrafted Code proposes that key commitments become enforceable in consumer contracts once ASIC approves it.

Who do customers complain to?

First to the insurer, then to AFCA if the complaint isn't resolved.
