Regulatory landscape map: who regulates what

Australia spreads regulation across several agencies. This page maps who does what, so you know which regulator cares about which part of your work.

Beginner About 10 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Financial services use a "twin peaks" model: APRA for financial soundness, ASIC for conduct and disclosure.
  • AUSTRAC (money laundering and terrorism financing), the OAIC (privacy) and the ACCC (competition and general consumer law) cut across many industries.
  • Most financial institutions deal with several regulators at once. A large super fund, for example, is regulated by APRA, ASIC, AUSTRAC and the OAIC.
  • The Reserve Bank, Treasury, the ATO and AFCA also play important roles, even though they aren't the main "risk and compliance" regulators for most firms.

The big picture

Parliament makes the laws. Government departments (mainly Treasury, for financial services) advise on policy. Independent regulators then administer and enforce the laws, often by making more detailed rules and publishing guidance. Courts and tribunals decide disputes and impose most serious penalties.

The main regulators at a glance

RegulatorFocusWho it regulatesKey laws (examples)
APRA
Australian Prudential Regulation Authority		 Financial safety: that institutions stay sound enough to keep their promises to depositors, policyholders and members. Banks, building societies and credit unions (ADIs); general, life and private health insurers; friendly societies; super funds other than SMSFs. Banking Act 1959; Insurance Act 1973; Life Insurance Act 1995; Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993. APRA makes binding prudential standards such as CPS 220 and CPS 230.
ASIC
Australian Securities and Investments Commission		 Conduct and disclosure: fair treatment of consumers and investors, fair and efficient markets. All companies; financial markets; AFS licensees (including banks, insurers and super trustees for their conduct); credit licensees; auditors and liquidators. Corporations Act 2001; ASIC Act 2001 (including consumer protection for financial services); National Consumer Credit Protection Act 2009.
AUSTRAC
Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre		 Stopping money laundering, terrorism financing and other serious crime; financial intelligence. "Reporting entities" that provide designated services, e.g. banks, remitters, casinos, digital currency exchanges. From 1 July 2026, also certain services by lawyers, accountants, real estate professionals and others. Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006 and the AML/CTF Rules.
OAIC
Office of the Australian Information Commissioner		 Privacy and access to government information. Australian Government agencies and most private organisations with annual turnover above $3 million, plus some smaller businesses (such as health service providers). Privacy Act 1988, including the Australian Privacy Principles and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme.
ACCC
Australian Competition and Consumer Commission		 Competition, fair trading and general consumer protection. Businesses across the economy. For financial services, most consumer protection sits with ASIC instead, but competition law still applies. Competition and Consumer Act 2010, including the Australian Consumer Law.

Other bodies worth knowing

  • Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA): sets monetary policy, oversees the payments system and promotes financial stability. It chairs the Council of Financial Regulators, a non-statutory group (RBA, APRA, ASIC and Treasury) that coordinates on financial system issues.
  • Treasury: advises the government on financial services policy and new laws.
  • Australian Taxation Office (ATO): regulates self-managed super funds (SMSFs), as well as administering tax and the super guarantee.
  • Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA): not a regulator, but the free external dispute resolution scheme for consumers and small businesses. Financial firms must be members by law or licence condition.
  • ASX: operates the main stock exchange and administers its Listing Rules. Its Corporate Governance Council publishes the governance principles that listed companies report against.

Why so many regulators?

After the 1997 Financial System Inquiry (the Wallis Inquiry), Australia moved from regulating by type of institution to regulating by objective: one regulator for financial safety (APRA), one for conduct and disclosure (ASIC), with competition policy in the ACCC. That is the origin of "twin peaks". Specialist issues such as money laundering and privacy have their own regulators because they affect many industries.
