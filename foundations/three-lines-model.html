The Three Lines model (and internal audit's role)

A simple way to describe who does what in risk management: the people who run the business, the specialists who support and challenge them, and the independent auditors who check it all.

Beginner About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • The Three Lines model is published by the Institute of Internal Auditors (IIA). Its current version dates from July 2020.
  • First line: the business, which owns and manages its risks. Second line: specialist risk and compliance functions that set frameworks, advise, monitor and challenge. Third line: internal audit, which gives independent assurance to the board.
  • The governing body (the board) sits above all three and is accountable to stakeholders.
  • The 2020 update moved away from "defence" language, stressed that first and second line roles are both part of management, and emphasised collaboration as well as independence.
  • The model is a way of thinking about roles, not a rigid org chart. Critics warn that it can blur accountability if applied mechanically.

The model in plain English

Picture a restaurant. The chefs and waiters cook and serve food, and they are responsible for doing it safely: storing food at the right temperature, washing their hands, checking for allergies. That is the first line.

The owner hires a food safety specialist who writes the kitchen's safety procedures, trains the staff, and does regular spot checks, pointing out problems and pushing for fixes. That is the second line.

Once a year, an independent inspector who doesn't work for the kitchen or the specialist reviews everything, including whether the specialist's checks are any good, and reports directly to the owner. That is the third line.

The owner, who sets the standards and is ultimately answerable to customers and the council, is the governing body.

The six principles

The 2020 model is built on six principles. In our own words:

  1. Governance. An organisation needs structures and processes for accountability, for action (including managing risk) and for independent assurance.
  2. Governing body roles. The board makes sure those structures exist, sets direction and delegates to management, and oversees internal audit.
  3. Management and first and second line roles. Management is responsible for achieving objectives and managing risk. The first line does this directly; the second line provides expertise, support, monitoring and challenge.
  4. Third line roles. Internal audit gives independent, objective assurance and advice on how well governance and risk management are working.
  5. Third line independence. Internal audit must be independent of management, with direct access to the governing body, to be credible.
  6. Creating and protecting value. All roles should work together, aligned with the interests of stakeholders.

What changed in 2020?

The model grew out of the earlier "three lines of defence", which the IIA promoted in a 2013 position paper and which became standard in banking. In July 2020 the IIA published an updated version, renamed the Three Lines Model.

Old "three lines of defence"2020 Three Lines Model
Defensive framing: protecting valueCreating and protecting value
Three separate "lines" with fairly rigid boundariesFirst and second line roles both sit within management and can blend in practice
Board and senior management shown alongside the linesGoverning body clearly on top, accountable to stakeholders
Emphasis on independence and separationIndependence of internal audit kept, plus collaboration and coordination between all roles

In practice, many Australian organisations (and regulators) still say "three lines of defence". The words matter less than being clear about who owns what.

Internal audit: the third line up close

Internal audit is an independent function inside the organisation that reviews whether governance, risk management and controls are working, and reports its findings to the board, usually through the audit committee. It differs from the second line in two key ways: it is independent of management, and it reviews the second line too.

  • Professional standards. The IIA's Global Internal Audit Standards took effect on 9 January 2025, replacing the previous 2017 framework.
  • Regulatory requirements. APRA's governance standards (CPS 510 and SPS 510) require regulated banks, insurers and super trustees to have an internal audit function with a reporting line to, and unrestricted access to, the board audit committee.
  • A risk-based plan. Internal audit usually sets a plan each year focused on the organisation's biggest risks, approved by the audit committee.
  • Assurance and advice. As well as audits, it can advise on new systems or projects, provided this doesn't compromise its independence.

Common pitfalls

"Risk is the risk team's job."

If the first line hands ownership of its risks to the second line, nobody who actually runs the process is accountable. The second line then drifts into doing first-line work and can no longer challenge objectively.

Duplication and "assurance fatigue".

Without coordination, the business can be reviewed by its own quality team, compliance monitoring, risk reviews and internal audit, all on the same topic. Many organisations build an "assurance map" to show who checks what and avoid gaps and overlaps.

Treating the lines as an org chart.

The model describes roles, not departments. One team can perform both first and second line activities. What matters is that the independence needed for challenge and assurance is preserved.

Accountability gets diffused.

Researchers have questioned how well-evidenced the model is and warned that spreading responsibility across three lines can make it less clear who is really accountable (Davies and Zhivitskaya, 2018). This is one reason accountability regimes such as the FAR focus on named individuals.

Worked example: who does what when a control fails

An illustrative example of the roles in practice:

LineRole in the example
First line (business)The payments team finds that a payment was released without a second approval. It stops further payments, reports the incident and fixes the cause.
Second line (risk and compliance)Reviews the incident, assesses whether it is a reportable breach, challenges the fix and checks similar controls elsewhere.
Third line (internal audit)Later audits the payments process independently and tells the audit committee whether controls now work.
Governing body (board)Receives reports from management and assurance, asks whether the response is enough, and holds people to account.

Frequently asked questions

Can one person work in two lines?

It happens in small organisations, but it weakens independence. The important thing is that someone independent of the activity provides challenge and assurance.

Check your understanding

Which line is it?

Choose the role that best fits each activity.

1. A claims team leader checks a sample of the team's claims decisions each week before they are paid.

This is a control run by the business as part of its own work, so it is first line, even though it is a "check".

2. The compliance team writes the organisation's breach management policy and trains staff on it.

Setting frameworks and policies and building capability is a classic second line role.

3. A team reports directly to the audit committee on whether the risk team's monitoring program is effective.

Independent assurance to the board, including over the second line, is internal audit's role.

4. The risk team challenges a business unit's rating of its own fraud risk as "low" and asks for evidence.

Monitoring and challenging the first line's risk assessments is second line.

5. The IT operations manager decides how often backups are taken and makes sure they run.

IT operations owns and runs this control, so it is first line.
