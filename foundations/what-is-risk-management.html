What is risk management?

Risk management is the discipline of thinking ahead about what could affect your goals, and deciding in advance what to do about it.

Beginner About 8 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • A risk is uncertainty that could affect what you are trying to achieve. It is always tied to an objective.
  • Risk management is not about removing all risk. It is about taking the right risks, knowingly, and keeping the rest at a level you can live with.
  • The core process is a loop: set the context, identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, then monitor and review, with communication running throughout.
  • There are only a handful of ways to respond to a risk: avoid it, reduce it, share it, accept it, or (for opportunities) pursue it.
  • In Australia, APRA-regulated banks, insurers and super funds are legally required to have a risk management framework (Prudential Standard CPS 220 or SPS 220).

Risk in plain English

Imagine you need to be at the airport by 9 am. Traffic might be heavy, your car might not start, the train might be cancelled. None of these is certain, but each could stop you reaching your goal. That uncertainty is risk.

Now imagine you check the traffic app the night before, leave 30 minutes early and keep a rideshare app on your phone as a backup. You have just done risk management: you thought about what could go wrong, judged which things mattered most, and put measures in place before anything happened.

Organisations do the same thing, just more formally and at a larger scale. The international risk management guideline ISO 31000 describes risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives. Three ideas are packed into that short phrase:

  • Objectives come first. A risk only exists in relation to something you are trying to achieve. "Cyber attack" is a threat; "a cyber attack stops members accessing their super accounts for three days" is a risk to a clear objective.
  • Uncertainty is the core. If something is certain to happen, it is a fact or an issue to be dealt with, not a risk.
  • Effects can be good or bad. Most day-to-day risk work focuses on the downside, but uncertainty also creates opportunities. A new product might succeed beyond expectations.
Three words that often get mixed up.
TermWhat it meansExample
RiskSomething that might happen and would affect objectives.Our payment system could fail on the day contributions are processed.
IssueA problem that exists now and needs fixing (often a risk that has "landed" or a control that isn't working).The backup system has not been tested for 18 months.
IncidentAn event that has happened and caused, or nearly caused, harm.The payment system failed on Tuesday and 4,000 contributions were delayed.

Why organisations manage risk

Good risk management helps an organisation:

  • Make better decisions. Leaders can weigh up the upside and downside of a choice with their eyes open.
  • Protect the people it serves. Customers, members, policyholders and staff are less likely to be harmed.
  • Avoid nasty surprises. Problems are spotted earlier, when they are cheaper and easier to fix.
  • Meet legal and regulatory expectations. For example, APRA requires the banks, insurers and super trustees it regulates to maintain a board-approved risk management framework, including a risk appetite statement, under Prudential Standards CPS 220 and SPS 220.
  • Build trust. Regulators, investors and the public place more confidence in organisations that can show they understand and control their risks.

Notice what is not on the list: eliminating risk. Every organisation must take some risk to do anything useful. A bank that never lends money has no credit risk, but it is not a bank. The aim is to take risks deliberately, within limits the board has agreed, which is the idea of risk appetite.

The risk management process

Most frameworks describe a similar cycle. The version below is our simplified description of the process in ISO 31000, which is widely used in Australia.

1. Set the context

Be clear about the objective, the scope (a whole organisation, one product, one project) and the criteria you will use to judge risks, such as what counts as a "major" consequence. Without agreed criteria, two people can look at the same risk and rate it completely differently.

2. Identify risks

Work out what could happen, why (the causes) and what the effects would be. Useful sources include workshops with the people who do the work, past incidents and near misses, audit findings, complaints, regulator publications and industry events. A good risk description links cause, event and consequence, for example: "Because key-person knowledge is not documented (cause), the unexpected loss of the fund accountant (event) could delay unit pricing and cause pricing errors for members (consequence)."

3. Analyse

Understand the risk better: how likely it is, how severe the consequences could be, and how well existing controls are working. Analysis can be simple (a rating on a five-point scale) or highly quantitative (statistical modelling of losses).

4. Evaluate

Compare the analysed risk with your criteria and risk appetite. Is it acceptable as it is, or does something need to change? This step turns analysis into a decision and a priority order.

5. Treat

Choose and carry out a response (see the options below), assign an owner and a due date, and check that the treatment does not create new risks of its own.

6. Monitor and review

Risks change. Check regularly whether controls are working, whether actions were completed, and whether anything new has appeared. Key risk indicators can give early warning between formal reviews.

Throughout: communicate, consult and record

Talk to the people who know the risks best, tell decision-makers what they need to know, and keep a record (usually a risk register) so decisions can be understood and checked later.

Ways to treat a risk

Once a risk has been assessed, there are only a few basic choices. Most real situations use a mix.

OptionWhat it meansSuperannuation example
AvoidStop or don't start the activity that creates the risk.Decide not to launch a complex investment option the fund can't administer reliably.
ReduceAdd or improve controls to make the event less likely or less severe.Introduce a second-person check on manual adjustments to member accounts.
Share or transferPass some of the financial impact to someone else, for example through insurance or a contract. Accountability usually stays with you.Take out professional indemnity insurance; include service-level credits in the administrator's contract.
AcceptConsciously live with the risk, because it is within appetite or treatment would cost more than it saves. This should be a documented decision by someone with authority.Accept a small risk of minor processing delays during a planned system upgrade.
PursueFor upside risk: take on more risk to chase an opportunity, within appetite.Invest in a new digital advice tool that could improve member outcomes, knowing it may not succeed.

Worked example: late contributions

Objective: allocate employer contributions to member accounts accurately and on time.

Risk: a manual file upload step fails without anyone noticing, so contributions sit unallocated and members miss investment returns.

Analysis: it has happened twice in three years (likely), and each time affected thousands of members (major consequence). Existing control: a weekly spot check, which would not catch a failure quickly.

Evaluation: outside the fund's risk appetite for member-impacting errors, so action is needed.

Treatment: automate a daily reconciliation between contributions received and contributions allocated, with an alert to the operations manager (reduce). Owner: Head of Operations. Due: end of quarter.

Monitor: track the number of unallocated contributions older than 3 days as a key risk indicator, reported monthly.

Who does risk management?

Everyone, but with different roles:

  • The board sets the risk appetite, approves the framework and oversees whether risk is being managed well.
  • Management and staff in the business own the risks in their area and run the controls day to day. This is the first line.
  • The risk management function designs the framework, provides tools and advice, and independently challenges the business. This is the second line.
  • Internal audit gives the board independent assurance that it is all working. This is the third line.

How these roles fit together is explained in The Three Lines model.

Common misunderstandings

"Risk management means filling in the risk register."

A register is a record, not the goal. Researchers have warned that risk management can turn into defensive paperwork that protects individuals from blame rather than improving decisions (Power, 2009). The test of good risk management is whether it changes what people actually do.

"The risk team manages the risks."

The risk function supports and challenges, but the people who run a process own its risks. Handing ownership to the risk team tends to weaken accountability.

"If a risk is rated low, we can ignore it."

Ratings are estimates and can be wrong, especially for rare but severe events. Low-rated risks still need occasional review, and several small risks can combine into a large one.

"Risk is always bad."

Uncertainty can also produce better-than-expected outcomes. Strategy is largely about choosing which risks to take on in pursuit of opportunities.
