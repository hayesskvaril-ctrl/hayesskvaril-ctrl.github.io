Core frameworks compared: ISO 31000, COSO and Three Lines

Four frameworks come up again and again in risk and governance. They are not competitors: each answers a different question, and most organisations draw on several.

Beginner About 10 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • ISO 31000 is short, general guidance on how to manage any kind of risk. It is not certifiable.
  • COSO ERM (2017) links risk management to strategy and performance at the whole-of-enterprise level.
  • COSO Internal Control (2013) focuses on the controls that give confidence in operations, reporting and compliance.
  • The Three Lines model describes who does what: management, specialist functions, internal audit and the board.
  • APRA's risk management standards don't require any particular external framework. Many Australian organisations use ISO 31000 language for their process and Three Lines for roles.

Why several frameworks?

A framework is a structured way of organising something complex. Risk frameworks exist so that an organisation doesn't have to invent its approach from scratch, and so that boards, auditors and regulators share a common language.

The four covered here were written by different bodies for different purposes, so they zoom in on different parts of the picture. The map below shows where each one sits.

A closer look at each

ISO 31000:2018 Risk management – Guidelines

The most widely referenced risk management guideline internationally. It has three parts, described here in our own words:

  • Principles describing what effective risk management looks like. In brief: it should be integrated into everything the organisation does; structured and comprehensive; customised to the organisation; inclusive of stakeholders; dynamic; based on the best available information; attentive to human and cultural factors; and continually improved.
  • Framework: how to embed risk management through leadership and commitment, integration, design, implementation, evaluation and improvement.
  • Process: the cycle of setting context, assessing risks (identify, analyse, evaluate), treating them, and monitoring, communicating and recording. See What is risk management?

ISO 31000 is deliberately generic, so it gives little detail on how to do things such as setting risk appetite. It defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives, which includes upside as well as downside.

COSO Enterprise Risk Management (2017)

COSO's ERM framework was substantially rewritten in 2017 to connect risk more closely with strategy. Its five components are:

  1. Governance and culture (5 principles)
  2. Strategy and objective-setting (4 principles)
  3. Performance: identifying, assessing, prioritising and responding to risks (5 principles)
  4. Review and revision (3 principles)
  5. Information, communication and reporting (3 principles)

Its distinctive message is that risk should be considered when strategy is chosen, not only when it is executed. It also gives more attention than ISO 31000 to risk appetite and culture.

COSO Internal Control – Integrated Framework (2013)

First published in 1992 and updated in 2013, this framework defines internal control and sets out what an effective system of control looks like. Its five components are:

  1. Control environment: integrity, ethical values, board oversight, structure, competence and accountability (principles 1–5)
  2. Risk assessment, including fraud risk (principles 6–9)
  3. Control activities, including technology controls and policies (principles 10–12)
  4. Information and communication (principles 13–15)
  5. Monitoring activities (principles 16–17)

Its famous "cube" diagram shows these components applying to three categories of objectives (operations, reporting and compliance) at every level of the organisation. It is the usual starting point for control design and testing.

The IIA Three Lines model (2020)

Rather than a process, this describes roles: a governing body accountable to stakeholders, management performing first and second line roles, and internal audit as the independent third line. See The Three Lines model for detail.

How they fit together in practice

Example: a mid-sized super trustee

  • Its risk management framework (required by APRA's SPS 220) uses ISO 31000-style language for the risk process: context, identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, monitor.
  • Its risk appetite statement and strategic planning draw on ideas from COSO ERM about linking risk to strategy.
  • Its control testing program uses COSO Internal Control concepts, such as testing both design and operating effectiveness.
  • Its roles and responsibilities are set out using the Three Lines model, with the board risk committee overseeing, the risk and compliance teams as second line, and internal audit as third line.

None of this is mandatory. APRA's standards set outcomes (for example, a board-approved framework that covers all material risks) rather than prescribing a framework. What matters is that the approach is coherent, fits the organisation and actually works.
