What is compliance?

Compliance means meeting your obligations, and being able to show that you have.

Beginner About 9 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Compliance is about meeting obligations: laws, regulations, licence conditions, codes, contracts and your own policies.
  • Obligations come in layers. Some are legally binding; others (like regulator guidance) aren't law but strongly signal what regulators expect.
  • A compliance program is a cycle: know your obligations, build them into how the business works, check they are being met, and fix and report failures.
  • Compliance is part of risk management. The risk that you fail to meet an obligation is called compliance risk.
  • "Box-ticking" is the classic failure mode. Good compliance focuses on outcomes for customers and the purpose behind the rule.

Compliance in plain English

Every organisation operates under rules. A café must follow food safety laws, pay staff correctly and not mislead customers about its prices. A super fund must also follow superannuation law, prudential standards set by APRA, disclosure rules enforced by ASIC and much more.

Compliance is the work of knowing which rules apply, making sure the organisation follows them in its day-to-day work, and being able to prove it. That last part matters: if a regulator asks "how do you know you are meeting this obligation?", "we think so" is not a good answer.

In a small business, compliance might be one person's part-time job. In a bank, insurer or super fund, there is usually a dedicated compliance team, but the responsibility for complying still sits with the people who run each part of the business.

Where obligations come from

Obligations come from many sources. It helps to think of them in layers, from binding law at the top to voluntary commitments at the bottom.

A few points trip newcomers up:

  • APRA prudential standards are law. They are legislative instruments, so a regulated entity must comply with them. APRA's prudential practice guides (with names like CPG 230) are guidance and are not binding in the same way.
  • ASIC regulatory guides are not law, but they explain how ASIC reads the law and what it will look for. Departing from them is risky and usually needs a good reason.
  • Codes can bind you. Subscribing to an industry code is voluntary, but once you do, its promises often form part of your contracts with customers.
  • Obligations change constantly. New laws, amended standards, court decisions and updated guidance all need to be tracked. This is often called regulatory change management.

How a compliance program works

Most compliance programs, whatever they are called, follow the same cycle. The international standard ISO 37301:2021 describes a formal compliance management system along similar lines, and ASIC's Regulatory Guide 104 refers to the processes a licensee uses to meet its obligations as "compliance measures".

  1. Identify obligations. Work out which laws, standards, licence conditions and codes apply, and record them in an obligations register. Break big obligations into specific, testable requirements.
  2. Assess and assign. Rate each obligation by the risk of getting it wrong (how likely, how harmful) and name an owner in the business.
  3. Embed controls. Build the obligation into processes, systems, templates, approvals and training so that meeting it is the normal way of working, not an extra step.
  4. Monitor and test. Check that controls are working, through the business's own checks, compliance monitoring and testing, and internal audit.
  5. Manage breaches. When something goes wrong, record it, assess its seriousness, decide if it must be reported to a regulator, fix the cause and put affected customers right (remediation).
  6. Report and improve. Tell the board and senior management how the organisation is tracking, report to regulators where required, and use lessons learned to strengthen controls.

In practice the three work closely together, and in smaller organisations they are often the same team. Compliance risk is one of the risk types that the risk management framework covers.

What happens when things go wrong?

Failing to comply can lead to consequences such as:

  • Regulatory action: infringement notices, court-imposed civil penalties, enforceable undertakings, extra licence conditions, or suspension or cancellation of a licence.
  • Remediation: refunding or compensating affected customers, often with interest. ASIC expects licensees to start remediation promptly and not wait for customers to complain (ASIC Regulatory Guide 277).
  • Reporting obligations: many breaches must be reported to a regulator within set timeframes. See Incident and breach management.
  • Reputational damage and loss of customer and investor trust.
  • Personal consequences for directors and executives, for example under the Financial Accountability Regime.

Beyond box-ticking

The 2019 Final Report of the Royal Commission into Misconduct in the Banking, Superannuation and Financial Services Industry identified six basic norms of conduct that sit behind the detailed rules: obey the law; do not mislead or deceive; act fairly; provide services that are fit for purpose; deliver services with reasonable care and skill; and, when acting for someone else, act in their best interests. It stressed complying with the spirit of the law, not just its letter. The lesson for compliance teams is that meeting the wording of a rule while missing its purpose is itself a risk.

Worked example: one obligation, end to end

Obligation: an AFS licensee must lodge a report with ASIC within 30 calendar days after it first knows, or is reckless about whether, there are reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen.

Owner: Head of Compliance (process); each business unit head (escalating potential breaches promptly).

Controls: an incident and breach procedure; a breach register with a date-of-awareness field; an automated alert at day 20; training for all staff on how to escalate.

Monitoring: quarterly check of every breach decision and lodgement date against the 30-day deadline.

Reporting: number of reportable situations, timeliness and themes reported to the board risk committee.
