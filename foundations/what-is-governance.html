What is governance?

Governance is how an organisation is directed, controlled and held to account: who decides, how decisions are checked, and who answers for the results.

Beginner About 9 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Governance is the system of direction and accountability that sits above day-to-day management.
  • The board sets direction and oversees; management runs the organisation. Confusing the two is a common source of trouble.
  • Authority flows down through delegations; accountability and information flow back up through reporting and independent assurance.
  • In Australia, governance rules come from the Corporations Act (directors' duties), the ASX Corporate Governance Principles for listed companies, APRA prudential standards and, for banks, insurers and super trustees, the Financial Accountability Regime.
  • Structures matter, but culture, meaning how people actually behave, is what makes governance work or fail.

Governance in plain English

Think of a local sports club. The members elect a committee. The committee decides what the club is trying to achieve, sets the budget and hires a coach. The coach runs training and picks the team. The treasurer reports to the committee each month, an independent person checks the books each year, and at the annual general meeting the committee answers to the members.

That is governance: a clear answer to who decides what, how decisions are checked, and who is accountable to whom. Large companies, super funds and government agencies do the same thing with more structure, but the questions are identical.

Board versus management

The single most important idea in governance is the difference between directing and overseeing (the board) and managing (the chief executive and their team). A common shorthand is that the board should have its "nose in, fingers out": curious and challenging, but not doing management's job.

How the pieces fit together

The building blocks of good governance

  • Clear purpose and strategy. Everyone should know what the organisation is for and whose interests it serves. For a super trustee, the law requires decisions to be made in members' best financial interests.
  • A capable, independent board. The right mix of skills, enough independent directors to challenge management, and regular renewal.
  • Board committees. Smaller groups of directors that look more deeply at audit, risk, remuneration and board nominations.
  • Delegations of authority. A written record of who can approve what (for example, spending limits), so decisions are made at the right level.
  • Policies and frameworks. Including the risk management framework, compliance framework and codes of conduct.
  • Clear accountability. Named individuals responsible for each key area, so it is obvious who answers when things go wrong.
  • Good information and reporting. Boards can only oversee what they can see. Reports should be accurate, timely and highlight what matters.
  • Independent assurance. Internal audit and external audit give the board an objective view.
  • Healthy culture. Tone at the top, the behaviours that are rewarded, and whether people feel safe to raise problems.

The Australian rulebook

There is no single "governance law" in Australia. Instead, several sources overlap:

SourceWho it applies toWhat it does, in brief
Corporations Act 2001 (Cth), directors' duties (ss 180–184)Directors and officers of companiesDuties to act with care and diligence, in good faith in the company's best interests and for a proper purpose, and not to misuse their position or information.
ASX Corporate Governance Council, Corporate Governance Principles and Recommendations (4th edition, 2019)ASX-listed entitiesRecommended practices. Listed entities must report against them each year and explain any they don't follow ("if not, why not").
APRA Prudential Standards CPS 510 and SPS 510 GovernanceBanks, insurers and super trustees regulated by APRABinding minimum governance requirements, including board composition, independence and board committees.
Financial Accountability Regime (FAR)Banks (from 15 March 2024), insurers and super trustees (from 15 March 2025)Accountability obligations for the entity and its most senior people, who must be registered as accountable persons. Jointly administered by APRA and ASIC.
Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 (Cth), covenantsSuper trustees and their directorsDuties built into every regulated fund's governing rules, including acting honestly, with care, skill and diligence, and in members' best financial interests.

Changes under way (as at September 2026)

  • APRA governance standards: APRA has been reviewing its governance requirements since a March 2025 discussion paper. On 16 June 2026 it released a draft new CPS 510 for consultation (closed 28 August 2026), with proposals including a 12-year tenure limit for non-executive directors. APRA has indicated a final standard by the end of 2026 and commencement in January 2028. Details may change.
  • ASX Principles, 5th edition: ASX released a consultation draft on 21 July 2026 (submissions closed 14 September 2026). Reporting against the new edition is expected to start with financial years ending on 30 June 2028 or 31 December 2028, depending on the entity's year end.

We'll update this page when these are finalised.

When governance fails

Governance failures rarely come from missing documents. More often the structures exist on paper but don't work in practice: boards get too much information and not enough insight, bad news is filtered on its way up, incentives reward the wrong behaviour, or nobody is clearly accountable.

Two Australian reference points are widely cited. APRA's Prudential Inquiry into the Commonwealth Bank of Australia (final report released 1 May 2018) examined the bank's governance, culture and accountability and made 35 recommendations. The following year, the Financial Services Royal Commission Final Report (2019) put culture, governance and remuneration at the centre of its analysis of misconduct across the industry. Both are worth reading for anyone serious about governance.
