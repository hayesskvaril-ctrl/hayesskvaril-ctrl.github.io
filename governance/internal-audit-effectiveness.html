Internal audit effectiveness: the research

What research says makes internal audit effective, whether it improves outcomes, and why independence is harder in practice than on paper.

Advanced About 21 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Research defines internal audit "effectiveness" in many different ways, which makes findings hard to compare.
  • Drivers of effectiveness include the competence and resources of the function, its independence and its relationship with the audit committee and management.
  • Studies link higher internal audit quality with better outcomes, such as less earnings management.
  • In practice, internal auditors often move between watchdog, helper and protector roles, and independence can be "grey".
  • Strong audit committee oversight is associated with internal audit spending more effort on internal control.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain how researchers define and measure internal audit effectiveness.
  2. Evaluate the evidence linking internal audit quality to organisational outcomes.
  3. Analyse threats to internal audit independence using Australian and international research.
  4. Critically assess the "serving two masters" tension between management and the audit committee.

The idea in plain English

Internal audit gives the board independent assurance that risk management, controls and governance work. Standards and regulators set out what a good function looks like. In Australia, APRA's governance standard requires regulated institutions to have an independent internal audit function, and the Institute of Internal Auditors sets global standards. But does internal audit actually improve outcomes, and what makes one function more effective than another? This page looks at the research. It builds on internal audit and the Global Internal Audit Standards.

Theory and research

What is "effective"?

Lenz and Hahn (2015) synthesised the empirical literature on internal audit effectiveness. They found researchers use many different definitions and measures, from auditees' satisfaction to the number of recommendations implemented, and grouped the drivers into macro, meso and micro factors. The lack of a shared definition is itself a finding: claims that internal audit "adds value" depend heavily on how value is measured.

Does internal audit quality improve outcomes?

Prawitt et al. (2009) measured internal audit quality using factors such as the function's experience, training, size and reporting lines, and found that higher quality was associated with less earnings management. This is some of the strongest evidence that internal audit affects outcomes the board cares about, though it is an association, not proof of cause, and it concerns financial reporting rather than operational or conduct risk.

Independence in practice

Standards call for internal audit to be independent of the activities it audits. Christopher et al. (2009) surveyed Australian chief audit executives and identified threats to independence, including management approving the audit plan and budget, and internal audit being used as a training ground for future managers. Roussy (2013) interviewed 42 internal auditors and stakeholders and found that auditors often act as helpers and protectors of top management rather than as watchdogs. She described their independence as "grey": neither fully present nor absent.

Serving two masters

Internal audit usually reports functionally to the audit committee but administratively to management. Abbott et al. (2010) found that when audit committees oversee internal audit more closely, the function spends more of its effort on internal control work. The finding suggests that who internal audit answers to shapes what it does, a tension also explored in research on the three lines model (Bantleon et al., 2021).

Applying the research: indicators an audit committee could use

Because research defines effectiveness in many ways, it helps to track several indicators rather than one:

IndicatorWhat it shows
Coverage of the top risks in the audit planWhether internal audit focuses on what matters most.
Repeat findingsWhether actions fix root causes.
Time to close agreed actionsWhether management takes findings seriously.
Results of the external quality assessmentConformance with professional standards.
Skills mix compared with the risk profileWhether the function can audit technical areas such as cyber and models.
Stakeholder feedback, read with careUseful, but a function that is always popular may not be challenging enough.

An interpretive application for discussion, not a finding of fact about any organisation.

Frequently asked questions

Can internal audit be too close to management?

Yes. The research describes internal auditors moving between watchdog and helper roles. Advisory work can add value, but too much can compromise independence.

Critical perspectives

  • Measurement problems. Without an agreed measure of effectiveness, studies can reach different conclusions about the same practices.
  • Survivor and self-report bias. Much evidence comes from surveys of chief audit executives, who have an interest in presenting their function well.
  • Consulting versus assurance. Advisory work can build relationships and influence, but research on the helper role suggests it can also erode independence.
  • Limits of assurance. Internal audit samples and reviews; it cannot guarantee that controls work everywhere, and boards can over-rely on a clean report.

Seminar questions

  1. Propose a way to measure internal audit effectiveness in a super fund. What would you measure, and what would your measure miss?
  2. Roussy describes internal auditors as helpers and protectors of management. Is this always a problem? When might it help?
  3. Using Christopher and colleagues' findings, design reporting lines and budget arrangements that protect internal audit independence.
  4. Prawitt and colleagues found an association between internal audit quality and less earnings management. What else could explain the association?

Further reading

  • Lenz, R., & Hahn, U. (2015). A synthesis of empirical internal audit effectiveness literature pointing to new research opportunities. Managerial Auditing Journal, 30(1), 5–33. https://doi.org/10.1108/MAJ-08-2014-1072 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews the empirical research on what makes internal audit effective, groups the drivers into macro, meso and micro factors, and notes that effectiveness itself is defined and measured in many different ways.
  • Prawitt, D. F., Smith, J. L., & Wood, D. A. (2009). Internal audit quality and earnings management. The Accounting Review, 84(4), 1255–1280. https://doi.org/10.2308/accr.2009.84.4.1255 Peer-reviewed
    Finds that higher internal audit function quality is associated with lower levels of earnings management, measured by abnormal accruals and by meeting analyst forecasts.
  • Roussy, M. (2013). Internal auditors' roles: From watchdogs to helpers and protectors of the top manager. Critical Perspectives on Accounting, 24(7–8), 550–571. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpa.2013.08.004 Peer-reviewed
    Based on 42 interviews in the public sector, finds internal auditors often act as protectors of and helpers to top management rather than independent watchdogs, a position the author calls 'grey' independence.
  • Abbott, L. J., Parker, S., & Peters, G. F. (2010). Serving two masters: The association between audit committee internal audit oversight and internal audit activities. Accounting Horizons, 24(1), 1–24. https://doi.org/10.2308/acch.2010.24.1.1 Peer-reviewed
    Finds that stronger audit committee oversight of internal audit is associated with internal audit spending more of its effort on internal control work, highlighting the tension between serving management and serving the audit committee.
  • Christopher, J., Sarens, G., & Leung, P. (2009). A critical analysis of the independence of the internal audit function: Evidence from Australia. Accounting, Auditing & Accountability Journal, 22(2), 200–220. https://doi.org/10.1108/09513570910933942 Peer-reviewed
    A survey of Australian chief audit executives identifying threats to internal audit independence, such as management approving the audit budget and plan, and internal audit being seen as a management 'partner' or a stepping stone.
