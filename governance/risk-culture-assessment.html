Assessing and changing risk culture

How boards and risk functions assess risk culture using several kinds of evidence, what APRA has found, and which interventions actually change behaviour.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Risk culture shows up in everyday behaviour, so no single measure captures it. Good assessments triangulate what people say, what they do and what the organisation has built.
  • Behavioural indicators such as self-identified incidents, escalation speed and overdue actions can signal culture, but always need interpretation.
  • CPS 220 requires boards to form a view of risk culture. APRA's surveys found decision-making and challenge, and responsibility and accountability, among the weakest dimensions.
  • Interventions that change behaviour include leadership example, psychological safety, balanced incentives, consistent consequences and trusted speak-up channels.
  • Culture change takes years, so track a few meaningful indicators over time.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why risk culture is hard to observe, and triangulate evidence from surveys, behaviours and outcomes.
  2. Choose behavioural indicators and survey questions that reveal how risk is actually handled.
  3. Use research on organisational culture and risk culture in financial institutions to interpret findings.
  4. Critically assess the limits of measuring and changing culture.

This page goes deeper than Culture and conduct, which introduces risk culture and APRA's Risk Culture 10 Dimensions. Here we look at how risk culture is assessed in practice, what APRA has found, and which interventions tend to change behaviour.

Why risk culture is hard to assess

  • It's mostly invisible. Culture shows up in everyday decisions, what people feel safe saying, and what happens when targets and risk collide.
  • Every source is biased. Surveys capture what people are willing to say; policies show intentions; metrics show outcomes but not causes.
  • It varies inside an organisation. Subcultures in different teams, locations or businesses can differ sharply from the corporate average.

The answer is triangulation: combining several kinds of evidence and looking for where they agree or conflict.

Triangulating the evidence

Behavioural indicators

Metrics can't measure culture directly, but patterns in them can signal it. An illustrative set, with how each might be read:

Illustrative indicators. Interpretation always needs context: a rise in reported incidents can mean better reporting, not more problems.

Surveys that tell you something

  • Ask about behaviour, not values. "In the last six months, have you seen a risk concern ignored?" is more revealing than "Do we value risk management?"
  • Protect anonymity and only report results for groups large enough that individuals can't be identified.
  • Compare across teams and over time, and follow up surprising results with focus groups.
  • Close the loop. Tell staff what was found and what will change, or response rates and honesty will fall.

What APRA has found

CPS 220 requires boards to form a view of their entity's risk culture and identify any desirable changes. APRA assesses risk culture using its Risk Culture 10 Dimensions, and has run risk culture surveys of staff at regulated entities. In its 2021 pilot with ten general insurers, around 11,600 staff were invited to respond. The three lowest-scoring dimensions were risk governance and controls, decision-making and challenge, and responsibility and accountability. APRA then extended the survey to banks, insurers and super funds.

Interventions that change behaviour

LeverExamples
LeadershipLeaders visibly welcome bad news, admit their own mistakes, and make risk part of business decisions, not an afterthought.
Psychological safetyResearch on teams (Edmondson, 1999) found that when people feel safe to speak up, they learn from errors more readily. Practical steps include thanking people for raising issues and blameless incident reviews.
IncentivesBalanced scorecards and risk adjustment of variable pay, as required for APRA-regulated entities by CPS 511. See Remuneration governance.
ConsequencesConsistent, visible consequences for poor risk behaviour, regardless of seniority, including under the FAR.
Structures and processesClear accountabilities, second-line challenge with real authority, and processes that make the right thing the easy thing.
Speak-up channelsTrusted, protected channels that lead to visible action. See Whistleblower protections.

Culture change is slow. Boards should expect to track a small set of indicators over several years, and to look for changes in behaviour rather than in policy documents.

Worked example: triangulating one dimension

Assessing the dimension "speaking up and challenge" from several sources:

EvidenceWhat it shows (illustrative)
Survey78% agree "I can challenge decisions without fear".
BehaviourFew documented challenges in risk committee minutes; the risk function rarely escalates disagreements.
OutcomesSeveral recent incidents were known to staff before they were reported.
Interviews and focus groupsStaff describe challenge as welcome in principle but career-limiting in practice.
ConclusionThe survey overstates psychological safety. The board's view of risk culture should say so, and set out what will change.

Frequently asked questions

How often should risk culture be assessed?

CPS 220 requires the board to form a view of risk culture; it doesn't set a frequency for formal assessments. Many entities do an in-depth assessment every few years, with indicators tracked in between.

Who should do the assessment?

Often the risk function, sometimes with external help. Independence helps: people being assessed shouldn't design or interpret the assessment alone.

Theory and research

Culture has layers

Schein (1990) described culture at three levels: visible artefacts (policies, structures, dashboards), espoused values (what people say they believe) and basic underlying assumptions (what people take for granted about how things really work). The deepest level drives behaviour and is the hardest to see. This is why a risk culture assessment that relies on policies and survey answers alone mostly measures the first two levels, and why this page recommends triangulating with behaviour and outcomes.

Risk culture in financial institutions

After the global financial crisis, regulators made risk culture a supervisory focus. Palermo et al. (2017) studied this in UK finance over four years and found that "risk culture" meant different things to different actors, because institutions were trying to balance a logic of risk-taking with a logic of precaution. That uncertainty about ends made it hard to agree on the means of assessing or changing culture. Australian research gives more direct evidence of effects: surveying bank staff, Sheedy and Griffin (2018) found that favourable risk culture together with effective risk structures was associated with more desirable and less undesirable risk behaviour, and that structures alone were not enough.

Culture also appears persistent. Fahlenbrach et al. (2012) found that banks that performed badly in the 1998 crisis were more likely to perform badly and fail in the 2007–08 crisis, consistent with an enduring risk culture or business model. Psychological safety, the belief that it is safe to speak up (Edmondson, 1999), is one of the most studied mechanisms linking culture to behaviour.

Critical perspectives

  • Can culture be measured at all? Some researchers argue that culture is something organisations are, not something they have, and resists measurement. Assessments are best treated as structured conversation starters, not scores.
  • Surveys measure what people will say. Staff may answer strategically, especially if results are linked to managers' performance. Behavioural indicators help but have their own gaps.
  • Persistence cuts both ways. If culture is persistent, as Fahlenbrach and colleagues suggest, it may be harder to change than frameworks imply, and change may take years.
  • Culture can be a scapegoat. Blaming "culture" for a failure can avoid naming specific decisions, incentives and people. Good assessments link cultural findings to concrete practices.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Schein's three levels, classify five sources of evidence in a risk culture assessment. Which reach the underlying assumptions?
  2. Sheedy and Griffin found culture and structures together mattered. Design an assessment that would show whether a weakness is cultural or structural.
  3. Palermo and colleagues found risk culture meant different things to different people. How would you agree a working definition with a board?
  4. What behavioural indicators could reveal low psychological safety in a risk or compliance team?

    Defines organisational culture in three levels, visible artefacts, espoused values and underlying assumptions, and argues that the deepest level is what drives behaviour and is hardest to change.

    Based on four years of fieldwork in UK finance, shows how 'risk culture' became an object of regulation and management, and how conflicting goals of risk-taking and precaution made it hard to define and change.
