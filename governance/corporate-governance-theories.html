Corporate governance theories: agency, stewardship and stakeholder

The main theories of why boards exist and what they should do, the evidence for each, and what they mean for Australian boards and super trustees.

Advanced About 23 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Agency theory sees governance as controlling managers whose interests differ from owners'; it underpins independent boards, incentives and disclosure.
  • Stewardship theory argues many managers act in the organisation's interest, so governance should also empower and trust.
  • Stakeholder theory holds that companies are accountable to more than shareholders; super trustees already owe duties to members, not owners.
  • Resource dependence theory sees boards as providers of expertise, networks and legitimacy, not only monitors.
  • Evidence supports parts of each theory; board practices depend on context, which is why "one size fits all" governance codes are contested.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain agency, stewardship, stakeholder and resource dependence theories of corporate governance.
  2. Apply each theory to board structure, incentives and oversight, including in super trustee boards.
  3. Evaluate the empirical evidence for each theory.
  4. Critically assess which theory best fits profit-for-member super funds.

The idea in plain English

Why do organisations have boards, independent directors, audit committees and incentive pay? The answers depend on assumptions about people and organisations. If managers can't be trusted to act for owners, you need monitoring and incentives. If they can, you need empowerment and support. If organisations serve many groups, boards must balance them. This page compares the theories that answer these questions and the evidence behind them. It builds on What is governance? and board structure and accountability.

Theory and research

Agency theory

Jensen and Meckling (1976) defined the firm as a set of contracts in which owners (principals) employ managers (agents). Because managers don't bear the full cost of their decisions, their interests can diverge from owners', creating agency costs: monitoring by owners, bonding by managers and the residual loss that remains. Fama and Jensen (1983) explained how organisations deal with this by separating decision management (initiating and implementing decisions) from decision control (ratifying and monitoring them), with the board at the top of the control system. They applied the analysis to corporations and also to mutuals and not-for-profits, which is relevant to super. Agency theory underpins independent directors, separate chair and CEO roles, audit committees, incentive pay and disclosure.

Stewardship theory

Stewardship theory starts from a different view of human motivation. Davis et al. (1997) argued that many managers are motivated by achievement, responsibility and identification with the organisation, and act as stewards of its interests. On this view, heavy monitoring and narrow incentives can undermine the very motivation governance relies on, and structures should empower as well as control. An early Australian test by Donaldson and Davis (1991) compared the two theories on whether the CEO should also chair the board: the results did not support agency theory and gave some support to stewardship theory.

Stakeholder theory

Donaldson and Preston (1995) distinguished three versions of stakeholder theory: descriptive (companies do in fact attend to many groups), instrumental (attending to stakeholders improves performance) and normative (stakeholders' interests matter in their own right). They argued the normative version is fundamental. In Australia, directors' duties are owed to the company, but debate continues about how far the company's interests include employees, customers and the community.

Resource dependence

Hillman and Dalziel (2003) integrated agency theory with resource dependence theory. Boards monitor, but they also provide resources: expertise, advice, networks and legitimacy. Directors' human and social capital ("board capital") affects both functions. This helps explain board skills matrices and why regulators look at the collective capability of a board, not just its independence.

TheoryView of managersRole of the boardTypical governance tools
AgencySelf-interested; need monitoringMonitor and control on owners' behalfIndependence, incentives, audit, disclosure
StewardshipMotivated to serve the organisationSupport and empowerShared roles, trust, intrinsic motivation
StakeholderAccountable to many groupsBalance competing interestsStakeholder engagement, broader reporting
Resource dependenceNeed external resourcesProvide expertise, networks, legitimacySkills mix, board capital

Governance in super funds

Super trustees don't have shareholders in the usual sense: they hold assets on trust for members and must act in members' best financial interests. Agency theory still applies, because members are principals who can't easily monitor trustees, and conflicts of interest are a recurring issue. Australian evidence suggests agency costs matter. Coleman et al. (2006) found that not-for-profit funds significantly outperformed for-profit funds over seven years to 2002, a difference they linked to higher agency costs in for-profit funds. For banks, research on risk governance supports independent risk oversight: banks whose chief risk officer reported directly to the board did better in the financial crisis (Aebi et al., 2012).

Applying the theory: a super trustee board decides on a merger

Each theory draws attention to different parts of the same decision:

TheoryWhat it highlights in the decision
Agency theoryExecutives may favour a merger that keeps their roles, or oppose one that doesn't. The board needs independent analysis of members' interests.
Stewardship theoryExecutives may genuinely seek the best outcome for members; the board should draw on their expertise rather than only monitor.
Stakeholder theoryTrustees owe duties to members, not shareholders. The best financial interests duty frames whose interests count.
Resource dependence theoryDirectors' skills and networks matter: does the board have merger, investment and administration expertise?

An interpretive application for discussion, not a finding of fact about any organisation.

Frequently asked questions

Which theory is right?

None fully. Each explains some behaviour well. Good governance design usually combines monitoring (agency), trust and empowerment (stewardship), and attention to all affected parties (stakeholder).

Critical perspectives

  • Assumptions about people drive the conclusions. Agency and stewardship theory start from different views of motivation. Both are partly true, which suggests governance should be designed for a mix of motivations.
  • Board practices are hard to test. Board composition and actions are jointly determined, so studies struggle to show that a given practice causes better performance (Adams et al., 2010).
  • Codes may impose one model. Governance codes built largely on agency theory may not suit every organisation, for example mutuals or profit-for-member funds.
  • Stakeholder accountability can blur. Critics argue that being accountable to everyone can mean being accountable to no one. Supporters argue that shareholder primacy ignores real harms to others.

Seminar questions

  1. Using agency theory, explain why the Hayne Royal Commission focused on conflicts of interest in super. Which agency costs were most visible?
  2. Donaldson and Davis found some support for combining the CEO and chair roles. Why do Australian governance codes nevertheless recommend separating them?
  3. Which theory best describes the governance of a profit-for-member super fund? Justify your answer.
  4. How would resource dependence theory change the way you design a board skills matrix?
  5. Adams and colleagues argue board practices and performance are jointly determined. Design a study that could test whether independent directors improve outcomes.

Further reading

  • Jensen, M. C., & Meckling, W. H. (1976). Theory of the firm: Managerial behavior, agency costs and ownership structure. Journal of Financial Economics, 3(4), 305–360. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304405X7690026X Peer-reviewed
    The foundation of agency theory in corporate governance: when managers act for owners, their interests diverge, creating agency costs of monitoring, bonding and residual loss.
  • Davis, J. H., Schoorman, F. D., & Donaldson, L. (1997). Toward a stewardship theory of management. Academy of Management Review, 22(1), 20–47. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1997.9707180258 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out stewardship theory as an alternative to agency theory: managers can be motivated to act in the organisation's interest, so governance should empower as well as control.
  • Donaldson, T., & Preston, L. E. (1995). The stakeholder theory of the corporation: Concepts, evidence, and implications. Academy of Management Review, 20(1), 65–91. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9503271992 Peer-reviewed
    Distinguishes descriptive, instrumental and normative versions of stakeholder theory and argues the normative base, why stakeholders' interests matter in their own right, is fundamental.
  • Hillman, A. J., & Dalziel, T. (2003). Boards of directors and firm performance: Integrating agency and resource dependence perspectives. Academy of Management Review, 28(3), 383–396. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2003.10196729 Peer-reviewed
    Integrates two board roles, monitoring management and providing resources such as expertise and networks, and argues that directors' human and social capital affects both.
