Whistleblower protections

Whistleblowers often see problems long before auditors or regulators do. The law protects them so that misconduct comes to light, and organisations must make it safe to speak up.

Beginner About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Part 9.4AAA of the Corporations Act, strengthened from 1 July 2019, protects eligible whistleblowers who report disclosable matters to eligible recipients.
  • Protections include confidentiality of identity, protection from detriment, and immunity from liability for making the disclosure. Disclosures can be anonymous.
  • Personal work-related grievances are generally not covered, unless they also involve something wider.
  • Public companies, large proprietary companies and super trustee companies must have a whistleblower policy (ASIC guidance: RG 270).
  • Calls for a dedicated Whistleblower Protection Authority continue; a 2025 bill to create one did not proceed.

Why protect whistleblowers?

Many major corporate scandals were first raised internally by staff who were ignored or punished. People who speak up can face job loss, harassment or legal threats. Without protection, most will stay silent. Protection laws aim to change that calculation, and a strong speak-up culture is one of the best early-warning systems an organisation can have.

How the protections work

What can be reported?

A disclosable matter is information that the whistleblower has reasonable grounds to suspect concerns misconduct, or an improper state of affairs or circumstances, in relation to the company or a related company. It includes conduct that breaches the Corporations Act, ASIC Act, Banking Act, Insurance Act, Life Insurance Act, SIS Act and other specified laws, offences punishable by 12 months' imprisonment or more, and conduct that represents a danger to the public or the financial system. The whistleblower doesn't need to be right, only to have reasonable grounds.

Personal work-related grievances, such as a dispute with a manager about a promotion, are generally not covered, and are handled under employment law instead. They can be covered if they also involve a disclosable matter or the person has suffered detriment for speaking up.

Going public: emergency and public interest disclosures

Disclosures to journalists or members of parliament are protected only in narrow circumstances. Broadly, the whistleblower must first have reported to ASIC, APRA or another prescribed body, and then either waited at least 90 days without reasonable action being taken (a public interest disclosure) or face a substantial and imminent danger to health, safety or the environment (an emergency disclosure), and must give the regulator written notice before going public. The conditions are strict, so people considering this often seek legal advice.

The protections

ProtectionWhat it means
ConfidentialityIt is generally illegal to disclose the whistleblower's identity, or information likely to identify them, without consent, except to ASIC, APRA, the AFP or a lawyer. Disclosures can be made anonymously.
Protection from detrimentIt is an offence, and grounds for compensation, to cause or threaten detriment (dismissal, demotion, harassment, damage to reputation or property) because someone made, or might make, a protected disclosure.
ImmunityThe whistleblower is protected from civil, criminal and administrative liability for making the disclosure. This doesn't protect them from liability for their own misconduct revealed in it.
RemediesCourts can order compensation, reinstatement, injunctions and apologies.

Separate schemes apply to tax whistleblowers (to the ATO, under the Taxation Administration Act 1953) and to Commonwealth public sector whistleblowers (under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013).

Whistleblower policies

Public companies, large proprietary companies and corporate trustees of APRA-regulated super funds must have a whistleblower policy and make it available to officers and employees (Corporations Act s 1317AI). ASIC's Regulatory Guide 270 sets out what the policy must cover, including:

  • the protections available and to whom and how disclosures can be made;
  • how the company will support whistleblowers and protect them from detriment;
  • how disclosures will be investigated;
  • how the company will ensure fair treatment of employees mentioned in disclosures; and
  • how the policy will be made available.

Speak-up program health check

Is reform coming?

Many commentators argue that the corporate regime is complex and that whistleblowers still struggle to enforce their protections. In February 2025, crossbench members introduced bills to create a Whistleblower Protection Authority. A Senate committee inquired into the Senate bill, but it did not proceed after the 2025 federal election. Separate reforms to public sector whistleblowing law are continuing. We'll update this page if the corporate regime changes.

Check your understanding

Is it likely to be protected?

1. A former employee of a super trustee tells APRA they believe fees were being charged to members for services not provided.

A former employee is an eligible whistleblower, suspected breaches of financial services and super law are disclosable matters, and APRA is an eligible recipient.

2. An employee complains to HR that their manager unfairly gave them a low performance rating, with nothing more.

This is a personal work-related grievance, generally dealt with under employment processes rather than the whistleblower regime.

3. An employee who suspects fraud goes straight to a journalist without contacting any regulator first.

Public interest and emergency disclosures require a prior disclosure to a regulator and other conditions to be met.
