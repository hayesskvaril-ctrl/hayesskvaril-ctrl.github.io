Reasonable steps and consequence management

What "reasonable steps" means for accountable persons under FAR, how to evidence it before you need to, and how to decide consequences fairly when things go wrong.

Advanced About 13 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Accountable persons must take reasonable steps to prevent matters that would harm the entity's prudential standing or reputation (FAR Act s 21).
  • Reasonable steps include appropriate governance, control and risk management, safeguards against inappropriate delegation, and procedures to identify and remediate problems (s 22).
  • The standard is objective and not judged with hindsight, but it must be evidenced: records of information received, questions asked and actions taken.
  • Entities must be able to reduce accountable persons' variable pay for failures, and CPS 511 requires malus and, for large entities, clawback.
  • Fair consequence management needs independent fact-finding, individual assessment, procedural fairness, consistency and credit for self-reporting.

The idea in plain English

Accountability regimes don't make executives guarantee that nothing goes wrong. They require them to take reasonable steps to prevent problems in their areas, and to be able to show what they did. When something does go wrong, the questions become: what did this person know, what did they do about it, and was that reasonable? And separately: what consequences should follow, for whom, and how are they decided fairly?

This page looks at what "reasonable steps" means under the Financial Accountability Regime, how to evidence it, and how to run consequence management that is fair, consistent and credible.

What the law says

Under s 21 of the Financial Accountability Regime Act 2023, an accountable person must, in conducting their responsibilities, act with honesty and integrity and with due skill, care and diligence; deal with the regulators in an open, constructive and cooperative way; and take reasonable steps to prevent matters arising that would adversely affect the entity's prudential standing or prudential reputation.

Section 22 says that, without limiting what reasonable steps are, they include:

  1. having appropriate governance, control and risk management in relation to the matter;
  2. having safeguards against inappropriate delegations of responsibility; and
  3. having appropriate procedures for identifying and remediating problems that arise or may arise.

The standard is objective: what a reasonable person in that role, with those responsibilities and that information, would have done. It is not judged with hindsight, and it depends on the size and complexity of the area.

What "reasonable steps" looks like in practice

Mapping the three statutory elements to evidence (illustrative)
Element (s 22)What it looks likeEvidence an accountable person could point to
Governance, control and risk managementClear structures, risk assessments, controls tested, information flowing up.Terms of reference, risk and control registers, control testing results, management information reviewed and acted on.
Safeguards against inappropriate delegationDelegations match capability; delegated work is monitored; nothing important is delegated and forgotten.Delegation registers, reporting lines, reports back from delegates and challenge of them.
Identifying and remediating problemsIncidents and issues surface quickly; fixes happen and are checked.Incident and issue registers, escalation records, overdue action reviews, validation of closed actions.
Acting on red flags (all three)Warning signs lead to enquiry and action, not just noting.Minutes and emails showing questions asked, decisions made and follow-up.

The courts' approach to directors' and officers' duties points the same way: in the 2026 Star Entertainment case, senior executives were found to have breached their duty of care by failing to act on and escalate serious risks. See directors' and officers' duties: key cases.

Consequence management

When things go wrong, organisations need to decide what consequences follow for individuals. Under FAR, entities must be able to reduce an accountable person's variable remuneration where they fail to meet their obligations, and at least 40% of it must be deferred for at least four years. CPS 511 separately requires APRA-regulated entities to be able to adjust variable pay for poor risk and conduct outcomes, through in-period adjustments, malus and, for significant financial institutions, clawback.

The hard questions

Individual or collective?
Some failures are nobody's individual fault but everyone's collective one, such as a culture that tolerated workarounds. Collective adjustments (for example, reducing a whole executive team's variable pay) can be appropriate, but they must not replace looking at individual conduct.
Seniority and knowledge
A senior executive who didn't know about a problem may still have failed to take reasonable steps to find out. A junior employee who knew but was ignored when they raised it may deserve no consequence at all, or recognition.
Consistency
Similar conduct should attract similar consequences, whatever the person's seniority or performance. Keep a register of decisions so consistency can be checked.
Speaking up
If people who self-report mistakes are punished as harshly as those who hide them, reporting stops. Credit for early disclosure supports a healthy culture.
Leavers
Deferral, malus and clawback exist partly so that consequences can still apply after someone leaves, when the problem emerges later.

Check your understanding

1. Under s 22 of the FAR Act, reasonable steps include:

Section 22 lists these three elements, without limiting what reasonable steps are.

2. How is 'reasonable steps' judged?

It is an objective standard, not judged by outcomes alone.

3. Why should consequence management give credit for self-reporting?

Punishing disclosure as harshly as concealment discourages speaking up.
