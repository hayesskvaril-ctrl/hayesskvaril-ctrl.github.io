Board structure and accountability

The board sits at the top of every company and super trustee. This page covers who is on it, what the law expects of them, and how accountability flows through the organisation.

Beginner About 14 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Boards are made up of executive and non-executive directors. Independent directors are non-executives free of relationships that could compromise their judgement.
  • Every director owes legal duties, including care and diligence, good faith, and not misusing their position or information (Corporations Act ss 180–184). Super trustee directors also have duties under the SIS Act.
  • For banks and insurers, APRA's CPS 510 requires a majority of independent directors, an independent chair, and board audit, risk and remuneration committees. A new consolidated CPS 510 is proposed to start in 2028.
  • The board delegates authority to management but can't delegate its accountability. Clear delegations and good information are how it keeps control.
  • A board is only as good as its mix of skills, independence, challenge and information, which is why boards use skills matrices and regular evaluations.

Who is on a board?

RoleWhat it means
ChairLeads the board, sets its agenda, runs meetings, and is the main link between the board and the CEO. In APRA-regulated entities the chair must usually be independent.
Executive directorA director who is also an employee, typically the CEO (often called the managing director).
Non-executive director (NED)A director who is not part of management. Brings an outside view and oversight.
Independent directorA NED who is free of business or other relationships (for example with a major shareholder, supplier or the group) that could materially interfere with their independent judgement.
Company secretaryNot a director, but responsible for supporting board processes, records and compliance with corporate law.

In super, some trustee boards have historically included directors nominated by employer and member organisations under "equal representation" rules in the SIS Act. Regardless of how they are appointed, all directors owe their duties to the fund's members.

Directors' duties

Key duties under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth). Directors also have equivalent duties under general law.
SectionDuty in plain English
s 180Act with the care and diligence a reasonable person would in the same position. A "business judgment rule" protects honest, informed, rational business decisions.
s 181Act in good faith in the best interests of the company and for a proper purpose.
s 182Don't improperly use your position to gain an advantage or cause detriment.
s 183Don't improperly use information obtained as a director.
s 184Serious, dishonest or reckless breaches of the above can be criminal offences.
ss 191 and 195Disclose material personal interests; in public companies, generally don't vote or be present when the matter is considered. See Conflicts of interest.
s 588GDon't let the company trade while insolvent.

Super trustees: the SIS Act builds covenants into every regulated fund's rules, for both the trustee and its directors. They include acting honestly, exercising care, skill and diligence as a prudent superannuation trustee would, acting in members' best financial interests, and giving priority to members' interests where there's a conflict.

Proposed changes: draft CPS 510 (June 2026)

APRA's draft standard would consolidate CPS 510, SPS 510, CPS 520, SPS 520 and SPS 521 into one cross-industry governance standard for banks, insurers and super trustees. Proposals include a 12-year tenure limit for non-executive directors, stricter entity-by-entity assessment of independence (including for directors who also sit on a parent board), a minimum of five directors (extending to super trustees) and strengthened conflicts requirements. Consultation closed on 28 August 2026; APRA has said it expects to finalise the standard by the end of 2026 for commencement in January 2028. The final rules may differ.

How accountability flows

The main tools for making accountability real are:

  • Board charter: what the board does itself and what it delegates.
  • Committee charters: the scope and authority of each committee.
  • Delegations of authority: who can approve what, up to what limits.
  • Accountability statements and maps: for banks, insurers and super trustees under the Financial Accountability Regime, a documented, regulator-registered record of who is accountable for what.
  • Board reporting: timely, accurate and concise information, including on risk and compliance, so that directors can actually oversee.

What makes a board effective?

  • The right mix of skills and experience, tracked through a skills matrix and refreshed through planned renewal.
  • Independence of mind: willingness to challenge management, not just independence on paper.
  • Good information: ASIC's 2019 review of seven large financial services companies (Report 631) found that the boards reviewed were challenged by key elements of non-financial risk management and that their oversight of these risks was less mature than required. Board papers should give insight, not just volume.
  • Time and focus: enough time on strategy and risk, not just reviewing past results.
  • Regular evaluation: of the board, committees and individual directors, sometimes with external facilitation.
