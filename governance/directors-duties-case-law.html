Directors' and officers' duties: key Australian cases

What the courts have said the duties of care and diligence really require, from AWA to Star Entertainment, and what that means for boards, executives and the people who report to them.

Advanced About 16 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Section 180 requires the care and diligence of a reasonable person in the same position. Courts have given this content through a line of cases.
  • Directors can delegate and rely on others, but must still understand matters central to their role and act on red flags (Centro).
  • Senior executives such as a general counsel or chief risk officer can be officers with personal duties (James Hardie, Star Entertainment).
  • Exposing the company to a foreseeable risk of serious harm from contraventions can itself breach the duty of care (Storm Financial).
  • In 2026 the Federal Court found Star's former CEO and Chief Legal and Risk Officer breached s 180 by failing to act on and escalate serious risks. The non-executive directors were not liable.

The idea in plain English

The Corporations Act sets out directors' and officers' duties in a few short sections. What those words actually demand has been worked out by the courts, case by case. For anyone who reports to a board, designs governance or advises directors, the cases are where the real standard lives.

The recurring theme is simple to state and hard to live up to: directors and senior officers must understand what is put in front of them, ask questions when something doesn't add up, and act on serious risks. Delegation and reliance are allowed, but they don't switch off personal responsibility. This page builds on Board structure and accountability.

The statutory duties

Core duties of directors and other officers (Corporations Act unless stated)
DutyIn brief
s 180 Care and diligenceExercise powers and discharge duties with the care and diligence a reasonable person would exercise in the same position and circumstances. Subject to the business judgment rule in s 180(2).
s 181 Good faithAct in good faith in the best interests of the company and for a proper purpose.
ss 182 and 183 Position and informationDon't improperly use your position, or information obtained through it, to gain an advantage or cause detriment to the company.
s 588G Insolvent tradingDirectors must prevent the company incurring debts while insolvent (with safe harbour provisions).
SIS Act s 52A (super)Directors of a super trustee company must, among other things, exercise care, skill and diligence, act in the best financial interests of members, and give priority to members' interests in a conflict.
Financial Accountability RegimeAccountable persons in banks, insurers and super trustees must act with honesty and integrity, due skill, care and diligence, deal openly with regulators and take reasonable steps to prevent contraventions. See FAR.

"Officer" is broader than "director". It includes people who make, or participate in making, decisions that affect the whole or a substantial part of the business. As the cases below show, that can capture a general counsel, a chief risk officer or a CFO.

Thirty years of cases, in order

  1. 1995Daniels v Anderson (AWA): directors must take reasonable steps to guide and monitor management. Non-executive directors can rely on others, but not blindly.
  2. 2011ASIC v Healey (Centro): directors who approved financial statements that misclassified billions in debt breached their duties. They had to read, understand and think about the accounts themselves.
  3. 2012ASIC v Hellicar and Shafron v ASIC (James Hardie): the High Court found non-executive directors breached their duty by approving a misleading market announcement, and that the general counsel and company secretary was an officer who also breached his duty.
  4. 2016 to 2020ASIC v Cassimatis (Storm Financial): directors breached s 180 by exposing the company to a foreseeable risk of serious harm from inappropriate advice being given to vulnerable clients. The Full Federal Court upheld this in 2020.
  5. 2026ASIC v Bekier (Star Entertainment): the former CEO and Chief Legal and Risk Officer breached s 180 by failing to act on or escalate serious AML/CTF and related risks and to keep the board informed. Claims against the non-executive directors failed. Penalties and disqualifications followed in June 2026.

What each case teaches

Principles in our own words; read the judgments for precise holdings
CasePrincipleWhat it means in practice
Daniels v Anderson (1995) 37 NSWLR 438Directors have a core, irreducible duty to become familiar with the business and monitor management.Board papers and risk reports must let directors actually monitor, not just receive, information.
ASIC v Healey [2011] FCA 717Directors may delegate and rely on advisers, but must still apply their own minds to matters central to their role, such as the financial statements."Management and the auditor signed off" isn't a complete answer. Directors need the literacy to spot obvious problems.
ASIC v Hellicar [2012] HCA 17Approving a document or announcement that is materially misleading can breach the duty of care.Board minutes matter: the court relied on them to find what the board approved.
Shafron v ASIC [2012] HCA 18A general counsel and company secretary who participated in key decisions was an officer, and owed a duty of care across that participation.Senior legal, risk and compliance leaders can be personally exposed, not just directors.
Cassimatis v ASIC [2020] FCAFC 52The duty of care is assessed by weighing the foreseeable risk of harm to the company, including harm from the company breaking the law, against the potential benefits of the conduct.Allowing serious compliance failures can itself be a breach of a director's duty, even without proof of loss to the company.
ASIC v Bekier [2026] FCA 196Senior executives breached s 180 by not acting on, or escalating, serious risks and by not keeping the board informed. The board, which was not given that information, was not liable.Escalation is a personal duty for executives. For boards, the case underlines the value of asking what they are not being told.

The business judgment rule

Section 180(2) protects a business judgment (a decision to take or not take action on a business matter) if the director or officer:

  1. made it in good faith and for a proper purpose;
  2. did not have a material personal interest in the subject matter;
  3. informed themselves to the extent they reasonably believed appropriate; and
  4. rationally believed the judgment was in the company's best interests.

The rule only covers the duty of care in s 180 (and the equivalent general law duty). It doesn't protect against the other duties, and courts have treated failures to monitor or to comply with the law as outside its scope in many cases. It protects decisions, which is why a documented decision process matters.

Reliance and delegation: where the line falls

Reasonable reliance tends to look likeUnreasonable reliance tends to look like
Relying on a competent, independent expert or officer on a matter within their expertise, after reading and understanding their advice.Accepting a conclusion without reading the underlying paper, or on a matter the director is expected to understand personally.
Asking questions where the advice conflicts with what the director already knows.Ignoring red flags, such as repeated incidents, audit findings or regulator correspondence.
Delegation with monitoring: reports come back and are challenged.Delegation and silence: no follow-up on whether delegated work happened.

Sections 189 and 190 set out when reliance on information or advice, and delegation, are treated as reasonable. The protections depend on good faith, independent assessment and, for delegation, a reasonable belief that the delegate is reliable and competent.

Implications for risk and compliance functions

  • Board reporting is a legal control. If directors can only discharge their duty through the information they receive, then the clarity, completeness and honesty of risk reporting protect them. See board risk reporting.
  • Escalation must be personal and timely. Bekier turned on what executives knew and didn't pass on. Escalation rules should say who must tell whom, by when.
  • Compliance failures are governance failures. After Cassimatis, persistent breaches can expose directors personally, not just the company.
  • Minutes are evidence. Record what was considered, questions asked and why decisions were made, not just the resolution.
  • Heads of risk, legal and compliance are officers too. Their own conduct can be tested against s 180.

Check your understanding

1. Which case established that a general counsel could be an 'officer' owing a duty of care?

In Shafron (2012), the High Court held the general counsel and company secretary of James Hardie was an officer.

2. In Centro (ASIC v Healey), why were the directors liable despite relying on management and auditors?

Directors may rely on others but must still read and understand key documents within their responsibility.

3. Which statement reflects Cassimatis?

The Full Federal Court weighed foreseeable harm against potential benefit.

4. In ASIC v Bekier (2026), who was found to have breached s 180?

The court found the two executives breached their duties; claims against the non-executive directors failed.
