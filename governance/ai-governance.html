AI governance

How organisations oversee artificial intelligence so they get the benefits without harming customers, breaking the law or losing control of their own decisions.

Intermediate About 17 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Australia has no AI-specific law. In its December 2025 National AI Plan, the Government confirmed it would rely on existing, technology-neutral laws and sector regulators, supported by voluntary guidance, rather than a standalone AI Act for now.
  • Existing obligations already apply to AI: financial services and consumer laws, privacy, anti-discrimination, directors' duties, and APRA's standards on risk, operational resilience and information security.
  • Regulators are watching closely. ASIC's 2024 review (REP 798) and APRA's April 2026 letter to industry both found that AI adoption is outpacing governance, risk management and assurance.
  • Good AI governance rests on a few basics: clear accountability, an inventory of AI uses, risk-based controls across the AI lifecycle, human oversight, transparency and monitoring.
  • From 10 December 2026, privacy policies must explain when personal information is used in substantially automated decisions that significantly affect individuals.

What makes AI risk different

AI systems learn patterns from data rather than following rules someone wrote. That brings real benefits, but also risks that traditional technology controls weren't designed for:

RiskWhat it looks like
Bias and unfair outcomesA model trained on historical data reproduces past discrimination, for example in credit or claims decisions.
OpacityIt's hard to explain why a model produced a particular result, making decisions hard to justify or challenge.
Errors and "hallucinations"Generative AI produces confident but wrong answers, which may reach customers.
DriftPerformance degrades as real-world data changes from the data the model was trained on.
Data and privacyPersonal information used to train or prompt models without a lawful basis, or leaked through third-party tools.
Third-party dependenceReliance on external models and providers the organisation can't fully see into or control.
SecurityNew attack types such as prompt injection or data poisoning.
ScaleAn error is repeated across thousands of customers before anyone notices.

A governance model

  • Board oversight: the board sets the organisation's appetite for AI risk, understands the most significant uses and receives reporting. Directors' duties of care and diligence apply to decisions about AI as to any other.
  • Accountability: a named senior executive is accountable for AI governance, and each AI use has a business owner. Under the Financial Accountability Regime, AI-related failures fall within existing accountable persons' responsibilities.
  • Inventory: you can't govern what you don't know about. Many organisations find unregistered "shadow AI" when they first build an inventory.
  • Risk tiering: controls are proportionate to impact: a tool that summarises internal documents needs far less than one that decides insurance claims.

    How Australian law and guidance apply

    SourceRelevance to AI
    Financial services law (ASIC)Licensees' general obligations (efficiently, honestly and fairly; adequate risk management), prohibitions on misleading conduct, design and distribution obligations and consumer protections all apply to AI-driven processes and outputs. ASIC's REP 798 (October 2024) reviewed 624 AI use cases across 23 licensees and warned of a governance gap as adoption outpaced risk frameworks.
    APRA prudential standardsCPS 220 (risk management), CPS 230 (operational risk and service providers, including AI vendors) and CPS 234 (information security) apply to AI like any other technology. In a letter to industry dated 30 April 2026, APRA called for a step-change in AI risk management, saying governance, risk management, assurance and operational resilience were not keeping pace with adoption. It set out expectations covering cyber and information security, governance, supplier risk, and change management and assurance, including for AI agents.
    Privacy ActThe Australian Privacy Principles govern collecting and using personal information to train or run AI. From 10 December 2026, privacy policies must describe substantially automated decisions that significantly affect individuals. See Privacy law.
    Anti-discrimination and consumer lawDiscriminatory outcomes are unlawful whether made by a person or a model; the Australian Consumer Law applies to AI products and claims about them.
    Government guidanceAustralia's AI Ethics Principles (2019), and the Guidance for AI Adoption (October 2025), which replaced the Voluntary AI Safety Standard's 10 guardrails with six essential practices: decide who is accountable; understand impacts and plan accordingly; measure and manage risks; share essential information; test and monitor; and maintain human control.
    National AI Plan (December 2025)Confirmed reliance on existing laws and regulators, supported by an Australian AI Safety Institute, rather than immediate mandatory guardrails for high-risk AI. This position could change.
    International standardsISO/IEC 42001 (AI management systems, certifiable), ISO/IEC 23894 (AI risk management guidance) and the US NIST AI Risk Management Framework are widely used as reference points.

    Worked example: assessing one AI use case

    An illustrative assessment using the governance model above:

    QuestionExample: AI that drafts replies to member complaints
    What does it do, and who is affected?Drafts responses that staff review before sending. Affects members who complain.
    What could go wrong?Wrong information about a member's account; missing that a complaint should be escalated or reported; tone that breaches the fund's standards; personal information exposed to the AI provider.
    Which obligations apply?Complaints handling (RG 271), privacy, conduct obligations, CPS 230 and CPS 234 if a provider handles member data.
    ControlsHuman review of every reply; checks for reportable matters; testing before launch and sampling afterwards; contract terms on data use; logging.
    Who is accountable?A named executive owns the use case, and it is in the AI inventory and risk register.
    DecisionApproved for use with human review, with a review after three months.

    Common mistakes

    • No inventory, so nobody knows where AI is being used, including inside vendors' products.
    • "Human in the loop" in name only, where reviewers approve outputs without checking them.
    • Testing only before launch, not monitoring how the system behaves over time.
    • Treating AI as an IT issue rather than a business risk with owners in the business.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is there an AI law in Australia?

    Not a standalone one, as at October 2026. Existing laws apply, including privacy, consumer protection, anti-discrimination, directors' duties and prudential standards. The Government has relied on these laws and voluntary guidance for now.

    Do APRA's standards apply to AI?

    Yes. APRA's April 2026 letter said existing standards, such as CPS 220, CPS 230 and CPS 234, apply to AI as to any other activity.

    Questions boards can ask

    • Where are we using AI today, including tools staff use informally, and who owns each use?
    • Which uses affect customers' outcomes, and how do we know they're fair and accurate?
    • Where is a human in the loop, and do they have the time, skills and authority to override the AI?
    • What do customers know about our use of AI, and can they challenge a decision?
    • What happens if a key AI provider fails, changes its model or suffers a breach?
    • How would we find out if a model started going wrong?
