Conflicts of interest

A conflict of interest arises when someone who is meant to act for others has an interest, or another duty, that could pull them in a different direction.

Beginner About 16 minutes Last reviewed: 7 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Conflicts can be actual, potential or perceived, and can involve personal interests or competing duties. Having a conflict isn't wrong in itself; failing to manage it is.
  • Directors must disclose material personal interests. AFS licensees must have adequate arrangements to manage conflicts (s 912A(1)(aa)); ASIC updated its guidance (RG 181) in December 2025.
  • Super trustees must give priority to members' interests where there's a conflict, and must have a board-approved conflicts management framework under APRA's SPS 521.
  • The three classic ways to manage a conflict are to avoid it, control it, or disclose it. Disclosure alone is often not enough.
  • Some conflicts are banned outright, such as most conflicted remuneration for financial advice.

What is a conflict of interest?

Imagine a council officer choosing a builder for a new library, and one of the tenderers is their brother-in-law. Even if the officer is scrupulously fair, the situation creates a risk that their judgement is affected, and it will certainly look that way. That is a conflict of interest.

TypeMeaningExample
ActualA real conflict exists now.A trustee director owns shares in a company bidding to be the fund's administrator.
PotentialA conflict could arise in future.A director's spouse works for an investment manager the fund might appoint.
PerceivedA reasonable person could think there is a conflict, even if there isn't.An executive accepts expensive hospitality from a supplier during a tender.
Duty vs interestA person's own interest competes with their duty.An adviser is paid more for recommending one product.
Duty vs dutyTwo duties owed to different parties compete.A director sits on the boards of both a super trustee and a company the fund deals with.
OrganisationalThe organisation's interests compete with its clients' or members'.A super fund uses a related-party insurer or administrator in the same corporate group.

The legal framework

WhoKey obligations
Company directorsGeneral law fiduciary duties (avoid conflicts, don't profit from position); Corporations Act s 191 (disclose material personal interests) and, for public companies, s 195 (generally don't vote or be present); ss 182–183 (don't misuse position or information).
AFS licenseesHave adequate arrangements for managing conflicts of interest (s 912A(1)(aa)). ASIC's RG 181 AFS licensing: Managing conflicts of interest was reissued on 16 December 2025, its first major update since 2004, signalling heightened expectations.
Financial advisersBest interests duty and priority rule (clients' interests before the adviser's or licensee's); ban on most conflicted remuneration (Part 7.7A, Division 4). ASIC's guidance is in RG 246.
Super trusteesSIS Act covenant to give priority to members' interests where there's a conflict; APRA's SPS 521 requires a board-approved conflicts management policy and registers of relevant duties and relevant interests.
Banks and insurersDirectors' duties, AFS licence obligations and APRA governance and fit and proper standards. APRA's draft CPS 510 (June 2026) would apply strengthened, SPS 521-style conflicts requirements across banks, insurers and super.

Managing conflicts: a practical framework

What good conflicts management looks like

  • A policy that defines conflicts, sets out roles, and explains how they're identified and managed.
  • Registers: for super trustees, registers of relevant duties and relevant interests; for many organisations, gifts, benefits and hospitality registers.
  • Regular declarations by directors and staff, updated when circumstances change, and a standing agenda item at board meetings.
  • Decision controls: conflicted people leave the room; decisions minuted with reasons; independent review of related-party dealings.
  • Structural controls: information barriers, separation of roles, arm's length terms for related parties.
  • Monitoring: the compliance function tests whether the framework actually works, not just whether registers exist.

Why disclosure alone often fails

Telling someone about a conflict doesn't remove it. Customers may not understand the implications, or may even trust the adviser more because they seemed honest. Parliament banned most conflicted remuneration for financial advice because disclosure alone was found not to prevent poor advice. For more on the limits of disclosure, see Disclosure obligations.

Check your understanding

Is it a conflict, and what's the best response?

1. The chief investment officer's family trust owns 30% of a private equity manager the fund is considering hiring.

A significant financial interest in a counterparty is an actual conflict. Disclosure alone is not enough; strong controls or avoidance are needed.

2. A trustee director is also a director of an industry association that lobbies on super policy.

This is a relevant duty that should be registered. Most of the time it can be managed; specific decisions may need the director to step out.

3. During a tender, the procurement manager accepts two premium tickets to a grand final from one of the bidders.

Hospitality during a live tender creates at least a perceived conflict. Most gifts policies would prohibit accepting it.

Worked example: a director's conflict (illustrative)

This is an illustrative example for learning only.

StepWhat happens
IdentifyA super fund director's family company bids for the fund's mailing contract.
DeclareThe director declares the interest to the board and it is recorded in the register of relevant interests.
AssessThe board decides the conflict is material: the director has a financial interest in the outcome.
ManageThe director leaves the meeting for the item and gets no papers on it; the tender is evaluated by management against set criteria.
RecordMinutes record the declaration, the director's absence and the reasons for the decision.
ReviewThe arrangement is reviewed if the contract is awarded, with independent monitoring of performance and price.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Are all directors' and executives' interests up to date in our registers?
  2. Which structural conflicts, such as related-party arrangements, does our organisation have, and how are they controlled?
  3. When did we last decide to avoid a conflict rather than manage it?

Frequently asked questions

Do APRA's governance standards change in 2026?

APRA consulted in 2026 on a single cross-industry CPS 510 that would consolidate governance, fit and proper and conflicts requirements, with new requirements expected from early 2028. Check APRA's website for the current position.
