Board risk reporting design

How to give a board the risk information it needs to oversee and challenge: the profile against appetite, the trends, the exceptions and the decisions, without drowning it in data.

Advanced About 24 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • The core question for any board risk report is: are we operating within our risk appetite, and if not, what are we doing about it?
  • Good reports lead with the conclusion and the decisions needed, then show the evidence. They highlight exceptions, trends and forward-looking risks rather than repeating everything that is fine.
  • KRI dashboards need clear thresholds linked to appetite, trend information and commentary that explains the "so what".
  • The Chief Risk Officer's independent opinion is a critical part of the pack: it tells the board what management's own reports may not.
  • Regulators have repeatedly found boards were given long, complex packs that made it hard to see and challenge non-financial risks. Less, better-designed information usually leads to better oversight.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Design a board risk report that shows the risk profile against appetite, with a clear CRO opinion.
  2. Apply principles of good board papers to avoid information overload.
  3. Use research on CRO reporting lines and board risk oversight to explain why independent risk reporting matters.
  4. Critically assess what boards can and can't learn from risk reports.

Why board risk reporting matters

Boards can only oversee what they can see. Under APRA's CPS 220, the board is ultimately responsible for the risk management framework and for setting risk appetite, and must be able to rely on information about the organisation's risk profile. Several major inquiries have highlighted how reporting can fail boards. APRA's 2018 Prudential Inquiry into the Commonwealth Bank found shortcomings in governance, culture and accountability, particularly in dealing with non-financial risks, including in how issues were escalated and overseen. The Financial Services Royal Commission also emphasised that directors must actively seek and challenge the information they need.

Anatomy of a good board risk report

Reporting the risk profile against appetite

The heart of the report is a view of each top-level risk type in the risk appetite statement, showing where the organisation sits, which way it's moving and why. An illustrative summary for a fictional super fund:

Illustrative risk profile summary. Status is shown by label and symbol, not colour alone.
Risk typeStatusTrendCommentary (the "so what")
Investment risk✓ Within appetite→ StableAll options within risk budgets; liquidity stress tests passed.
Operational risk▲ Approaching limit↑ WorseningTwo administration system outages this quarter; recovery within tolerance but close to the limit. Upgrade business case at item 6.
Compliance risk✕ Outside appetite↑ WorseningFee error (reported to ASIC and APRA) and three late rollover breaches. Remediation under way; root cause is manual processing. Decision required on automation funding.
Third-party risk▲ Approaching limit→ StableAdministrator contract renewal due; CPS 230 contract requirements not yet agreed.
Strategic risk✓ Within appetite↓ ImprovingMerger integration milestones met.

Notice what makes this useful: the status is linked to appetite, the trend adds direction, and the commentary explains the cause and the action or decision, not just the number. Aggregating individual risks into these ratings is covered in Risk aggregation and correlation.

Designing a KRI dashboard

Design choiceGood practice
SelectionA small set of indicators that genuinely predict or measure the risks in the appetite statement. Leading indicators (such as staff turnover in a key team) alongside lagging ones (such as losses).
ThresholdsAmber and red thresholds set from appetite and tolerances, approved and documented. Red should mean "outside appetite", with a defined escalation.
TrendShow at least the last four to six periods so the board can see direction, not just a snapshot.
CommentaryA line on why it moved and what is being done, with an owner and a date.
AccessibilityStatus shown with words or symbols as well as colour. Around 1 in 12 men has some colour vision deficiency.
HonestyAvoid "watermelon" reporting: green on the outside, red inside. Test whether the indicators actually turned amber before past incidents.

You can explore how thresholds drive status and escalation in the KRI dashboard demo on the Risk appetite and tolerance page.

What good board papers look like

Weaker papersStronger papers
Hundreds of pages; everything included "for completeness".A short core paper, with detail in appendices or a reading room.
Starts with background and methodology.Starts with the conclusion, the key risks and the decisions sought.
Data without interpretation.Each chart or table has a "so what" and an action.
Everything is green; issues appear only when they're resolved.Bad news early, including near misses and uncertainty.
Backward-looking only.Includes emerging risks, trends and scenario results.
Management's view only.Includes the CRO's independent opinion, and internal audit's view where relevant.
Same format regardless of audience.Tailored for the board, the board risk committee and management, with consistent data underneath.

The CRO's opinion

Many boards now ask the Chief Risk Officer for a short, independent written opinion in every risk report: an overall view of the risk profile, whether management's reporting is complete and balanced, where the CRO disagrees with management, and what the board should focus on. CPS 220 requires the CRO to be independent of business lines and to have regular and unfettered access to the board and the board risk committee; the opinion is one practical way that independence shows up.

Questions directors can ask

  • Are we within appetite for every risk type? Where aren't we, and when will we be?
  • What has changed since last time, and what is getting worse?
  • What isn't in this report that we should know about?
  • Which KRIs turned red before our last major incident, and which didn't?
  • Where do the CRO and management disagree?
  • What would it take to breach our tolerances, and how close are we?
  • Which actions are overdue, and why?

Worked example: one line of a risk dashboard

A good dashboard line tells the board where the risk is against appetite, which way it is moving, why, and what is being done. An illustrative example:

ElementExample
RiskMember administration errors (operational risk)
Appetite and limitLow appetite. Limit: no more than 0.5% of transactions with errors in any month.
Current level and trend0.62% this month, up from 0.41% three months ago: outside appetite, worsening.
WhyA new administration system went live in July; manual workarounds are causing errors.
Actions and ownerExtra quality checks from 1 October and a system fix due in November; owner: Chief Operating Officer.
CRO viewThe fix is credible, but the date has slipped once. The CRO recommends the board asks for a weekly update until the rate is back within appetite.

Lessons from the Star case

  • In ASIC v Bekier [2026] FCA 196 the Federal Court found that two senior executives breached their duties, partly by not keeping the board properly informed. The claims against the non-executive directors failed.
  • Even so, Justice Lee was critical that board minutes didn't show directors actively pressing management with difficult questions. Commentators took this as a reminder that directors shouldn't rely passively on management summaries or long board packs.
  • For people who design board reporting, the lesson is that what goes to the board, and what is left out, can itself be the subject of legal scrutiny.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a board risk report be?

There is no rule. Many boards prefer a short summary of the most important points, with detail in appendices. The test is whether directors can see quickly where the entity is outside appetite and what is being done.

Should the CRO's opinion be separate from management's report?

Many entities include a separate CRO opinion so the board hears the risk function's independent view, which supports the independence that CPS 220 requires.

Theory and research

Does the CRO's line to the board matter?

One of the clearest findings in bank governance research concerns reporting lines. Aebi et al. (2012) found that banks whose chief risk officer reported directly to the board performed significantly better during the 2007–08 crisis than banks where the CRO reported to the CEO. The mechanism is plausible: a CRO who reports to the board can bring bad news without it being filtered or softened by management. This supports the emphasis in CPS 220 and in this page on the CRO's independent opinion to the board.

Why agency theory makes board reporting hard

Agency theory explains why boards depend on information that management controls (Jensen & Meckling, 1976). Directors oversee managers whose interests may differ from those of owners or members, but most of what directors know comes from those same managers. Adams et al. (2010) survey research on boards and stress that what boards do is shaped by who is on them and what information they get, which makes it hard to show that any particular board practice causes better outcomes. Research on Australian listed companies has also examined how boards oversee risk in high-uncertainty businesses and proposed stronger integration of risk management into governance (Brown et al., 2009).

More information is not always better

Board packs of several hundred pages are common. Eppler and Mengis (2004) reviewed research across disciplines on information overload: beyond a certain point, more information reduces decision quality, because people can't process it and start relying on shortcuts. The practical implications match the advice on this page: summarise, show trends and exceptions, state what decision is needed, and put detail in appendices.

Critical perspectives

  • Correlation, not causation. Aebi and colleagues show an association between CRO reporting lines and crisis performance. Banks with strong risk governance may differ in many other ways.
  • Reports can reassure instead of inform. A report dominated by green indicators and completed actions may give comfort while the real risks sit outside the metrics. The CBA prudential inquiry criticised exactly this kind of complacency.
  • Directors' time is limited. Even a well-designed report competes with many other agenda items. Research on board effectiveness suggests the quality of discussion matters as much as the paper.
  • Little research on super boards. Most studies concern listed companies and banks. How trustee boards of super funds use risk reporting has been studied far less.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Aebi and colleagues' findings, argue for or against requiring the CRO to report directly to the board in all APRA-regulated entities.
  2. Take a board risk report you have seen or a published example. Where does it risk information overload, and how would you redesign it?
  3. From an agency theory perspective, what can a board do to reduce its dependence on information filtered by management?
  4. What should a CRO's opinion to the board include when the CRO disagrees with management? How should the board respond?
