Culture and conduct risk

Frameworks and policies set the rules, but culture decides whether people follow them. Conduct risk is what happens to customers and markets when culture goes wrong.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Culture is the shared values, beliefs and norms that shape behaviour: "how we really do things around here". Risk culture is the part that concerns risk.
  • Conduct risk is the risk that the behaviour of an organisation or its people causes poor outcomes for customers or harms markets.
  • Australian inquiries, especially APRA's 2018 CBA Prudential Inquiry and the 2019 Royal Commission, identified culture as a root cause of widespread misconduct.
  • APRA requires boards of the entities it regulates to form a view of their risk culture, and uses a Risk Culture 10 Dimensions framework to assess it.
  • Culture can be assessed using a mix of surveys, behavioural indicators and observation, but no single metric captures it.

From culture to outcomes

Why regulators care

  • APRA's CBA Prudential Inquiry (2018) found that the bank's continued financial success had dulled its senses, contributing to complacency, insularity and a reactive approach to non-financial risks, and made 35 recommendations.
  • The Financial Services Royal Commission (2019) put culture, governance and remuneration at the centre of its analysis and set out six basic norms of conduct: obey the law; do not mislead or deceive; act fairly; provide services that are fit for purpose; deliver services with reasonable care and skill; and, when acting for another, act in their best interests.
  • APRA's CPS 220 requires the board to form a view of the entity's risk culture and the extent to which it supports operating within risk appetite, and to identify and address any desirable changes.
  • ASIC focuses on conduct outcomes: whether customers are treated fairly, and whether products and services meet their needs.

APRA's Risk Culture 10 Dimensions

APRA assesses risk culture using ten dimensions grouped under two elements: observable behaviours, and the risk architecture (the formal systems, policies, processes and governance structures that support risk management). In 2021 APRA piloted a risk culture survey of staff at ten general insurers based on these dimensions; the lowest-scoring dimensions were risk governance and controls, decision-making and challenge, and responsibility and accountability. APRA has since extended the survey to other regulated entities.

DimensionWhat it's about (our summary)
LeadershipWhether leaders model and reinforce sound risk behaviour.
Decision-making and challengeWhether decisions consider risk and people feel able to challenge.
Communication and escalationWhether bad news and concerns travel quickly to the right people.
Risk capabilitiesWhether people have the skills, tools and resources to manage risk.
Alignment with purpose and valuesWhether behaviour matches stated purpose and values.
Risk culture assessment and board oversightWhether the board understands and oversees risk culture.
Risk appetite and strategyWhether appetite is understood and actually guides strategy and decisions.
Risk governance and controlsWhether frameworks, policies and controls work in practice.
Responsibility and accountabilityWhether people know and own their responsibilities, and face consequences.
Performance management and incentivesWhether rewards and consequences support sound risk behaviour.

Try it: a risk culture self-reflection

Rate your own team or organisation from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on each dimension. This is a personal reflection tool using APRA's dimension names, not APRA's survey or methodology. Nothing is saved or sent anywhere.

Conduct risk

Conduct risk has no single legal definition in Australia. A common working definition is the risk of inappropriate, unethical or unlawful behaviour by an organisation or its people that results in poor outcomes for customers, or harms the integrity of markets. Under APRA's CPS 230 it is one of the operational risks entities must manage.

Where conduct risk shows upExamples
Product designProducts with features few customers benefit from; poor value add-ons.
Sales and distributionPressure selling; products sold outside their target market.
AdviceAdvice driven by the adviser's interests; fees for no service.
ServicingSlow claims handling; ignoring hardship or vulnerability.
Complaints and remediationDefensive complaints handling; slow or narrow remediation.
MarketsInsider trading; market manipulation; misleading announcements.

How can culture be measured?

No single metric captures culture. Good assessments combine several sources and look for consistency between them:

SourceExamples
What people sayStaff and risk culture surveys, focus groups, exit interviews.
What people doShare of incidents raised by the first line (rather than found by audit); time from discovery to escalation; overdue audit actions; use of speak-up channels; breaches of policy.
What the system rewardsWhether risk and conduct failures actually affect pay and promotion (consequence management data).
What customers experienceComplaint themes, AFCA determinations, remediation programs, product outcomes.
What observers seeBoard and committee observation, audit and regulator findings.

Research insight: psychological safety

Organisational research has found that teams where members believe it is safe to take interpersonal risks, such as admitting mistakes or raising concerns, learn more and perform better. Edmondson (1999) called this psychological safety. For risk management, it matters because problems only get fixed if people feel able to raise them. A drop in incident reporting can signal fear, not improvement.

Changing culture

  • Leaders go first: what leaders pay attention to, reward and tolerate sends the strongest signals.
  • Align incentives: make sure pay and promotion reflect how results are achieved. See Remuneration governance.
  • Make speaking up safe and useful: respond well to bad news, protect people who raise concerns, and show that issues raised get fixed. See Whistleblower protections.
  • Be clear about accountability: named owners, consistent consequences. See FAR.
  • Measure and revisit: culture change takes years; track a consistent set of indicators over time.

Worked example: reading culture from everyday data

A fund's staff survey says 85% of people "feel safe to speak up". Other data tells a different story:

  1. Very few incidents are reported by the business itself; most are found by audit or complaints.
  2. Audit findings are often closed late, and the same issues come back.
  3. Two senior people with poor risk records received full bonuses.
  4. Exit interviews mention pressure to hit sales targets.

Illustrative only. Together, these signs suggest the survey result may not reflect how people actually behave, and that incentives and consequences deserve a closer look.

Common mistakes

  • Relying on one survey as the measure of culture.
  • Ignoring incentives, which often shape behaviour more than values statements.
  • Treating culture as an HR project rather than something the board and executives own.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. What does our incident data tell us about whether people raise problems?
  2. Where might incentives be pushing people to cut corners?
  3. How do leaders respond when someone brings bad news?

Frequently asked questions

Is culture regulated?

Not directly as a single rule, but APRA's CPS 220 requires boards to form a view of risk culture, and conduct obligations, FAR and remuneration rules all shape it. Regulators look at culture when things go wrong.
