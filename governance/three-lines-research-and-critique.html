The Three Lines model: research and critique

Where the three lines came from, what research says about whether they work, and the main criticisms, including the IIA's 2020 update.

Advanced About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • The three lines of defence spread rapidly after the global financial crisis, largely through regulators, before there was evidence it worked.
  • Research links a strong, independent risk function to better crisis outcomes, but not the three-lines structure as such.
  • Critics argue the model can diffuse accountability, create coordination problems and compromise the independence of the second and third lines.
  • The IIA's 2020 Three Lines Model dropped "defence" language and stresses roles that work together, partly in response to these critiques.
  • The model is a way of describing roles, not a guarantee of good risk management.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Trace the origins of the three lines of defence and its adoption by regulators.
  2. Summarise research evidence on independent risk and internal audit functions.
  3. Evaluate the main critiques: diffused accountability, coordination and independence.
  4. Assess whether the 2020 Three Lines Model resolves these problems.

The idea in plain English

Almost every Australian bank, insurer and super fund describes its risk management in terms of three lines: the business (first line), risk and compliance (second line) and internal audit (third line). The Three Lines model page explains how it works. This page asks harder questions: where did the model come from, is there evidence that it works, and what do its critics say?

Theory and research

Origins and spread

Davies and Zhivitskaya (2018) traced how regulators around the world recommended the three lines of defence after the financial crisis as a way to embed risk management throughout financial firms. They noted that its origins are opaque and its effectiveness was untested when it was adopted, and discussed the argument that dividing responsibility for risk among three groups can reduce accountability rather than strengthen it. The model spread as a regulatory expectation before research could evaluate it.

What the evidence supports

No study has shown that adopting the three-lines structure itself improves outcomes. But research does support one of its core ideas: that an independent, authoritative risk function matters. Banks with stronger and more independent risk management before the crisis had lower tail risk and performed better during it (Ellul & Yerramilli, 2013), and banks whose chief risk officer reported directly to the board did better than those where the CRO reported to the CEO (Aebi et al., 2012). The lesson is that the substance of independence and authority matters more than the diagram.

Independence of the third line

The model relies on internal audit being independent of management. Christopher et al. (2009) surveyed Australian chief audit executives and found several threats to that independence: management approving the internal audit budget and plan, internal audit being treated as a management "partner", and the audit role being used as a stepping stone to management positions. These findings suggest the third line's independence is often weaker in practice than the model assumes.

Coordination between the lines

Bantleon et al. (2021) examined the challenges organisations face in coordinating the three lines when implementing the model. When several functions assess the same risks, there can be duplication, gaps and "assurance fatigue" in the first line. The IIA's 2020 update, the Three Lines Model, responded by dropping the language of defence, emphasising that the lines are roles rather than rigid structures, and stressing collaboration and alignment with objectives. Whether the update solves the problems or simply redescribes them is still debated.

Critical perspectives

  • Diffused accountability. If everyone is responsible for risk, it can become unclear who is accountable when something goes wrong. The Financial Accountability Regime can be seen partly as a response, naming individuals rather than lines.
  • The second line's dilemma. Risk functions are asked both to advise the business and to challenge it. Close advice can compromise independence; distant challenge can lose relevance.
  • A model built for banks. Smaller organisations, including many super funds that outsource administration, struggle to fit three distinct lines. Where services are outsourced, the first line may sit partly in a service provider.
  • Ritual risk. Like ERM more broadly, the model can become a way of documenting responsibilities rather than managing risk (Power, 2009).

Seminar questions

  1. Davies and Zhivitskaya note the model's effectiveness was untested when regulators adopted it. How could a regulator test whether it works?
  2. Using Christopher and colleagues' findings, propose three changes that would strengthen internal audit independence in an Australian super fund.
  3. Does the IIA's 2020 update answer the critique of diffused accountability? Why or why not?
  4. How would you apply the Three Lines model to a fund that outsources administration and investment management?
  5. What does the evidence from Ellul and Yerramilli and from Aebi and colleagues suggest a board should focus on, if not the structure of the lines?

Further reading

  • Davies, H., & Zhivitskaya, M. (2018). Three lines of defence: A robust organising framework, or just lines in the sand? Global Policy, 9(S1), 34–42. https://doi.org/10.1111/1758-5899.12568 Peer-reviewed
    Traces how regulators adopted the three lines of defence model after the financial crisis, notes its opaque origins and untested effectiveness, and discusses the argument that spreading responsibility reduces accountability.
  • Christopher, J., Sarens, G., & Leung, P. (2009). A critical analysis of the independence of the internal audit function: Evidence from Australia. Accounting, Auditing & Accountability Journal, 22(2), 200–220. https://doi.org/10.1108/09513570910933942 Peer-reviewed
    A survey of Australian chief audit executives identifying threats to internal audit independence, such as management approving the audit budget and plan, and internal audit being seen as a management 'partner' or a stepping stone.
  • Ellul, A., & Yerramilli, V. (2013). Stronger risk controls, lower risk: Evidence from U.S. bank holding companies. The Journal of Finance, 68(5), 1757–1803. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12057 Peer-reviewed
    Banks with a stronger, more independent risk management function before the crisis (a higher 'risk management index') had lower tail risk and better performance during the crisis.
