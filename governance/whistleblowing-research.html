Whistleblowing: research on who speaks up and why

What research shows about why people report wrongdoing or stay silent, how often whistleblowers uncover fraud, and what makes speak-up programs work.

Advanced About 21 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Employees are among the most important sources for detecting corporate fraud, ahead of auditors and regulators in one major study.
  • Whistleblowing is a process: people must notice wrongdoing, decide it is serious, believe reporting will help and judge the risk to themselves.
  • Intentions to report are easier to predict than actual reporting, and retaliation depends mostly on the organisational context.
  • Many people self-censor because of taken-for-granted beliefs that speaking up is risky, even when managers are open.
  • Protections, credible investigation and visible follow-through matter as much as having a hotline.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain models of the whistleblowing decision and the role of power and motivation.
  2. Summarise evidence on the predictors of whistleblowing and retaliation.
  3. Evaluate evidence on the role of whistleblowers in detecting fraud and in enforcement outcomes.
  4. Critically assess how organisations can design speak-up arrangements under Part 9.4AAA.

The idea in plain English

Australia's whistleblower protections in Part 9.4AAA of the Corporations Act, strengthened from 1 July 2019, require public companies, large proprietary companies and super trustees that are proprietary companies to have a whistleblower policy, and protect eligible whistleblowers from detriment. But protections and policies only matter if people actually speak up. This page reviews research on why they do or don't. It builds on whistleblower protections.

Theory and research

Whistleblowing as a decision process

Near and Miceli (1985) defined whistleblowing as disclosure by organisation members of illegal, immoral or illegitimate practices to people who can act, and proposed a model of the decisions involved. A potential whistleblower must first recognise wrongdoing, then judge whether it warrants action, whether they are responsible for acting and whether reporting will make a difference, and weigh the likely response of the organisation. Power relationships matter throughout: whistleblowers challenge the authority of the organisation, which is why retaliation is a central risk.

What predicts speaking up and retaliation

Mesmer-Magnus and Viswesvaran (2005) combined results from 26 samples covering 18,781 people. Personal, situational and wrongdoing characteristics predicted intentions to blow the whistle more strongly than actual whistleblowing: many people who say they would report do not. Retaliation was best predicted by contextual factors, such as the organisation and the seriousness of the wrongdoing, rather than by the whistleblower's characteristics. This supports a focus on organisational design rather than on selecting "brave" employees.

Silence and voice

Detert and Edmondson (2011) found across four studies that people hold widely shared, taken-for-granted beliefs about when speaking up is risky, such as not embarrassing the boss in public or not raising a problem without a solution, and these beliefs lead them to stay silent even when their own managers are open. Psychological safety research points the same way: people learn and raise problems in teams where they feel safe to take interpersonal risks (Edmondson, 1999).

Do whistleblowers matter?

Dyck et al. (2010) examined how large US corporate frauds between 1996 and 2004 came to light. Employees revealed 19% of frauds, industry regulators 16%, the media 14% and auditors 14%, while the securities regulator revealed only 6%. Monetary incentives helped explain employee whistleblowing. Whistleblowers also affect outcomes: Call et al. (2018) found that whistleblower involvement in US financial misrepresentation cases was associated with higher penalties, longer prison sentences for executives and faster enforcement.

Critical perspectives

  • Rewards are contested. Dyck and colleagues found monetary incentives help explain employee whistleblowing. The US pays whistleblower rewards; Australia's corporate regime generally does not. Whether it should is debated, including concerns about opportunistic reports.
  • Intentions are not actions. Surveys asking staff whether they would report wrongdoing overstate actual reporting, as the meta-analysis shows. Speak-up metrics based on surveys need care.
  • Most evidence is overseas. The fraud detection and enforcement studies use US data. Australian research on the effects of the 2019 reforms is still developing.
  • Channels are not culture. A hotline can exist in an organisation where no one trusts it. The implicit voice research suggests beliefs about risk run deeper than formal policies.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Near and Miceli's model, analyse why a staff member might not report a fee error they suspect is deliberate. Which stage is the biggest barrier?
  2. Mesmer-Magnus and Viswesvaran found context predicts retaliation. What contextual features would you examine when reviewing a whistleblower program?
  3. Should Australia introduce financial rewards for corporate whistleblowers? Use Dyck and colleagues' findings and consider the risks.
  4. How would you measure whether employees believe it is safe to speak up, given Detert and Edmondson's findings about implicit beliefs?
  5. What does the evidence suggest a board should ask about its organisation's whistleblower program, beyond the number of reports?

Further reading

  • Near, J. P., & Miceli, M. P. (1985). Organizational dissidence: The case of whistle-blowing. Journal of Business Ethics, 4(1), 1–16. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00382668 Peer-reviewed
    Defines whistleblowing and proposes a model of the decisions made by people who believe they have seen wrongdoing, and of how organisations respond, drawing on motivation and power.
  • Mesmer-Magnus, J. R., & Viswesvaran, C. (2005). Whistleblowing in organizations: An examination of correlates of whistleblowing intentions, actions, and retaliation. Journal of Business Ethics, 62(3), 277–297. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-005-0849-1 Peer-reviewed
    A meta-analysis of 26 samples (18,781 people): personal, situational and wrongdoing factors predict intentions to blow the whistle more strongly than actual whistleblowing, and retaliation is best predicted by context.
  • Dyck, A., Morse, A., & Zingales, L. (2010). Who blows the whistle on corporate fraud? The Journal of Finance, 65(6), 2213–2253. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01614.x Peer-reviewed
    In large US corporate frauds from 1996 to 2004, employees (19%), industry regulators (16%) and the media (14%) revealed more frauds than auditors (14%) or the SEC (6%); monetary incentives help explain employee whistleblowing.
  • Detert, J. R., & Edmondson, A. C. (2011). Implicit voice theories: Taken-for-granted rules of self-censorship at work. Academy of Management Journal, 54(3), 461–488. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2011.61967925 Peer-reviewed
    Across four studies, identifies widely held, taken-for-granted beliefs about when speaking up at work is risky, which lead people to stay silent even when their own managers are open.
