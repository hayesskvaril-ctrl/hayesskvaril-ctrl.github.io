Remuneration, incentives and risk-taking: the evidence

What economic theory and research say about how pay shapes risk-taking in financial firms, and what that means for CPS 511, deferral and clawback.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Principal-agent theory explains why pay is linked to performance, and why that link can encourage the wrong behaviour.
  • When some tasks are measurable and others aren't, strong incentives on the measurable ones crowd out the rest, including risk management and customer care.
  • Evidence from the financial crisis is mixed: aligning CEOs with shareholders did not prevent losses, and higher pay went with more risk-taking.
  • Clawbacks are associated with better reporting quality, supporting deferral and malus in rules such as CPS 511.
  • Financial incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation, so non-financial measures and culture matter.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain principal-agent and multitask theories of incentive pay.
  2. Evaluate the evidence on executive pay and risk-taking in banks around the financial crisis.
  3. Assess the evidence on clawbacks, deferral and non-financial measures.
  4. Critically assess what the research implies for CPS 511 and the Financial Accountability Regime.

The idea in plain English

After the Hayne Royal Commission, remuneration became one of the main levers regulators use to change behaviour in Australian financial services. APRA's CPS 511 requires deferral, malus and clawback, and non-financial measures in variable pay, and the Financial Accountability Regime links pay to accountability. Behind these rules sits a body of theory and evidence about how pay affects risk. This page reviews it. It builds on remuneration governance.

Theory and research

Principal-agent theory

Owners can't observe managers' effort directly, so they tie pay to measurable outcomes. Holmström (1979) showed that when effort is hidden, contracts should use any available information that says something about effort, the foundation of performance measurement in pay design. But paying for outcomes also rewards luck and can encourage risk: if managers share in the upside but not fully in the downside, they may take risks owners wouldn't choose.

The multitask problem

Holmström and Milgrom (1991) identified a deeper problem. Most jobs involve several tasks, some easy to measure (sales, revenue) and some hard to measure (quality of advice, compliance, risk management). Strong incentives on the measurable tasks pull effort away from the unmeasured ones, so weak incentives can be optimal. This is the theoretical core of many Royal Commission findings: sales-based incentives in banking and advice diverted effort from customers' interests. It is also the logic behind CPS 511's requirement for material non-financial measures. The same idea appears in Kerr (1975)'s warning about rewarding A while hoping for B.

Evidence from the financial crisis

If misaligned pay caused the crisis, banks whose CEOs had more at stake in their shares should have done better. Fahlenbrach and Stulz (2011) found they did not: banks with CEOs whose incentives were better aligned with shareholders performed no better, and on some evidence worse, and CEOs held their shares and suffered large losses. Alignment with shareholders is not the same as prudent risk-taking, because shareholders themselves may favour risk. Cheng et al. (2015) found that financial firms with higher total executive pay took more risk before the crisis. DeYoung et al. (2013) found that risk-taking incentives in bank CEO pay increased around 2000 as deregulation expanded growth opportunities, and CEOs responded by taking more risk.

Clawbacks and deferral

There is some direct evidence that clawbacks change behaviour. Chan et al. (2012) found that after US firms adopted clawback provisions, accounting restatements declined and auditors perceived lower risk. Deferral and malus work on the same principle: pay is held back long enough for problems to emerge before it vests.

When incentives backfire

Gneezy et al. (2011) reviewed evidence that financial incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation and change how people see a task, so incentives sometimes backfire. For risk and compliance roles, where professional judgement and integrity matter, heavy reliance on financial incentives may undermine the very behaviour organisations want.

Critical perspectives

  • Mostly US evidence. The crisis studies use US banks. Australian banks came through the global financial crisis relatively well, so the lessons may transfer imperfectly, and Australian research on CPS 511's effects is still limited given how recent it is.
  • Association, not causation. Firms choose their pay structures, so it is hard to tell whether pay causes risk-taking or risky firms choose certain pay.
  • Non-financial measures can be gamed. Measures such as "risk behaviours" rely on judgement and can become box-ticking, the multitask problem in a new form.
  • Clawback in practice. Clawing back pay already paid is legally and practically difficult. Malus on unvested pay is easier, which is why deferral periods matter.

Seminar questions

  1. Use Holmström and Milgrom's multitask model to explain why sales incentives led to poor advice in cases examined by the Royal Commission.
  2. Fahlenbrach and Stulz found shareholder-aligned CEOs didn't perform better in the crisis. What does this imply for regulators who want to limit risk-taking?
  3. Design a non-financial measure for a head of a super fund's member services. How would you stop it being gamed?
  4. Is clawback likely to change behaviour, or mainly to satisfy public demands for accountability? Use the evidence on this page.
  5. How should remuneration for risk and compliance staff differ from that for business line staff, given Gneezy and colleagues' findings?
