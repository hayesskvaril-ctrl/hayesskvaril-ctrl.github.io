Control framework architecture

A control framework turns risks and obligations into a single library of well-described controls, each tested once and reused wherever it is relied on, so that control results flow straight into the risk profile and compliance reporting.

Intermediate About 16 minutes Last reviewed: 1 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • Design from control objectives (what must be true), then map controls to them. This avoids hundreds of near-duplicate controls.
  • Hold controls in one common library, linked many-to-many to risks, obligations, processes and critical operations.
  • Classify controls by level (entity-level, process-level, IT general controls), by type (preventive, detective, corrective) and by automation.
  • Identify key controls: the ones the organisation relies on for a material risk or critical obligation. These get the most testing.
  • Control effectiveness should drive residual risk ratings and compliance status automatically, with a documented judgement when they differ.

The idea in plain English

Ask five teams in a large organisation to list their controls and you will get five lists that overlap and contradict each other. A control framework is the agreed architecture for controls: what they are for, how they are described, which ones matter most, how they are tested and how the results are used. It builds on the basics in control design and testing.

Start from control objectives

A control objective states what must be true for a risk to be managed or an obligation to be met, for example "Only authorised changes are made to unit pricing systems" or "Member contributions are allocated accurately and on time". Controls are then mapped to objectives. Objectives are more stable than controls and make gaps obvious: an objective with no controls is a gap; an objective with twelve controls may be over-controlled.

A standard control description

Every control in the library should be described the same way, so that anyone can understand and test it. A useful structure:

  • Who performs it (a role, not a name), and who owns it;
  • What they do, and to what (the population);
  • When and how often;
  • How: the threshold, tolerance or criteria, and what happens to exceptions;
  • Evidence: what record proves it was done.

Example: "The Unit Pricing Manager (who) reviews the daily unit price movement report for every investment option (what) before prices are released each business day (when), investigates any movement more than 0.5 percentage points different from the benchmark movement, and records the explanation and approval in the pricing log (how and evidence)."

Rationalising the library

When organisations first combine registers they often find thousands of controls, many duplicated or poorly described. Rationalisation means merging duplicates, rewriting vague controls, removing "controls" that are really policies or intentions, and confirming which are key. It is slow, but it is the single biggest source of efficiency in a GRC programme.

Testing and how results roll up

Control testing (see control testing and sampling) checks design effectiveness (would the control work if performed as described?) and operating effectiveness (was it performed consistently?). In an integrated system:

  • first-line control self-assessments, second-line reviews and internal audit testing are recorded against the same control;
  • an assurance map shows which lines test which key controls, avoiding gaps and duplication (see assurance mapping);
  • control ratings feed the residual rating of every linked risk and the status of every linked obligation;
  • failed tests raise issues with owners and due dates (see issue and action management).
