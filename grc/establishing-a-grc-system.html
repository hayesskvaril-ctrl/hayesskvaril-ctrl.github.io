Establishing a GRC system: a step-by-step roadmap

Establishing a GRC system in a complex organisation is a multi-year change programme, not a software install. This roadmap sets out seven phases, what each delivers, who decides, and the pitfalls that sink most programmes.

Intermediate About 17 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Treat GRC as a business change programme with board sponsorship, an accountable executive and a steering committee, not as an IT project.
  • Design before you buy: taxonomies, rating scales, the data model and the operating model come before technology choices.
  • Data clean-up (rationalising controls, rewriting weak risks, mapping obligations) is usually the largest and most underestimated piece of work.
  • Implement in waves, starting with a pilot, and run old and new in parallel until reports reconcile.
  • Plan for embedding from day one: adoption measures, training and post-implementation review decide whether the system is used or ignored.

The idea in plain English

Setting up a GRC system is like replacing the plumbing in a building that people are still living in. The risks, obligations and controls already exist, spread across registers and spreadsheets, and the business must keep running and reporting while everything is redesigned and moved. The phases below give the order of work. Durations are illustrative for a large, multi-entity organisation; smaller organisations move faster.

Phase 2 in more depth: the design decisions

Most of the long-term value, and most of the arguments, sit in phase 2. Decide these explicitly and record the reasons:

  1. Scope and boundaries: which legal entities, risk types and functions are in, and whether IT risk, financial reporting controls (for example SOX-style controls in listed groups), model risk and health and safety use the same system.
  2. The risk taxonomy: Level 1 categories aligned to material risks, Level 2 sub-types with definitions, and the rules for writing Level 3 risks (see risk taxonomy and the risk hierarchy).
  3. Rating methodology: one likelihood and impact scale for the whole organisation, with impact categories (financial, customer, regulatory, operational, reputation) and calibration examples; inherent and residual ratings; control effectiveness ratings and how they combine.
  4. Obligations structure: the levels from source to atomic obligation, the applicability model, and whether to licence content (see obligations architecture).
  5. Control architecture: control objectives, control classification, the standard description, key control criteria (see control framework architecture).
  6. Hierarchies: the organisation hierarchy (legal entities and business units, which rarely match) and the process hierarchy, including CPS 230 critical operations if relevant.
  7. The data model: the records, links, identifiers and ownership rules (see the GRC data model).
  8. The operating model: committees, three lines roles, escalation and the calendar (see governance and operating model).
  9. Reporting: what each committee and the board needs to see, designed backwards from decisions, not forwards from available data.

Who does what in the programme

RoleResponsibilities
Board or board risk committeeSponsors the programme; approves the charter, frameworks and appetite; monitors progress and benefits.
Accountable executive (often the CRO)Owns delivery and benefits; chairs or reports to the steering committee.
Steering committeeMakes cross-functional decisions; resolves conflicts between risk, compliance, audit, technology and the business.
Design authorityA small group that owns the taxonomies, methodology and data model, and approves changes to them during and after the programme.
Business unit championsRepresent the first line, test designs, lead migration and adoption in their area.
Technology teamConfigures, integrates and supports the platform; owns access and data security.
Internal auditAdvises on control and assurance design while staying independent; later audits the system.

Measuring success

Agree the measures in phase 0 and report them throughout. Examples: share of Level 3 risks with complete links to controls and Level 2 types; share of key controls tested in the cycle; duplicate controls retired; time to produce the board risk report; issues overdue; regulator and audit findings about GRC; and user adoption (active risk and control owners).

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to establish a GRC system?

It varies. A mid-sized organisation might get the core in place in about a year, but maturing it takes several years.

Who should lead it?

Usually the CRO or head of compliance, with strong sponsorship from the executive and the board, and close involvement of the business.

Common pitfalls

  • Buying the tool first. The vendor's default taxonomy becomes yours by accident.
  • Migrating the mess. Loading thousands of duplicated controls and vague risks into a new system makes them harder to fix.
  • Designing for the second line only. If the first line finds the system a burden, data quality collapses.
  • Too much at once. A big-bang go-live across every entity and risk type rarely works; waves do.
  • No owner after go-live. Without a design authority and a funded support team, taxonomies drift and the system decays.
  • Treating the system as the answer. Research on GRC systems stresses goals, stakeholder involvement and requirements set before implementation as critical to success Papazafeiropoulou & Spanaki, 2016, and research on ERM shows it takes different shapes in different organisations rather than one standard design Arena et al., 2010.
