What is a GRC system?

GRC brings governance, risk management and compliance together, so an organisation can pursue its objectives, deal with uncertainty and meet its obligations using one connected set of frameworks, people, processes and data.

Beginner About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 1 October 2026

Key takeaways

  • A GRC system is not a piece of software. It is the combination of frameworks, people, processes, data and technology an organisation uses to govern itself, manage risk and meet its obligations.
  • Integration is the point: one shared set of objectives, risks, obligations, controls and evidence, used by everyone, instead of separate registers that disagree.
  • The core idea is "assess once, use many times": one control can address several risks and obligations, and one test of that control provides evidence to all of them.
  • For APRA-regulated entities, the risk management framework required by CPS 220 (or SPS 220 for super) sits at the heart of the GRC system.
  • Most organisations build GRC maturity in stages, from spreadsheets and silos to an integrated, data-driven system.

The idea in plain English

Every organisation has to do three things at once. It has to decide what it is trying to achieve and who is accountable (governance). It has to understand what could get in the way and decide what to do about it (risk management). And it has to meet the laws, regulations, codes, contracts and internal policies that apply to it (compliance).

In many organisations these grew up separately: a risk team with a risk register, a compliance team with an obligations register, internal audit with its own findings, IT security with its own controls list. Each asks the business the same questions in a different format. The registers disagree, nobody can see the whole picture, and the board receives reports that don't connect.

A GRC system joins these up. OCEG, a non-profit that developed one of the best-known GRC models, describes GRC as an integrated set of capabilities that lets an organisation reliably achieve its objectives, address uncertainty and act with integrity. The emphasis is on integrated.

The five components

A useful way to describe any GRC system is by five components. Weakness in any one of them undermines the rest.

ComponentWhat it includesExamples
FrameworkThe documents that set the rules: what must be done, by whom, and to what standard.Risk management framework, risk appetite statement, compliance framework, policies, standards, procedures.
People and structuresWho is accountable and who does the work.Board and committees, accountable executives, risk and compliance functions, risk owners, control owners, internal audit.
ProcessesThe recurring activities that keep the picture current.Risk and control self-assessments, control testing, incident and breach management, regulatory change, issue management, reporting.
DataThe shared records and how they link.Risk taxonomy, obligations library, control library, KRIs, incidents, issues, assurance results.
TechnologyThe tools that hold the data and run the workflows.A GRC platform, or a well-governed set of registers; links to HR, finance and IT systems.

Assess once, use many times

The single biggest benefit of integration is reuse. Suppose a super fund has a control: "Each month, the fund reconciles member contributions received with contributions allocated, and investigates differences over $1,000 within five business days." That one control helps manage several things at once:

  • an operational risk (members' accounts are wrong);
  • a compliance obligation (allocating contributions correctly and on time);
  • a CPS 230 critical operation (contributions processing) and its tolerance level;
  • a service provider risk, if an administrator does the processing.

In a siloed world, four teams would each ask about the control, test it, and store the result separately. In an integrated system the control is recorded once, linked to all four, tested once, and the result flows to every report that needs it. The GRC data model shows how.

Where the regulations fit

No Australian law requires a "GRC system" by that name. But several requirements only work well inside one:

  • CPS 220 (and SPS 220 for super trustees) requires a risk management framework covering all material risks, a board-approved risk appetite statement and risk management strategy, a risk management function and a compliance function.
  • CPS 230 requires entities to manage operational risk, map critical operations, set tolerance levels and manage material service providers, which needs processes, risks, controls and providers to be linked.
  • The Financial Accountability Regime requires clear accountability for parts of the business, which links naturally to risk and control ownership.
  • Most Australian financial services licensees must have adequate risk management systems, and licensees must report certain breaches (see breach reporting), which needs a reliable obligations and incident process.

A simple maturity model

Organisations rarely jump straight to a fully integrated system. This illustrative scale (our own, not an official standard) helps describe where an organisation is and what the next step looks like.

LevelWhat it looks like
1. Ad hocRisks and obligations live in personal spreadsheets. Ratings and terms differ by team. Reporting is manual and late.
2. DefinedFrameworks and policies exist, with common rating scales, but registers are still separate and linked by hand.
3. IntegratedOne taxonomy and one set of records. Risks, obligations and controls are linked. Testing results flow to every report.
4. Managed with dataKRIs, incidents and testing feed the risk profile automatically. Management uses the system to make decisions, not just to report.
5. OptimisedContinuous control monitoring, forward-looking indicators and scenario analysis. The system is reviewed and improved as the business changes.

A system is not the same as a tool

Buying GRC software doesn't create a GRC system. Without agreed taxonomies, clear ownership and good processes, a tool simply stores the old confusion more expensively. Design comes first; see establishing a GRC system.
