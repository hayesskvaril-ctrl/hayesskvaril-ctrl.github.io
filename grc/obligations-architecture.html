Obligations architecture: from the law to the control

An obligations library breaks every law, standard, code and contract into specific, testable obligations, decides who they apply to, and links each one to the risks and controls that make sure it is met.

Intermediate About 16 minutes Last reviewed: 1 October 2026 Checked against sources
Obligation
A specific requirement the organisation must meet, from a law, regulation, prudential standard, industry code, licence condition, contract or internal policy.
Atomic obligation
An obligation broken down to a single requirement that one owner can be accountable for and that can be tested.

Key takeaways

  • Structure obligations in layers: source, then atomic obligation, then the organisation's interpretation, then the controls that meet it.
  • Each atomic obligation needs an applicability decision (which entities, products and processes), an owner, a criticality rating and links to risks and controls.
  • Obligations link to the Level 2 compliance and conduct risks in the taxonomy, so a gap in meeting an obligation shows up in the risk profile.
  • Regulatory change is a process, not a list: scan, assess impact, update the library, change controls, and confirm the change is embedded.
  • Regulated entities often licence obligation content from legal publishers, but the interpretation and mapping must be the organisation's own.

The idea in plain English

A large financial group may be subject to thousands of individual requirements from dozens of laws, prudential standards, regulatory guides and codes. "Comply with the Corporations Act" is not something anyone can own or test. An obligations architecture turns each source into specific obligations, decides who they apply to, and connects them to the controls that make sure they are met.

The attributes of an obligation record

AttributeWhy it matters
Source and citationLinks back to the exact section, paragraph or clause, so the record can be checked when the source changes.
Plain-English summaryWhat the obligation requires, in words the business understands. Not a copy of the source text.
InterpretationHow the organisation reads the requirement, including any legal advice and assumptions. Changes here need approval.
ApplicabilityWhich legal entities, licences, products, channels and processes it applies to. In a group this is often the hardest step.
Obligation ownerThe first-line executive responsible for meeting it, ideally aligned to accountability statements under FAR where relevant.
CriticalityA rating of the consequence of not meeting it (for example penalty exposure, customer harm, licence impact). Drives monitoring intensity.
Regulator and regimeAPRA, ASIC, AUSTRAC, OAIC, ACCC and so on; useful for reporting and for breach assessment.
Linked risks and controlsThe Level 2 risk types and the controls that meet the obligation. An obligation with no controls is a gap.
Reporting and timingAny reporting deadlines (for example breach reporting timeframes) so they can drive reminders and workflows.
Status and review dateWhether the record is current, under change, or retired, and when it was last confirmed.

Mapping: many to many

The links are rarely one to one. One obligation may need several controls (for example a privacy obligation met by access controls, training and a data breach response plan). One control may help meet many obligations (a complaints system meets obligations under several regimes). The GRC system must hold these as many-to-many links, so that a failed control test shows every obligation it affects.

Example mapping (illustrative)
Obligation (summary)Linked risk (Level 2)Key controls
Report certain significant breaches to the regulator within the required timeframeCompliance: reporting obligationsIncident capture with breach triage; significance assessment by compliance; reporting deadline tracking; monthly reconciliation of incidents to breach register
Notify the regulator of a disruption to a critical operation outside toleranceOperational: business disruptionTolerance monitoring; incident escalation thresholds; notification procedure and on-call roster
Give retail clients disclosure documents before they acquire a productConduct: disclosureAutomated document delivery in onboarding; exception reporting; quarterly sample testing

See breach reporting and CPS 230 for the actual requirements and timeframes, which differ by regime.

Regulatory change management

  1. Horizon scanning

    Monitor regulators, legislation, consultations and industry bodies. Record each change against the affected sources.

  2. Impact assessment

    Which obligations, entities, products and processes are affected? Who owns the response? What is the deadline?

  3. Update the library

    Add, change or retire obligations and interpretations, with approval and version history.

  4. Change the controls

    Design new or changed controls, systems and procedures, tracked as actions with owners and dates.

  5. Confirm it is embedded

    Test the new controls after go-live, and close the change only when the evidence shows it works.

Attestation and monitoring

Many organisations ask obligation and control owners to confirm periodically that obligations are met. Attestation is useful for accountability, but it is self-assessment: it should be supported by second-line compliance monitoring and testing (see compliance monitoring and testing) and by internal audit, and weighted by obligation criticality.

Content: buy, build or both?

Legal publishers and some GRC vendors sell obligation libraries that are updated as laws change. They save a great deal of effort, but they describe the law, not your organisation. The applicability decisions, interpretations, owners and control mappings must still be your own, and someone must be accountable for them.
