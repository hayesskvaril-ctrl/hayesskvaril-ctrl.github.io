Risk taxonomy and the risk hierarchy

A risk taxonomy is the shared language of a GRC system. It sorts every risk into levels, from the few enterprise risks the board debates to the thousands of process risks the business manages, so that they can be linked, rolled up and compared with appetite.

Intermediate About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 1 October 2026 Checked against sources
Risk taxonomy
A structured, hierarchical classification of the types of risk an organisation faces, used consistently across the whole organisation.
Material risk
A risk that could have a material impact on the organisation, its customers or members. CPS 220 requires the risk management framework to cover all material risks.
Enterprise (or strategic) risk
A top-down view of the most significant risks to the organisation's strategy and objectives, usually owned by executives and discussed by the board.

Key takeaways

  • Risks exist at different levels: enterprise risks the board debates, material risk categories that frame appetite, Level 2 sub-types, Level 3 specific risk events in registers, and causes and controls at process level.
  • Most complex organisations use a three-level taxonomy: Level 1 categories (often the material risks), Level 2 sub-types and Level 3 specific risks recorded in risk registers.
  • Enterprise risks are a top-down lens across the taxonomy, not just another level. One enterprise risk may draw on several Level 1 categories.
  • Register risk events, not causes or impacts. "Key person leaves" is a cause; "critical process fails because knowledge is lost" is the risk event.
  • A taxonomy is only useful if it is mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive, stable over time and used by every function and system.

The idea in plain English

Imagine a library with no catalogue. Every team shelves books its own way, so nobody can find anything or know what is missing. A risk taxonomy is the catalogue for risk. It gives every risk a place, so that a cyber risk in one business and a cyber risk in another are recognised as the same type, can be added up, and can be compared with the board's appetite for cyber risk.

In a complex organisation the taxonomy also has to work at very different levels. The board cannot discuss 4,000 risks; a process owner cannot manage "operational risk". The hierarchy connects the two.

Enterprise risks are a lens, not just a level

A common mistake is to treat enterprise risks as simply the "top" of the taxonomy. In practice they are a top-down view that often draws on several Level 1 categories at once. "We fail to maintain member trust after a major data breach" involves technology and cyber, third party, conduct and compliance risk. Good GRC systems link each enterprise risk to the underlying Level 2 and Level 3 risks that feed it, so the board's discussion is grounded in the detailed data, and the detailed data is aggregated in a way the board can use.

Material risks

"Material risk" is a regulatory as well as a practical idea. CPS 220 requires the risk management framework to cover all material risks, including credit, market and investment, liquidity, insurance, operational and strategic risks, and the board must approve a risk appetite statement covering them. In practice most entities set their Level 1 categories to match their material risks, then attach the appetite statement, tolerances and metrics to each category.

Materiality should be reassessed at least yearly and whenever the business changes. A risk type that was immaterial (for example climate transition risk for a small insurer) can become material as the business or the environment changes.

Reputation is usually treated as an outcome of other risks rather than a category of its own, because almost every risk can damage reputation. Some organisations add it as a Level 1 category anyway, so that the board can set an appetite for it.

Writing a good Level 3 risk

Level 3 risks are where most registers go wrong. A well-written risk describes an event, with its main causes and consequences recorded separately, using the bow-tie idea (see root cause analysis).

Other kinds of risk the hierarchy must handle

  • Emerging risks: risks that are new or not yet well understood (for example new technologies or regulation). Keep a watch list linked to the taxonomy, and promote risks into registers as they crystallise.
  • Project and change risks: risks that exist for the life of a project. Link them to the same Level 2 types, so a project's operational risk can be seen alongside business-as-usual risk.
  • Entity-level risks in groups: each legal entity may need its own risk profile and appetite, while the group needs an aggregated view. See GRC for complex groups.
  • Risks to critical operations: under CPS 230, risks are also viewed through the lens of each critical operation and its tolerance levels, which cuts across business units.

How ratings roll up

To compare the risk profile with appetite, ratings must aggregate up the hierarchy. Three common approaches, each with trade-offs:

  • Highest rating wins: a Level 2 type takes the worst rating of its Level 3 risks. Simple and conservative, but one outlier dominates.
  • Distribution: report how many Level 3 risks sit in each rating band, with trends. More informative, but harder to compare with a single appetite threshold.
  • Judgement overlay: the second-line category owner proposes a Level 1 rating informed by the data, KRIs, incidents and testing, and documents the reasoning. Most mature organisations use this, with the data shown alongside.

Simply adding or averaging ordinal ratings (such as 1 to 5 scores) is misleading; see risk aggregation and correlation.

Tests of a good taxonomy

  • Mutually exclusive: each Level 3 risk belongs to one Level 2 type (secondary tags can show other links).
  • Collectively exhaustive: every risk has a home, including new ones.
  • Stable: changes are rare, controlled and approved, because every link and trend depends on the structure.
  • Shared: risk, compliance, audit, IT security and finance all use it, and so does the technology.
  • Understandable: definitions and examples for every type, so people classify risks consistently.
