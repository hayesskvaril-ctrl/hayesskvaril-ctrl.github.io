GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research

Groups with many legal entities, licences and regulators face GRC design trade-offs that simpler organisations never meet. This page sets out the main design choices and what the research says about integrated GRC.

Advanced About 24 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Complex groups must choose how centralised their GRC system is: one group system, entity systems, or a hybrid with group standards and entity ownership.
  • A common control framework, mapped to many regimes, avoids testing the same control separately for each regulator.
  • Appetite and materiality cascade from group to entity, but each regulated entity's board keeps its own legal responsibilities.
  • Group-level aggregation must capture contagion: shared services, intra-group exposures and common providers can link entities' risks.
  • Research supports integration in principle but warns that GRC and ERM vary by organisation, are hard to evaluate, and can become ritual rather than real control.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Compare centralised, federated and hybrid GRC models for complex groups.
  2. Explain how a common control framework supports multiple regulatory regimes.
  3. Analyse how appetite, materiality and accountability cascade in a group with separately regulated entities.
  4. Critically evaluate research on integrated GRC and ERM, and its limits.

The idea in plain English

A diversified financial group might include a bank, a general insurer, a super trustee, a funds management business with responsible entities, a financial advice licensee and offshore subsidiaries. Each has its own board, licence conditions and regulators (APRA, ASIC, AUSTRAC and others), and its own legal obligations. The group also wants one view of risk across everything. GRC design for such a group is about balancing group consistency with entity accountability. This page builds on the rest of the GRC systems section.

Theory and research

What "integrated GRC" means

Racz et al. (2010) reviewed definitions of GRC and proposed a scientifically grounded definition and a frame of reference for research, covering governance, risk management and compliance, their integration, and supporting technology. Vicente and Mira da Silva (2011) built a reference conceptual model of the key functions of GRC and how they relate, and evaluated it against OCEG's GRC capability model. Both works treat integration as a set of shared processes, information and technology rather than a single tool, which is the approach taken across this section.

What makes GRC systems succeed

Papazafeiropoulou and Spanaki (2016) combined a literature review with the views of professional experts on GRC information systems. Their framework highlights goals and objectives, the purpose of the system, key stakeholders, requirements and methodology set before implementation, critical success factors and barriers. For complex groups the stakeholder dimension is especially important: entity boards, group functions and regulators may want different things from the same system.

One size does not fit all

Research on enterprise risk management, the risk "R" in GRC, cautions against assuming a single best design. Arena et al. (2010) followed three companies over seven years and found ERM took different forms as it met existing organisational logics, experts and technologies. Mikes and Kaplan (2015), drawing on a long field project with senior risk officers, proposed a contingency view: the right mix of ERM practices depends on the organisation's circumstances. For groups, this suggests a hybrid design, with group standards where consistency matters and entity flexibility where businesses differ, rather than forcing one model on every entity.

Design choices for complex groups

Centralised, federated or hybrid

ModelHow it worksStrengthsWeaknesses
CentralisedOne group system, taxonomy and methodology; group functions run the processes for all entitiesConsistency, efficient aggregation, one control libraryEntity boards may struggle to show they own their risks; can ignore local differences
FederatedEach entity runs its own GRC system; the group collects summariesStrong entity ownership; fits very different businessesDuplication, inconsistent ratings, weak aggregation and contagion view
HybridGroup sets the taxonomy, data model, minimum standards and platform; entities own their registers, appetite and decisions within themConsistency with accountability; a single data set cut by entityNeeds strong design governance and clear decision rights

A common control framework for many regimes

Groups subject to several regimes (prudential standards, ASIC obligations, AML/CTF, privacy, and overseas rules) benefit from one control library mapped to every regime's obligations. A single control, such as quarterly privileged access review, can be mapped to information security, privacy and outsourcing obligations and tested once. The mapping must be at the level of atomic obligations, and each entity must still confirm that the controls operating in its business meet its own obligations.

Cascading appetite and materiality

The group can set a group risk appetite and taxonomy, but each APRA-regulated entity's board must approve its own risk appetite statement and framework under CPS 220 (or SPS 220). Entity appetites should be consistent with, but not simply copied from, the group's; for a super trustee, for example, decisions must be in members' best financial interests, which may differ from the group's commercial interests (see conflicts of interest). Materiality also differs by entity: a risk immaterial to the group may be material to a small entity.

Aggregation and contagion

Group aggregation must go beyond adding up entity profiles. Shared services, common technology, intra-group funding and guarantees, common service providers (relevant to CPS 230) and reputational linkages can make one entity's problem every entity's problem. The data model should record these dependencies explicitly, so scenarios can trace how a failure spreads (see risk aggregation and correlation).

Accountability

Under the Financial Accountability Regime, accountable persons and accountability maps are specific to each accountable entity, even when one executive holds group-wide roles. The GRC system should record ownership against both the group role and each entity's accountability map.

Worked example: a group with a bank, an insurer and a super trustee

Illustrative design choices for a group with entities under different prudential standards:

Design questionOne possible answer
One framework or many?One group framework, with entity-level appetite statements and policies where laws differ (for example SPS 220 for the trustee, CPS 220 for the bank and insurer).
Shared or separate data?One GRC system and data model, with access controls so each board sees its own entity.
Who owns shared controls?The group function that operates them, with each entity assessing whether they meet its own obligations.
ConflictsThe super trustee's duty to act in members' best financial interests may conflict with group interests; the framework must let its board decide independently.
ReportingGroup-wide view for the group board; entity views for each regulated entity's board.

Frequently asked questions

Can a subsidiary board rely on group risk management?

It can rely on group services, but it remains responsible for its own entity. It needs enough information and challenge to be satisfied that group arrangements meet its obligations.

Critical perspectives

  • Integration can become ritual. Power (2009) argued that ERM after the financial crisis was shaped by a "logic of the audit trail", producing comfort more than challenge. A highly integrated GRC system can make this worse if completeness of records is mistaken for control of risk.
  • Weak evidence base. Reviews of ERM research note weak measures and limited evidence on how practice actually works Bromiley et al., 2015. There is little rigorous evidence that integrated GRC systems improve outcomes, as opposed to efficiency.
  • Standardisation versus specialist depth. Forcing every risk type into one taxonomy and rating scale can lose the specialised methods that credit, market or actuarial risk need.
  • Technology-led design. Vendor platforms embed assumptions about taxonomies and workflows; groups can end up designing around the tool.

Seminar questions

  1. For a group with a bank, a super trustee and an advice licensee, argue for a centralised, federated or hybrid GRC model. How would you protect the super trustee board's independence?
  2. Mikes and Kaplan propose a contingency view of ERM. Which design parameters of a GRC system should be standard across a group, and which should vary by entity?
  3. How could an integrated GRC system make the problem Power describes worse, and how could it be designed to avoid that?
  4. Design a data model extension that would let a group trace contagion from a shared technology provider to each regulated entity's critical operations.

  • Racz, N., Weippl, E., & Seufert, A. (2010). A frame of reference for research of integrated governance, risk and compliance (GRC). In B. De Decker & I. Schaumüller-Bichl (Eds.), Communications and multimedia security (Lecture Notes in Computer Science, Vol. 6109) (pp. 106–117). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-13241-4_11 Peer-reviewed
    Develops a definition of integrated GRC from a literature review and earlier surveys, and a frame of reference for research covering its components (governance, risk management, compliance), integration and supporting technology.
  • Vicente, P., & Mira da Silva, M. (2011). A conceptual model for integrated governance, risk and compliance. In Advanced information systems engineering (Lecture Notes in Computer Science, Vol. 6741) (pp. 199–213). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-21640-4_16 Peer-reviewed
    Proposes a reference conceptual model of the key functions of governance, risk and compliance and how they relate, and evaluates it against OCEG's GRC capability model.
  • Papazafeiropoulou, A., & Spanaki, K. (2016). Understanding governance, risk and compliance information systems (GRC IS): The experts view. Information Systems Frontiers, 18(6), 1251–1263. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-015-9572-3 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews GRC research and interviews professional experts, producing a framework of what to consider when putting GRC systems in place: goals, purpose, stakeholders, requirements before implementation, critical success factors and barriers.
  • Arena, M., Arnaboldi, M., & Azzone, G. (2010). The organizational dynamics of enterprise risk management. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 35(7), 659–675. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2010.07.003 Peer-reviewed
    A seven-year study of three companies showing ERM takes different forms as it meets existing organisational logics, experts and technologies, rather than being implemented in one standard way.
  • Mikes, A., & Kaplan, R. S. (2015). When one size doesn't fit all: Evolving directions in the research and practice of enterprise risk management. Journal of Applied Corporate Finance, 27(1), 37–40. https://doi.org/10.1111/jacf.12102
    Drawing on a ten-year field project and over 250 interviews with senior risk officers, proposes a contingency theory of ERM: the right 'ERM mix' depends on the organisation's circumstances.
  • Power, M. (2009). The risk management of nothing. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 34(6–7), 849–855. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2009.06.001 Peer-reviewed
    A critique of ERM after the financial crisis: an impoverished idea of risk appetite and a 'logic of the audit trail' made ERM boundary-preserving rather than challenging, so its security was limited or illusory.
