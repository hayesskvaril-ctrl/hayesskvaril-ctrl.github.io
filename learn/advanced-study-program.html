Advanced study program

A free, self-paced course at university level: 12 modules built from the site's Advanced pages, each with learning outcomes, required readings from peer-reviewed research, seminar questions and an essay question.

Last reviewed: 30 September 2026

How the program works

  • Work through one module a week, or at your own pace. Allow roughly three to five hours per module, including the readings.
  • Start with the core pages, which explain the ideas in plain English and summarise the research.
  • The required readings are the original peer-reviewed articles. Some are free to read online; others may need access through a university or public library.
  • Use the seminar questions for discussion or reflection, and the essay question to test whether you can build an argument from the evidence.
  • This is general education. It is not an accredited course and does not lead to a qualification.

If you are new to the field, start with the learning pathways and the Beginner and Intermediate pages first. Every reading below is also listed, with a plain-English summary, in the research library.

Module 1 Theories of risk

Why people and organisations perceive risk differently, and why some systems fail while others stay reliable.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Compare psychometric, social amplification and systems theories of risk.
  2. Explain how cognitive biases distort risk judgements in organisations.

Core pages

  1. Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  2. Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions

Required reading

  • Slovic, P. (1987). Perception of risk. Science, 236(4799), 280–285. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3563507 Peer-reviewed
    Shows that lay people judge risk on qualitative dimensions, especially 'dread' and 'unknown', not just expected deaths, which explains gaps between expert and public views of risk.
  • Kasperson, R. E., Renn, O., Slovic, P., Brown, H. S., Emel, J., Goble, R., Kasperson, J. X., & Ratick, S. (1988). The social amplification of risk: A conceptual framework. Risk Analysis, 8(2), 177–187. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x Peer-reviewed
    Proposes that risk events interact with psychological, social, institutional and cultural processes that amplify or attenuate public responses, producing 'ripple effects' far beyond the direct harm.
  • Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124 Peer-reviewed
    The classic account of three heuristics people use to judge probability (representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment) and the systematic errors each produces.
  • Moore, D. A., & Healy, P. J. (2008). The trouble with overconfidence. Psychological Review, 115(2), 502–517. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.115.2.502 Peer-reviewed
    Separates three kinds of overconfidence (overestimation, overplacement and overprecision) and shows overprecision, being too sure of one's own estimates, is the most persistent.
Further reading
  • La Porte, T. R., & Consolini, P. M. (1991). Working in practice but not in theory: Theoretical challenges of "high-reliability organizations". Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, 1(1), 19–48. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.jpart.a037070 Peer-reviewed
    Launched high reliability organisation research by studying organisations such as air traffic control that operate hazardous systems with remarkably few failures, and asking how they do it.
  • Leveson, N., Dulac, N., Marais, K., & Carroll, J. (2009). Moving beyond normal accidents and high reliability organizations: A systems approach to safety in complex systems. Organization Studies, 30(2–3), 227–249. https://doi.org/10.1177/0170840608101478 Peer-reviewed
    Critiques both normal accident theory and high reliability research, arguing that a systems approach from safety engineering better explains and prevents accidents in complex organisations.
  • Kahneman, D., & Lovallo, D. (1993). Timid choices and bold forecasts: A cognitive perspective on risk taking. Management Science, 39(1), 17–31. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.39.1.17 Peer-reviewed
    Explains why decision-makers are both too timid (judging each risk in isolation) and too bold (forecasting from an 'inside view' of their own plans), and recommends taking an 'outside view' based on reference classes.
  • Edmondson, A. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. Administrative Science Quarterly, 44(2), 350–383. https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999 Peer-reviewed
    A study of 51 work teams introduced team psychological safety, a shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks, and linked it to learning behaviour and performance.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Slovic's dread and unknown dimensions, explain why a data breach at a health insurer provoked a stronger public reaction than a larger financial loss at another firm.
  2. Take a risk register you know (or a published example). Which ratings are most likely to be affected by anchoring and availability, and how would you test whether they are?

Essay question: Are normal accident theory and high-reliability theory compatible? Use a financial services example.

Module 2 Does enterprise risk management work?

The evidence on whether ERM adds value, and how to combine risks into one enterprise view.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the empirical evidence on ERM and firm value.
  2. Explain the methods and pitfalls of risk aggregation.

Core pages

  1. Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  2. Risk aggregation and correlation

Required reading

  • Hoyt, R. E., & Liebenberg, A. P. (2011). The value of enterprise risk management. Journal of Risk and Insurance, 78(4), 795–822. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6975.2011.01413.x Peer-reviewed
    Studying 275 US publicly traded insurers from 1998 to 2005, finds that firms engaged in ERM have a significantly higher market valuation.
  • Ellul, A., & Yerramilli, V. (2013). Stronger risk controls, lower risk: Evidence from U.S. bank holding companies. The Journal of Finance, 68(5), 1757–1803. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12057 Peer-reviewed
    Banks with a stronger, more independent risk management function before the crisis (a higher 'risk management index') had lower tail risk and better performance during the crisis.
  • Embrechts, P., McNeil, A., & Straumann, D. (2002). Correlation and dependence in risk management: Properties and pitfalls. In M. A. H. Dempster (Ed.), Risk management: Value at risk and beyond (pp. 176–223). Cambridge University Press.
    A widely used explanation of why linear correlation is a poor summary of dependence outside normal distributions, and why copulas and tail dependence matter for aggregating risks.
  • Longin, F., & Solnik, B. (2001). Extreme correlation of international equity markets. The Journal of Finance, 56(2), 649–676. https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00340 Peer-reviewed
    Using extreme value theory, finds that correlation between international share markets rises in bear markets but not in bull markets: diversification weakens when it is needed most.
Further reading
  • Power, M. (2009). The risk management of nothing. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 34(6–7), 849–855. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2009.06.001 Peer-reviewed
    A critique of ERM after the financial crisis: an impoverished idea of risk appetite and a 'logic of the audit trail' made ERM boundary-preserving rather than challenging, so its security was limited or illusory.
  • Bromiley, P., McShane, M., Nair, A., & Rustambekov, E. (2015). Enterprise risk management: Review, critique, and research directions. Long Range Planning, 48(4), 265–276. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2014.07.005 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews ERM research and criticises it for weak measures of ERM, limited theory and little evidence on how ERM is actually practised, and sets out research directions.
  • Kretzschmar, G., McNeil, A. J., & Kirchner, A. (2010). Integrated models of capital adequacy: Why banks are undercapitalised. Journal of Banking & Finance, 34(12), 2838–2850. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2010.02.028 Peer-reviewed
    Compares modular, correlation-based aggregation with a fully integrated model driven by economic scenarios, and finds the modular approach common in practice could leave banks undercapitalised.

Seminar questions

  1. Hoyt and Liebenberg found ERM associated with higher value. Design a study that could test whether ERM causes higher value, rather than the reverse.
  2. Use the calculator to find the diversification benefit at correlations of 0, 0.5 and 1. How would you explain to a board why the benefit is uncertain?

Essay question: “ERM is mainly a ritual of verification.” Critically assess this claim against the evidence.

Module 3 Measuring risk

Loss distributions, scenarios and indicators: what quantitative methods can and can't tell a board.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Apply loss distribution, VaR and expected shortfall concepts to operational risk.
  2. Design scenarios and indicators that support decisions rather than false precision.

Core pages

  1. Quantitative operational risk
  2. Scenario analysis and stress testing
  3. KRI design and statistical thresholds

Required reading

  • Artzner, P., Delbaen, F., Eber, J.-M., & Heath, D. (1999). Coherent measures of risk. Mathematical Finance, 9(3), 203–228. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9965.00068 Peer-reviewed
    Proposes four properties a sensible risk measure should have (the 'coherence' axioms) and shows that value at risk can fail one of them, subadditivity, so it can penalise diversification.
  • Chernobai, A., Jorion, P., & Yu, F. (2011). The determinants of operational risk in U.S. financial institutions. Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 46(6), 1683–1725. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109011000500 Peer-reviewed
    Using publicly reported losses from 1980 to 2005, finds most operational losses trace to breakdowns of internal control, and that losses are more likely at younger, more complex firms with higher credit risk and stronger CEO option-based pay.
  • Borio, C., Drehmann, M., & Tsatsaronis, K. (2014). Stress-testing macro stress testing: Does it live up to expectations? Journal of Financial Stability, 12, 3–15. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1572308913000454 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that macro stress tests before the 2008 crisis failed to flag the vulnerabilities that mattered, because models could not capture the non-linear, self-reinforcing dynamics of real crises; stress tests are better at crisis management than at early warning.
  • Breuer, T., Jandačka, M., Rheinberger, K., & Summer, M. (2009). How to find plausible, severe, and useful stress scenarios. International Journal of Central Banking, 5(3), 205–224. https://www.ijcb.org/journal/ijcb09q3a7.pdf Peer-reviewed
    Proposes a systematic search for the worst scenarios within a defined region of plausibility, instead of relying on hand-picked scenarios that may miss the combinations that hurt most.
Further reading
  • Cope, E. W., Mignola, G., Antonini, G., & Ugoccioni, R. (2009). Challenges and pitfalls in measuring operational risk from loss data. Journal of Operational Risk, 4(4), 3–27. https://doi.org/10.21314/JOP.2009.069 Peer-reviewed
    Shows how hard it is to estimate operational risk at very high quantiles such as 99.9% from the loss data banks actually have, and how unstable model results and validation can be.
  • Bradfield, R., Wright, G., Burt, G., Cairns, G., & van der Heijden, K. (2005). The origins and evolution of scenario techniques in long range business planning. Futures, 37(8), 795–812. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.futures.2005.01.003 Peer-reviewed
    Traces the history of scenario methods and classifies them into three main schools, showing that 'scenario analysis' covers quite different techniques with different purposes.
  • Scandizzo, S. (2005). Risk mapping and key risk indicators in operational risk management. Economic Notes, 34(2), 231–256. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0391-5026.2005.00150.x Peer-reviewed
    Sets out a method for mapping operational risks to the steps of a business process, then choosing key risk indicators that estimate likelihood and severity at each step and designing controls around them.
  • Campbell, D. T. (1979). Assessing the impact of planned social change. Evaluation and Program Planning, 2(1), 67–90. https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X Peer-reviewed
    Source of 'Campbell's law': the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the process it is meant to monitor.
  • Woodall, W. H. (2000). Controversies and contradictions in statistical process control. Journal of Quality Technology, 32(4), 341–350. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224065.2000.11980013 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews debates in statistical process control, including whether control charts are hypothesis tests, how to model their performance and how to compare competing methods.

Seminar questions

  1. Explain, with a simple numerical example, how value at risk can fail subadditivity. Why does this matter more for operational risk than for many market risks?
  2. Choose an operational risk for an Australian super fund and write a severe but plausible scenario. How would you defend it against the charge that it is either too mild or too extreme?

Essay question: Why did regulators move away from internal models for operational risk capital, and what was lost?

Module 4 Incidents, root causes and learning

How organisations investigate failures, assess breaches and learn, or fail to learn, from them.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Compare linear, systemic and just-culture approaches to incident analysis.
  2. Apply significance tests to breach scenarios and justify the conclusion.

Core pages

  1. Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  2. Breach significance analysis in practice

Required reading

  • Reason, J. (2000). Human error: Models and management. BMJ, 320(7237), 768–770. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.320.7237.768 Peer-reviewed
    Contrasts the 'person approach' to error (blame, retraining) with the 'system approach', and introduces the Swiss cheese model of layered defences with active failures and latent conditions.
  • Leveson, N. (2004). A new accident model for engineering safer systems. Safety Science, 42(4), 237–270. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-7535(03)00047-X Peer-reviewed
    Proposes STAMP, an accident model based on systems theory: accidents arise from inadequate control of interactions in a system, not just chains of failed components, so analysis must include management and organisational control.
  • Kaplow, L., & Shavell, S. (1994). Optimal law enforcement with self-reporting of behavior. Journal of Political Economy, 102(3), 583–606. https://doi.org/10.1086/261947 Peer-reviewed
    The economic case for self-reporting regimes: rewarding people who report their own violations saves enforcement resources and replaces uncertain sanctions with certain, smaller ones.
  • Toffel, M. W., & Short, J. L. (2011). Coming clean and cleaning up: Does voluntary self-reporting indicate effective self-policing? The Journal of Law and Economics, 54(3), 609–649. https://doi.org/10.1086/658494 Peer-reviewed
    In US environmental regulation, regulators eased enforcement on firms that voluntarily disclosed violations, and those firms went on to improve their compliance, suggesting self-reporting can signal genuine self-policing.
Further reading
  • Peerally, M. F., Carr, S., Waring, J., & Dixon-Woods, M. (2017). The problem with root cause analysis. BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(5), 417–422. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005511 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out problems with root cause analysis as applied in healthcare, including a misleading search for a single cause and weak, poorly followed-up fixes. Argues it was borrowed from other industries without enough attention to what makes it work there.
  • Dekker, S. W. A., & Breakey, H. (2016). 'Just culture:' Improving safety by achieving substantive, procedural and restorative justice. Safety Science, 85, 187–193. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925753516000321 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that a just culture needs more than rules about when to punish: organisations should also attend to fair process and to restoring relationships after an incident, so people keep reporting.
  • Messier, W. F., Jr., Martinov-Bennie, N., & Eilifsen, A. (2005). A review and integration of empirical research on materiality: Two decades later. Auditing: A Journal of Practice & Theory, 24(2), 153–187. https://doi.org/10.2308/aud.2005.24.2.153 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews two decades of research on how auditors and others judge materiality, including how quantitative rules of thumb interact with qualitative factors and how judgements vary between people.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Reason's distinction between active failures and latent conditions, analyse a publicly reported incident at an Australian financial institution, for example from a regulator's enforcement action or an inquiry report. Which latent conditions were identified, and which do you think were missed?
  2. Using Kaplow and Shavell's framework, explain why the law deems some breaches significant rather than leaving every case to judgement.

Essay question: Is root cause analysis a useful fiction? Discuss with reference to systems theories of accidents.

Module 5 Controls, assurance and internal audit

Testing controls, the three lines and whether internal audit makes a difference.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Explain sampling and evidence in control testing.
  2. Evaluate the research on the three lines model and internal audit effectiveness.

Core pages

  1. Control testing: sampling and assurance confidence
  2. Internal audit effectiveness: the research
  3. The Three Lines model: research and critique

Required reading

  • Elder, R. J., Akresh, A. D., Glover, S. M., Higgs, J. L., & Liljegren, J. (2013). Audit sampling research: A synthesis and implications for future research. Auditing: A Journal of Practice & Theory, 32(Supplement 1), 99–129. https://doi.org/10.2308/ajpt-50394 Peer-reviewed
    Synthesises decades of research on how auditors plan, select and evaluate samples, and sets out open questions for research and practice.
  • Doyle, J., Ge, W., & McVay, S. (2007). Determinants of weaknesses in internal control over financial reporting. Journal of Accounting and Economics, 44(1–2), 193–223. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacceco.2006.10.003 Peer-reviewed
    Across 779 US firms disclosing material weaknesses, control failures were more likely in firms that were smaller, younger, financially weaker, more complex, growing fast or restructuring; entity-wide weaknesses clustered in weaker firms.
  • Lenz, R., & Hahn, U. (2015). A synthesis of empirical internal audit effectiveness literature pointing to new research opportunities. Managerial Auditing Journal, 30(1), 5–33. https://doi.org/10.1108/MAJ-08-2014-1072 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews the empirical research on what makes internal audit effective, groups the drivers into macro, meso and micro factors, and notes that effectiveness itself is defined and measured in many different ways.
  • Prawitt, D. F., Smith, J. L., & Wood, D. A. (2009). Internal audit quality and earnings management. The Accounting Review, 84(4), 1255–1280. https://doi.org/10.2308/accr.2009.84.4.1255 Peer-reviewed
    Finds that higher internal audit function quality is associated with lower levels of earnings management, measured by abnormal accruals and by meeting analyst forecasts.
Further reading
  • Power, M. (1997). The audit society: Rituals of verification. Oxford University Press.
    Argues that the 'audit explosion' produces comfort and legitimacy as much as real assurance, because checking often focuses on whether systems exist rather than on outcomes.
  • Roussy, M. (2013). Internal auditors' roles: From watchdogs to helpers and protectors of the top manager. Critical Perspectives on Accounting, 24(7–8), 550–571. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpa.2013.08.004 Peer-reviewed
    Based on 42 interviews in the public sector, finds internal auditors often act as protectors of and helpers to top management rather than independent watchdogs, a position the author calls 'grey' independence.
  • Abbott, L. J., Parker, S., & Peters, G. F. (2010). Serving two masters: The association between audit committee internal audit oversight and internal audit activities. Accounting Horizons, 24(1), 1–24. https://doi.org/10.2308/acch.2010.24.1.1 Peer-reviewed
    Finds that stronger audit committee oversight of internal audit is associated with internal audit spending more of its effort on internal control work, highlighting the tension between serving management and serving the audit committee.
  • Christopher, J., Sarens, G., & Leung, P. (2009). A critical analysis of the independence of the internal audit function: Evidence from Australia. Accounting, Auditing & Accountability Journal, 22(2), 200–220. https://doi.org/10.1108/09513570910933942 Peer-reviewed
    A survey of Australian chief audit executives identifying threats to internal audit independence, such as management approving the audit budget and plan, and internal audit being seen as a management 'partner' or a stepping stone.
  • Davies, H., & Zhivitskaya, M. (2018). Three lines of defence: A robust organising framework, or just lines in the sand? Global Policy, 9(S1), 34–42. https://doi.org/10.1111/1758-5899.12568 Peer-reviewed
    Traces how regulators adopted the three lines of defence model after the financial crisis, notes its opaque origins and untested effectiveness, and discusses the argument that spreading responsibility reduces accountability.
  • Ellul, A., & Yerramilli, V. (2013). Stronger risk controls, lower risk: Evidence from U.S. bank holding companies. The Journal of Finance, 68(5), 1757–1803. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12057 Peer-reviewed
    Banks with a stronger, more independent risk management function before the crisis (a higher 'risk management index') had lower tail risk and better performance during the crisis.

Seminar questions

  1. Show why a sample of 59 with no deviations supports 95% confidence that the deviation rate is below 5%. What changes if one deviation is found?
  2. Propose a way to measure internal audit effectiveness in a super fund. What would you measure, and what would your measure miss?

Essay question: Does the three lines model improve accountability, or diffuse it? Use the research to argue a position.

Module 6 Operational resilience and third parties

Critical operations, tolerance levels and dependence on service providers under CPS 230.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Map a critical operation and set defensible tolerance levels.
  2. Analyse outsourcing and concentration risk using transaction cost and resilience theory.

Core pages

  1. Mapping critical operations end to end
  2. Setting CPS 230 tolerance levels
  3. Service provider concentration, fourth parties and exit planning

Required reading

  • Herbane, B. (2010). The evolution of business continuity management: A historical review of practices and drivers. Business History, 52(6), 978–1002. https://doi.org/10.1080/00076791.2010.511185 Peer-reviewed
    Traces business continuity management from 1970s IT disaster recovery to an organisation-wide discipline, showing how major events and regulation drove each phase and how 'resilience' became the organising idea.
  • Annarelli, A., & Nonino, F. (2016). Strategic and operational management of organizational resilience: Current state of research and future directions. Omega, 62, 1–18. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.omega.2015.08.004 Peer-reviewed
    A systematic review and co-citation analysis of organisational resilience research, distinguishing strategic resilience (preparing and adapting) from operational resilience (keeping critical processes running).
  • Williams, T. A., Gruber, D. A., Sutcliffe, K. M., Shepherd, D. A., & Zhao, E. Y. (2017). Organizational response to adversity: Fusing crisis management and resilience research streams. Academy of Management Annals, 11(2), 733–769. https://doi.org/10.5465/annals.2015.0134 Peer-reviewed
    Brings together crisis management and resilience research to explain how organisations anticipate, respond to and recover from adversity, and why capabilities built before a crisis shape the response.
  • Williamson, O. E. (1979). Transaction-cost economics: The governance of contractual relations. The Journal of Law and Economics, 22(2), 233–261. https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/jle/vol22/iss2/3/ Peer-reviewed
    Explains how uncertainty, frequency and transaction-specific investments shape whether activities are best governed by markets, contracts or in-house, and why specific investments create lock-in.
Further reading
  • Lacity, M. C., Khan, S. A., & Willcocks, L. P. (2009). A review of the IT outsourcing literature: Insights for practice. The Journal of Strategic Information Systems, 18(3), 130–146. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsis.2009.06.002 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews 191 studies of IT outsourcing and draws practical lessons on motives, contracts, relationship management and why outcomes vary.
  • Aron, R., Clemons, E. K., & Reddi, S. (2005). Just right outsourcing: Understanding and managing risk. Journal of Management Information Systems, 22(2), 37–55. https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Just-Right-Outsourcing:-Understanding-and-Managing-Aron-Clemons/0fb3901b8ed6e32bb32dcf7ef44677411cd12898 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out a taxonomy of business process outsourcing risks, including opportunistic behaviour by providers, and argues that splitting work between providers and redesigning processes can reduce them.

Seminar questions

  1. Using the explorer on this page, find the single points of failure in the benefit payment example. Which would you fix first, and how would you justify the cost?
  2. Use the builder to set a tolerance for a critical operation you know. At what point does harm become intolerable, and for whom? Would your answer change for vulnerable members?

Essay question: Can an organisation ever fully exit a critical service provider? Discuss the limits of exit planning.

Module 7 Regulation and enforcement

Why regulators regulate the way they do, and what deters misconduct.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Compare responsive, risk-based and principles-based regulation.
  2. Evaluate economic and behavioural theories of deterrence against Australian enforcement practice.

Core pages

  1. Theories of regulation: responsive, risk-based and principles-based
  2. Enforcement and penalties: how regulators respond
  3. Interpreting legislation for compliance professionals

Required reading

  • Ayres, I., & Braithwaite, J. (1992). Responsive regulation: Transcending the deregulation debate. Oxford University Press.
    The classic statement of responsive regulation and the enforcement pyramid: start with persuasion and escalate to tougher sanctions only when firms don't cooperate.
  • Baldwin, R., & Black, J. (2008). Really responsive regulation. The Modern Law Review, 71(1), 59–94. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2230.2008.00681.x Peer-reviewed
    Extends responsive regulation: regulators should respond not only to a firm's attitude but to its operating and cognitive frameworks, the institutional environment, the logic of different tools and how all of these change.
  • Becker, G. S. (1968). Crime and punishment: An economic approach. Journal of Political Economy, 76(2), 169–217. https://doi.org/10.1086/259394 Peer-reviewed
    The foundation of the economics of enforcement: people weigh the expected cost of a violation, the probability of being caught times the penalty, against its benefit.
  • Armour, J., Mayer, C., & Polo, A. (2017). Regulatory sanctions and reputational damage in financial markets. Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 52(4), 1429–1448. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109017000461 Peer-reviewed
    Studying UK enforcement announcements, finds reputational losses nearly nine times the size of fines, but only where misconduct harmed the firm's own customers or investors, not third parties.
Further reading
  • Stigler, G. J. (1971). The theory of economic regulation. The Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science, 2(1), 3–21. https://doi.org/10.2307/3003160 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that regulation is often acquired by the industry it regulates and designed and operated primarily for its benefit: the foundation of 'regulatory capture' theory.
  • Black, J. (2008). Forms and paradoxes of principles-based regulation. Capital Markets Law Journal, 3(4), 425–457. https://doi.org/10.1093/cmlj/kmn026 Peer-reviewed
    Distinguishes forms of principles-based regulation and sets out seven paradoxes it faces, including interpretation, compliance, enforcement and trust: principles need shared understanding to work.
  • Parker, C. (2013). Twenty years of responsive regulation: An appreciation and appraisal. Regulation & Governance, 7(1), 2–13. https://doi.org/10.1111/rego.12006 Peer-reviewed
    Introduces a special issue marking 20 years of Ayres and Braithwaite's responsive regulation, appraising its influence on regulators and the questions it left open.
  • Braithwaite, J. (2002). Rules and principles: A theory of legal certainty. Australian Journal of Legal Philosophy, 27, 47–82. https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/journals/AUJlLegPhil/2002/2.html Peer-reviewed
    Argues that in complex areas precise rules can deliver less certainty than principles, because rules multiply and invite gaming, and proposes combining binding principles with non-binding rules.
  • Black, J., Hopper, M., & Band, C. (2007). Making a success of principles-based regulation. Law and Financial Markets Review, 1(3), 191–206. https://doi.org/10.1080/17521440.2007.11427879 Peer-reviewed
    Discusses the UK Financial Services Authority's move to principles-based regulation and what regulators and firms need for it to work, including guidance, dialogue and credible enforcement.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Stigler's theory of capture, analyse the criticisms of Australian financial regulators before the Hayne Royal Commission. Does capture explain what happened, or are there better explanations?
  2. Using Becker's model, explain why maximum penalties were linked to benefits obtained and turnover in the 2019 reforms.

Essay question: Are larger penalties the best way to deter corporate misconduct? Use theory and evidence.

Module 8 Boards, directors and governance

Governance theories, directors' duties and what boards need to know about risk.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Compare agency, stewardship and stakeholder theories of governance.
  2. Analyse how case law shapes directors' oversight of risk.

Core pages

  1. Corporate governance theories: agency, stewardship and stakeholder
  2. Directors' and officers' duties: key Australian cases
  3. Board risk reporting design

Required reading

  • Jensen, M. C., & Meckling, W. H. (1976). Theory of the firm: Managerial behavior, agency costs and ownership structure. Journal of Financial Economics, 3(4), 305–360. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0304405X7690026X Peer-reviewed
    The foundation of agency theory in corporate governance: when managers act for owners, their interests diverge, creating agency costs of monitoring, bonding and residual loss.
  • Davis, J. H., Schoorman, F. D., & Donaldson, L. (1997). Toward a stewardship theory of management. Academy of Management Review, 22(1), 20–47. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1997.9707180258 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out stewardship theory as an alternative to agency theory: managers can be motivated to act in the organisation's interest, so governance should empower as well as control.
  • Herzberg, A., & Anderson, H. (2012). Stepping stones: From corporate fault to directors' personal civil liability. Federal Law Review, 40(2), 181–205. https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/journals/FedLRev/2012/8.html Peer-reviewed
    Analyses the 'stepping stones' approach in which a company's contravention becomes the basis for finding that directors breached their duty of care, and asks whether this derivative liability is justified.
  • Hanrahan, P., & Bednall, T. (2021). From stepping-stones to throwing stones: Officers' liability for corporate compliance failures after Cassimatis. Federal Law Review, 49(3), 380–409. https://doi.org/10.1177/0067205X211016573 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews officers' liability for corporate compliance failures after the Cassimatis appeal, including judicial criticism of stepping stone cases, and proposes legislative reform to rebalance individual liability.
Further reading
  • Donaldson, T., & Preston, L. E. (1995). The stakeholder theory of the corporation: Concepts, evidence, and implications. Academy of Management Review, 20(1), 65–91. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1995.9503271992 Peer-reviewed
    Distinguishes descriptive, instrumental and normative versions of stakeholder theory and argues the normative base, why stakeholders' interests matter in their own right, is fundamental.
  • Hillman, A. J., & Dalziel, T. (2003). Boards of directors and firm performance: Integrating agency and resource dependence perspectives. Academy of Management Review, 28(3), 383–396. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2003.10196729 Peer-reviewed
    Integrates two board roles, monitoring management and providing resources such as expertise and networks, and argues that directors' human and social capital affects both.
  • Adams, R. B., Hermalin, B. E., & Weisbach, M. S. (2010). The role of boards of directors in corporate governance: A conceptual framework and survey. Journal of Economic Literature, 48(1), 58–107. https://doi.org/10.1257/jel.48.1.58 Peer-reviewed
    Surveys research on what determines board composition and what boards do, stressing that the two are jointly determined, which makes causal claims about 'good' boards hard to establish.
  • Aebi, V., Sabato, G., & Schmid, M. (2012). Risk management, corporate governance, and bank performance in the financial crisis. Journal of Banking & Finance, 36(12), 3213–3226. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2011.10.020 Peer-reviewed
    Banks whose chief risk officer reported directly to the board performed significantly better in the 2007–08 crisis than those where the CRO reported to the CEO.
  • Eppler, M. J., & Mengis, J. (2004). The concept of information overload: A review of literature from organization science, accounting, marketing, MIS, and related disciplines. The Information Society, 20(5), 325–344. https://doi.org/10.1080/01972240490507974 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews 30 years of research on information overload: beyond a point, more information reduces decision quality, and the causes, effects and countermeasures are well documented.

Seminar questions

  1. Using agency theory, explain why the Hayne Royal Commission focused on conflicts of interest in super. Which agency costs were most visible?
  2. Explain the stepping stones approach in your own words. Is it a fair way to hold directors accountable? Draw on Herzberg and Anderson and on Zhou.

Essay question: Which theory of governance best explains the design of Australia's accountability regimes?

Module 9 Culture, incentives and misconduct

Why misconduct happens, how incentives shape risk-taking and whether culture can be measured.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Evaluate the evidence on misconduct, peer effects and incentives.
  2. Critically assess methods for measuring risk culture.

Core pages

  1. Misconduct in financial services: what the research shows
  2. Remuneration, incentives and risk-taking: the evidence
  3. Assessing and changing risk culture
  4. Reasonable steps and consequence management

Required reading

  • Egan, M., Matvos, G., & Seru, A. (2019). The market for financial adviser misconduct. Journal of Political Economy, 127(1), 233–295. https://doi.org/10.1086/700735 Peer-reviewed
    About 7% of US financial advisers have misconduct records (over 15% at some large firms), a third of those are repeat offenders, and many are rehired by other firms, some of which appear to specialise in misconduct.
  • Dimmock, S. G., Gerken, W. C., & Graham, N. P. (2018). Is fraud contagious? Coworker influence on misconduct by financial advisors. The Journal of Finance, 73(3), 1417–1450. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12613 Peer-reviewed
    Using firm mergers as a natural experiment, finds advisers are more likely to commit misconduct when their new coworkers have misconduct histories: misconduct spreads through peers.
  • Holmström, B., & Milgrom, P. (1991). Multitask principal-agent analyses: Incentive contracts, asset ownership, and job design. Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization, 7(special issue), 24–52. https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24 Peer-reviewed
    When people do several tasks but only some are measurable, strong incentives on the measured tasks divert effort from the others, so weak incentives can be optimal.
  • Fahlenbrach, R., & Stulz, R. M. (2011). Bank CEO incentives and the credit crisis. Journal of Financial Economics, 99(1), 11–26. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2010.08.010 Peer-reviewed
    Banks whose CEOs' incentives were better aligned with shareholders did not perform better in the crisis, and some evidence suggests they did worse; CEOs held their shares and suffered large losses.
Further reading
  • Rahwan, Z., Yoeli, E., & Fasolo, B. (2019). Heterogeneity in banker culture and its influence on dishonesty. Nature, 575(7782), 345–349. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1741-y Peer-reviewed
    A larger replication across five populations did not find that reminding bankers of their profession made them significantly more dishonest, or that any effect was unique to bankers.
  • Zingales, L. (2015). Presidential address: Does finance benefit society? The Journal of Finance, 70(4), 1327–1363. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12295 Peer-reviewed
    Argues academics overestimate the social benefits of finance, and that without proper rules finance can easily become rent-seeking, eroding public trust.
  • Gneezy, U., Meier, S., & Rey-Biel, P. (2011). When and why incentives (don't) work to modify behavior. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 25(4), 191–210. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.25.4.191 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews evidence that financial incentives can crowd out intrinsic motivation and change how people see a task, so incentives sometimes backfire.
  • Schein, E. H. (1990). Organizational culture. American Psychologist, 45, 109–119. Peer-reviewed
    Defines organisational culture in three levels, visible artefacts, espoused values and underlying assumptions, and argues that the deepest level is what drives behaviour and is hardest to change.
  • Palermo, T., Power, M., & Ashby, S. (2017). Navigating institutional complexity: The production of risk culture in the financial sector. Journal of Management Studies, 54(2), 154–181. https://doi.org/10.1111/joms.12241 Peer-reviewed
    Based on four years of fieldwork in UK finance, shows how 'risk culture' became an object of regulation and management, and how conflicting goals of risk-taking and precaution made it hard to define and change.
  • Sheedy, E., & Griffin, B. (2018). Risk governance, structures, culture, and behavior: A view from the inside. Corporate Governance: An International Review, 26(1), 4–22. https://doi.org/10.1111/corg.12200 Peer-reviewed
    Australian research surveying bank staff: favourable risk culture together with effective risk structures was associated with more desirable and less undesirable risk behaviour.

Seminar questions

  1. Egan and colleagues found misconduct concentrated in some firms and rehired by others. What does this suggest about reference checking and the role of the Financial Advisers Register?
  2. Use Holmström and Milgrom's multitask model to explain why sales incentives led to poor advice in cases examined by the Royal Commission.

Essay question: Is misconduct in financial services best explained by bad apples, bad barrels or bad incentives?

Module 10 Speaking up and fraud

Who blows the whistle and why, and what fraud theory adds to controls.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Explain the research on whistleblowing decisions and retaliation.
  2. Critically evaluate the fraud triangle and its extensions.

Core pages

  1. Whistleblowing: research on who speaks up and why
  2. Fraud theory beyond the triangle

Required reading

  • Near, J. P., & Miceli, M. P. (1985). Organizational dissidence: The case of whistle-blowing. Journal of Business Ethics, 4(1), 1–16. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00382668 Peer-reviewed
    Defines whistleblowing and proposes a model of the decisions made by people who believe they have seen wrongdoing, and of how organisations respond, drawing on motivation and power.
  • Mesmer-Magnus, J. R., & Viswesvaran, C. (2005). Whistleblowing in organizations: An examination of correlates of whistleblowing intentions, actions, and retaliation. Journal of Business Ethics, 62(3), 277–297. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-005-0849-1 Peer-reviewed
    A meta-analysis of 26 samples (18,781 people): personal, situational and wrongdoing factors predict intentions to blow the whistle more strongly than actual whistleblowing, and retaliation is best predicted by context.
  • Dorminey, J., Fleming, A. S., Kranacher, M.-J., & Riley, R. A., Jr. (2012). The evolution of fraud theory. Issues in Accounting Education, 27(2), 555–579. https://doi.org/10.2308/iace-50131 Peer-reviewed
    Revisits the fraud triangle in the light of later research and anti-fraud practice, and proposes a broader meta-model of fraud for teaching and research.
  • Free, C. (2015). Looking through the fraud triangle: A review and call for new directions. Meditari Accountancy Research, 23(2), 175–196. https://doi.org/10.1108/MEDAR-02-2015-0009 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews research built on the fraud triangle and argues for new directions, including group and organisational influences that the individual-focused triangle leaves out.
Further reading
  • Dyck, A., Morse, A., & Zingales, L. (2010). Who blows the whistle on corporate fraud? The Journal of Finance, 65(6), 2213–2253. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2010.01614.x Peer-reviewed
    In large US corporate frauds from 1996 to 2004, employees (19%), industry regulators (16%) and the media (14%) revealed more frauds than auditors (14%) or the SEC (6%); monetary incentives help explain employee whistleblowing.
  • Detert, J. R., & Edmondson, A. C. (2011). Implicit voice theories: Taken-for-granted rules of self-censorship at work. Academy of Management Journal, 54(3), 461–488. https://doi.org/10.5465/amj.2011.61967925 Peer-reviewed
    Across four studies, identifies widely held, taken-for-granted beliefs about when speaking up at work is risky, which lead people to stay silent even when their own managers are open.
  • Murphy, P. R., & Dacin, M. T. (2011). Psychological pathways to fraud: Understanding and preventing fraud in organizations. Journal of Business Ethics, 101(4), 601–618. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-011-0741-0 Peer-reviewed
    Identifies three psychological pathways to fraud: lack of awareness (moral intuition and disengagement), rationalisation, and negative emotion, with implications for prevention.
  • Morales, J., Gendron, Y., & Guénin-Paracini, H. (2014). The construction of the risky individual and vigilant organization: A genealogy of the fraud triangle. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 39(3), 170–194. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2014.01.006 Peer-reviewed
    A history of how the fraud triangle became dominant, arguing that it constructs fraud as a matter of risky individuals and vigilant organisations, which shapes how fraud is understood and controlled.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Near and Miceli's model, analyse why a staff member might not report a fee error they suspect is deliberate. Which stage is the biggest barrier?
  2. Apply the fraud triangle to a case of fees charged for no service. Does it explain the conduct well? What does it miss?

Essay question: Design a speak-up and fraud control program informed by the research. What would you do differently from common practice?

Module 11 Consumers, disclosure and superannuation

Behavioural economics of super, the limits of disclosure and what remediation owes consumers.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Apply behavioural research on defaults and inertia to super.
  2. Evaluate disclosure as a consumer protection tool and the principles of remediation.

Core pages

  1. Behavioural economics of super: defaults, inertia and choice
  2. Misleading or deceptive conduct: the legal tests
  3. Remediation calculations in practice
  4. Unit pricing and unit pricing errors

Required reading

  • Madrian, B. C., & Shea, D. F. (2001). The power of suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) participation and savings behavior. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 116(4), 1149–1187. https://doi.org/10.1162/003355301753265543 Peer-reviewed
    When a US employer switched to automatic enrolment, retirement plan participation rose sharply, and many employees stayed at the default contribution rate and investment choice.
  • Thaler, R. H., & Benartzi, S. (2004). Save More Tomorrow: Using behavioral economics to increase employee saving. Journal of Political Economy, 112(S1), S164–S187. https://doi.org/10.1086/380085 Peer-reviewed
    A program where employees committed in advance to save part of future pay rises: 78% of those offered it joined, and average saving rates rose from 3.5% to 11.6% over 28 months.
  • Bakos, Y., Marotta-Wurgler, F., & Trossen, D. R. (2014). Does anyone read the fine print? Consumer attention to standard-form contracts. The Journal of Legal Studies, 43(1), 1–35. https://doi.org/10.1086/674424 Peer-reviewed
    Tracking 48,154 shoppers at 90 software companies' websites, only one or two in 1,000 opened the licence agreement, and most who did read very little of it.
  • Loewenstein, G., Sunstein, C. R., & Golman, R. (2014). Disclosure: Psychology changes everything. Annual Review of Economics, 6, 391–419. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-economics-080213-041341 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews how limited and motivated attention and biased probability judgements can weaken or reverse the effects of mandatory disclosure, and when simplified, standardised or comparative disclosure works better.
Further reading
  • Bateman, H., Eckert, C., Geweke, J., Louviere, J., Satchell, S., & Thorp, S. (2016). Risk presentation and portfolio choice. Review of Finance, 20(1), 201–229. https://doi.org/10.1093/rof/rfv001 Peer-reviewed
    Australian choice experiment: people made choices inconsistent with basic principles of rational choice in about a quarter of cases, and the rate varied with how investment risk was described.
  • Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409–425. https://doi.org/10.1086/651235 Peer-reviewed
    A meta-analysis of 50 experiments found an average choice overload effect of about zero, with large variation between studies: overload happens in some conditions but is not universal.
  • Delmas, M. A., & Burbano, V. C. (2011). The drivers of greenwashing. California Management Review, 54(1), 64–87. https://doi.org/10.1525/cmr.2011.54.1.64 Peer-reviewed
    Explains greenwashing through external (regulatory and market), organisational and individual drivers, and argues weak and uncertain regulation is a major enabler.
  • Innes, R. (1999). Remediation and self-reporting in optimal law enforcement. Journal of Public Economics, 72(3), 379–393. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272798001017 Peer-reviewed
    Adds remediation to the theory of self-reporting: firms that self-report can be required to fix the harm every time, whereas non-reporters fix it only if they are caught, strengthening the case for self-reporting.
  • Gillespie, N., & Dietz, G. (2009). Trust repair after an organization-level failure. Academy of Management Review, 34(1), 127–145. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2009.35713319 Peer-reviewed
    A framework for repairing trust after organisational failures, arguing that repair must address the whole system: leadership, culture, strategy, structures and policies, external governance and reputation.
  • Kim, P. H., Ferrin, D. L., Cooper, C. D., & Dirks, K. T. (2004). Removing the shadow of suspicion: The effects of apology versus denial for repairing competence- versus integrity-based trust violations. Journal of Applied Psychology, 89(1), 104–118. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104 Peer-reviewed
    Experiments showing apologies repair trust better after competence failures, while integrity failures are harder to repair by apology, which can confirm the violation.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Madrian and Shea's findings, explain why the design of MySuper products matters more than the quality of choice products for most members.
  2. Apply the four-step analysis to a real financial product advertisement. What is the dominant message, and would the fine print change it for an ordinary reader?

Essay question: “Disclosure is necessary but not sufficient.” Discuss with reference to super and the research on consumer behaviour.

Module 12 Emerging risks: cyber, climate and financial crime

Economic perspectives on three risks that challenge traditional risk management.

Learning outcomes

By the end of this module you should be able to:

  1. Apply economic reasoning to cyber security investment and insurance.
  2. Evaluate the evidence on climate risk pricing and on AML/CTF effectiveness.

Core pages

  1. The economics of cyber risk
  2. Climate risk: the research
  3. Does AML/CTF work? The research

Required reading

  • Anderson, R., & Moore, T. (2006). The economics of information security. Science, 314(5799), 610–613. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1130992 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that security failures are often failures of incentives rather than technology, for example when the party who could prevent harm is not the one who bears it.
  • Gordon, L. A., & Loeb, M. P. (2002). The economics of information security investment. ACM Transactions on Information and System Security, 5(4), 438–457. https://doi.org/10.1145/581271.581274 Peer-reviewed
    The Gordon-Loeb model: under its assumptions, the optimal investment to protect information never exceeds about 37% of the expected loss from a breach.
  • Giglio, S., Kelly, B., & Stroebel, J. (2021). Climate finance. Annual Review of Financial Economics, 13, 15–36. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-financial-102620-103311 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews research on how climate risks affect asset prices and how to discount long-run climate costs, and sets out open questions for the field.
  • Bolton, P., & Kacperczyk, M. (2021). Do investors care about carbon risk? Journal of Financial Economics, 142(2), 517–549. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2021.05.008 Peer-reviewed
    Finds that US firms with higher carbon emissions earn higher stock returns, consistent with investors demanding compensation for exposure to carbon risk.
Further reading
  • Eling, M., & Wirfs, J. (2019). What are the actual costs of cyber risk events? European Journal of Operational Research, 272(3), 1109–1119. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2018.07.021 Peer-reviewed
    Uses operational loss data and extreme value methods to show cyber losses are heavy-tailed: most are small, but a few extreme events dominate, so averages understate the risk.
  • Biener, C., Eling, M., & Wirfs, J. H. (2015). Insurability of cyber risk: An empirical analysis. The Geneva Papers on Risk and Insurance – Issues and Practice, 40(1), 131–158. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2577286 Peer-reviewed
    Analyses 994 cyber loss events and discusses what limits the insurability of cyber risk, including correlated losses, lack of data and information asymmetry.
  • Kamiya, S., Kang, J.-K., Kim, J., Milidonis, A., & Stulz, R. M. (2021). Risk management, firm reputation, and the impact of successful cyberattacks on target firms. Journal of Financial Economics, 139(3), 719–749. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2019.05.019 Peer-reviewed
    Successful cyberattacks that expose personal financial information cause shareholder losses far larger than the direct costs, and affected firms tend to strengthen risk management afterwards.
  • Romanosky, S. (2016). Examining the costs and causes of cyber incidents. Journal of Cybersecurity, 2(2), 121–135. https://doi.org/10.1093/cybsec/tyw001 Peer-reviewed
    Analyses a large dataset of cyber incidents and their costs, finding the typical cost smaller than often claimed, which raises questions about firms' incentives to invest in security.
  • Battiston, S., Mandel, A., Monasterolo, I., Schütze, F., & Visentin, G. (2017). A climate stress-test of the financial system. Nature Climate Change, 7, 283–288. https://doi.org/10.1038/nclimate3255 Peer-reviewed
    Maps financial institutions' exposures to climate-policy-relevant sectors and shows how losses could be amplified through interconnections between institutions.
  • Krueger, P., Sautner, Z., & Starks, L. T. (2020). The importance of climate risks for institutional investors. The Review of Financial Studies, 33(3), 1067–1111. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz137 Peer-reviewed
    A survey of institutional investors finding they believe climate risks matter financially and have begun to materialise, and that many prefer engagement to divestment.

Seminar questions

  1. Identify an externality in cyber security affecting super fund members. Who bears the cost, and what could change the incentives?
  2. Bolton and Kacperczyk find higher returns for higher emitters. Is this evidence that markets price carbon risk, or that they misprice it? What evidence would help decide?

Essay question: What do cyber, climate and financial crime risk have in common that makes them hard to measure and manage?
