Research library

The research behind the Advanced pages: 44 sources, 36 of them peer-reviewed journal articles, each with a short summary in plain English.

Advanced 44 sources Last reviewed: 30 September 2026

How to use this list

  • Every source here is cited on at least one page, or recommended as further reading. Each was checked to exist, with its details confirmed against the publisher or an index such as PubMed, before it was added.
  • References follow APA 7th style. The DOI link goes to the publisher's page. Many articles are paywalled, but university and state libraries often give free access, and authors often share accepted versions on their own or their university's website.
  • The summaries are our own short descriptions, not the authors' abstracts. Read the original before relying on a finding.
  • Peer review is a quality filter, not a guarantee. Look at how each study was done, where and when, and whether later research agrees.

Theories of risk, accidents and reliability

  • Vaughan, D. (1996). The Challenger launch decision: Risky technology, culture, and deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press.
    A sociological study of the Challenger disaster that introduced the 'normalization of deviance': repeated anomalies that caused no harm gradually came to be accepted as normal.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, KRI design and statistical thresholds

Judgement, bias and decision-making

  • Baron, J., & Hershey, J. C. (1988). Outcome bias in decision evaluation. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 54(4), 569–579. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569 Peer-reviewed
    Across five studies, people rated the same decision as better reasoned, and the decision-maker as more competent, when it happened to turn out well.
    Also relevant to: Incidents, root causes and learning
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Esser, J. K. (1998). Alive and well after 25 years: A review of groupthink research. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 73(2–3), 116–141. https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2758 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews 25 years of historical case studies and laboratory tests of groupthink, identifies theoretical and methodological problems in the research, and concludes that the idea remains a useful guide.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Fischhoff, B. (1975). Hindsight is not equal to foresight: The effect of outcome knowledge on judgment under uncertainty. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1(3), 288–299. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.1.3.288 Peer-reviewed
    The first experimental demonstration of hindsight bias: people told an outcome judge it to have been more likely, and more foreseeable, than people who were not told.
    Also relevant to: Incidents, root causes and learning
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302 Peer-reviewed
    Explains why project forecasts are systematically optimistic and sets out reference class forecasting: basing estimates on how comparable past projects actually turned out.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Gigerenzer, G., & Gaissmaier, W. (2011). Heuristic decision making. Annual Review of Psychology, 62, 451–482. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-120709-145346 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews research showing that simple heuristics, which ignore part of the information, can make accurate decisions in uncertain environments, challenging the view that heuristics are simply errors.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Janis, I. L. (1982). Groupthink: Psychological studies of policy decisions and fiascoes (2nd ed.). Houghton Mifflin.
    The original account of groupthink: cohesive groups under pressure suppress dissent and reach poor decisions.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Kahneman, D., & Lovallo, D. (1993). Timid choices and bold forecasts: A cognitive perspective on risk taking. Management Science, 39(1), 17–31. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.39.1.17 Peer-reviewed
    Explains why decision-makers are both too timid (judging each risk in isolation) and too bold (forecasting from an 'inside view' of their own plans), and recommends taking an 'outside view' based on reference classes.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Klein, G. (2007). Performing a project premortem. Harvard Business Review, 85(9), 18–19. https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem
    A short practitioner piece introducing the premortem: before a decision, imagine it has failed and list the reasons why.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Lichtenstein, S., Fischhoff, B., & Phillips, L. D. (1982). Calibration of probabilities: The state of the art to 1980. In D. Kahneman & P. Slovic & A. Tversky (Eds.), Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases (pp. 306–334). Cambridge University Press.
    A review of calibration research: people's confidence usually exceeds their accuracy, especially on hard questions.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Moore, D. A., & Healy, P. J. (2008). The trouble with overconfidence. Psychological Review, 115(2), 502–517. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.115.2.502 Peer-reviewed
    Separates three kinds of overconfidence (overestimation, overplacement and overprecision) and shows overprecision, being too sure of one's own estimates, is the most persistent.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Morewedge, C. K., Yoon, H., Scopelliti, I., Symborski, C. W., Korris, J. H., & Kassam, K. S. (2015). Debiasing decisions: Improved decision making with a single training intervention. Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2(1), 129–140. https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215600886 Peer-reviewed
    Two longitudinal experiments found that a single training session, a game or an instructional video, produced medium to large reductions in several biases, and the improvements persisted at later follow-up.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Samuelson, W., & Zeckhauser, R. (1988). Status quo bias in decision making. Journal of Risk and Uncertainty, 1(1), 7–59. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564 Peer-reviewed
    Experiments and field data (including retirement plan choices) show people disproportionately stick with the current option or default, even when alternatives are better.
    Also relevant to: Superannuation and pensions
  • Staw, B. M. (1976). Knee-deep in the big muddy: A study of escalating commitment to a chosen course of action. Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 16(1), 27–44. https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-5073(76)90005-2 Peer-reviewed
    In a simulated investment decision, 240 business students committed the most extra money to a losing course of action when they were personally responsible for the original choice.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124 Peer-reviewed
    The classic account of three heuristics people use to judge probability (representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment) and the systematic errors each produces.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions

Incidents, root causes and learning

  • Card, A. J. (2017). The problem with '5 whys'. BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(8), 671–677. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005849 Peer-reviewed
    Critiques the popular '5 whys' technique: it assumes a single, linear chain of causes, oversimplifies complex events and has little evidence that it works for serious incidents.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Dekker, S. (2016). Just culture: Restoring trust and accountability in your organization (3rd ed.). CRC Press.
    A practitioner-focused book on responding to incidents in ways that balance accountability with learning, including restorative approaches.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Dekker, S. W. A., & Breakey, H. (2016). 'Just culture:' Improving safety by achieving substantive, procedural and restorative justice. Safety Science, 85, 187–193. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925753516000321 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that a just culture needs more than rules about when to punish: organisations should also attend to fair process and to restoring relationships after an incident, so people keep reporting.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Hibbert, P. D., Thomas, M. J. W., Deakin, A., Runciman, W. B., Braithwaite, J., Lomax, S., Prescott, J., Gorrie, G., Szczygielski, A., Surwald, T., & Fraser, C. (2018). Are root cause analyses recommendations effective and sustainable? An observational study. International Journal for Quality in Health Care, 30(2), 124–131. https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzx181 Peer-reviewed
    Australian study of 227 root cause analyses of sentinel events in Victoria (2010 to 2015): of 1,137 recommendations, 8% were strong, 44% medium and 48% weak, and about 15% of analyses made only weak recommendations.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Kellogg, K. M., Hettinger, Z., Shah, M., Wears, R. L., Sellers, C. R., Squires, M., & Fairbanks, R. J. (2017). Our current approach to root cause analysis: Is it contributing to our failure to improve patient safety? BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(5), 381–387. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005991 Peer-reviewed
    A review of 302 root cause analyses at a US hospital system found that many proposed solutions were weak, mostly training, process changes and enforcing existing policy.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Leveson, N. (2004). A new accident model for engineering safer systems. Safety Science, 42(4), 237–270. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-7535(03)00047-X Peer-reviewed
    Proposes STAMP, an accident model based on systems theory: accidents arise from inadequate control of interactions in a system, not just chains of failed components, so analysis must include management and organisational control.
    Also relevant to: Theories of risk, accidents and reliability
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Peerally, M. F., Carr, S., Waring, J., & Dixon-Woods, M. (2017). The problem with root cause analysis. BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(5), 417–422. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005511 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out problems with root cause analysis as applied in healthcare, including a misleading search for a single cause and weak, poorly followed-up fixes. Argues it was borrowed from other industries without enough attention to what makes it work there.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Reason, J. (2000). Human error: Models and management. BMJ, 320(7237), 768–770. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.320.7237.768 Peer-reviewed
    Contrasts the 'person approach' to error (blame, retraining) with the 'system approach', and introduces the Swiss cheese model of layered defences with active failures and latent conditions.
    Also relevant to: Theories of risk, accidents and reliability
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls

Risk measurement, capital and stress testing

  • Acerbi, C., & Tasche, D. (2002). On the coherence of expected shortfall. Journal of Banking & Finance, 26(7), 1487–1503. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0378-4266(02)00283-2 Peer-reviewed
    Compares definitions of expected shortfall and identifies the one that is a coherent risk measure for any loss distribution, supporting its use in place of value at risk.
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk
  • Artzner, P., Delbaen, F., Eber, J.-M., & Heath, D. (1999). Coherent measures of risk. Mathematical Finance, 9(3), 203–228. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9965.00068 Peer-reviewed
    Proposes four properties a sensible risk measure should have (the 'coherence' axioms) and shows that value at risk can fail one of them, subadditivity, so it can penalise diversification.
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk
  • Chernobai, A., Jorion, P., & Yu, F. (2011). The determinants of operational risk in U.S. financial institutions. Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 46(6), 1683–1725. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109011000500 Peer-reviewed
    Using publicly reported losses from 1980 to 2005, finds most operational losses trace to breakdowns of internal control, and that losses are more likely at younger, more complex firms with higher credit risk and stronger CEO option-based pay.
    Also relevant to: Internal control, testing and audit
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk, APS 115 Operational risk capital
  • Cope, E. W., Mignola, G., Antonini, G., & Ugoccioni, R. (2009). Challenges and pitfalls in measuring operational risk from loss data. Journal of Operational Risk, 4(4), 3–27. https://doi.org/10.21314/JOP.2009.069 Peer-reviewed
    Shows how hard it is to estimate operational risk at very high quantiles such as 99.9% from the loss data banks actually have, and how unstable model results and validation can be.
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk, APS 115 Operational risk capital
  • de Fontnouvelle, P., DeJesus-Rueff, V., Jordan, J. S., & Rosengren, E. S. (2006). Capital and risk: New evidence on implications of large operational losses. Journal of Money, Credit and Banking, 38(7), 1819–1846. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=648164 Peer-reviewed
    Uses external data on large operational losses at banks to estimate how heavy the tail of losses is, and what this implies for the operational risk capital large banks need.
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk, APS 115 Operational risk capital
  • Embrechts, P., McNeil, A., & Straumann, D. (2002). Correlation and dependence in risk management: Properties and pitfalls. In M. A. H. Dempster (Ed.), Risk management: Value at risk and beyond (pp. 176–223). Cambridge University Press.
    A widely used explanation of why linear correlation is a poor summary of dependence outside normal distributions, and why copulas and tail dependence matter for aggregating risks.
    Cited on: Risk aggregation and correlation
  • Kretzschmar, G., McNeil, A. J., & Kirchner, A. (2010). Integrated models of capital adequacy: Why banks are undercapitalised. Journal of Banking & Finance, 34(12), 2838–2850. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2010.02.028 Peer-reviewed
    Compares modular, correlation-based aggregation with a fully integrated model driven by economic scenarios, and finds the modular approach common in practice could leave banks undercapitalised.
    Cited on: Risk aggregation and correlation
  • Longin, F., & Solnik, B. (2001). Extreme correlation of international equity markets. The Journal of Finance, 56(2), 649–676. https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00340 Peer-reviewed
    Using extreme value theory, finds that correlation between international share markets rises in bear markets but not in bull markets: diversification weakens when it is needed most.
    Cited on: Risk aggregation and correlation
  • Peters, G. W., Shevchenko, P. V., Hassani, B., & Chapelle, A. (2016). Should the advanced measurement approach be replaced with the standardized measurement approach for operational risk? Journal of Operational Risk, 11(3), 1–49. https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.02319 Peer-reviewed
    Stress tests the Basel Committee's proposed standardised approach and argues it can be unstable, insensitive to risk and super-additive, and proposes alternatives.
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk, APS 115 Operational risk capital
  • Rosenberg, J. V., & Schuermann, T. (2006). A general approach to integrated risk management with skewed, fat-tailed risks. Journal of Financial Economics, 79(3), 569–614. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X05001509 Peer-reviewed
    Builds the joint distribution of market, credit and operational risk for a large bank using copulas, and shows how the choice of dependence assumptions changes total risk.
    Cited on: Risk aggregation and correlation

Indicators, metrics and control charts

  • Campbell, D. T. (1979). Assessing the impact of planned social change. Evaluation and Program Planning, 2(1), 67–90. https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189(79)90048-X Peer-reviewed
    Source of 'Campbell's law': the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the process it is meant to monitor.
    Also relevant to: Regulation, compliance and enforcement
    Cited on: KRI design and statistical thresholds
  • Ittner, C. D., & Larcker, D. F. (1998). Are nonfinancial measures leading indicators of financial performance? An analysis of customer satisfaction. Journal of Accounting Research, 36, 1–35. https://doi.org/10.2307/2491304 Peer-reviewed
    Tests whether customer satisfaction predicts later financial results, finding it is a leading indicator in some settings and that the relationship is not simply linear: an early empirical test of the 'leading indicator' idea.
    Cited on: KRI design and statistical thresholds
  • Kerr, S. (1975). On the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B. Academy of Management Journal, 18(4), 769–783. https://doi.org/10.2307/255378 Peer-reviewed
    A classic essay with examples showing that organisations often reward behaviour they don't want while hoping for behaviour they aren't rewarding, and that people respond to what is measured and rewarded.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: KRI design and statistical thresholds
  • Nelson, L. S. (1984). The Shewhart control chart: Tests for special causes. Journal of Quality Technology, 16(4), 237–239. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224065.1984.11978921
    A short technical note setting out eight tests (the 'Nelson rules') for spotting non-random patterns on a control chart, such as runs of points on one side of the mean.
    Cited on: KRI design and statistical thresholds
  • Scandizzo, S. (2005). Risk mapping and key risk indicators in operational risk management. Economic Notes, 34(2), 231–256. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0391-5026.2005.00150.x Peer-reviewed
    Sets out a method for mapping operational risks to the steps of a business process, then choosing key risk indicators that estimate likelihood and severity at each step and designing controls around them.
    Also relevant to: Risk measurement, capital and stress testing
    Cited on: KRI design and statistical thresholds
  • Woodall, W. H. (2000). Controversies and contradictions in statistical process control. Journal of Quality Technology, 32(4), 341–350. https://doi.org/10.1080/00224065.2000.11980013 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews debates in statistical process control, including whether control charts are hypothesis tests, how to model their performance and how to compare competing methods.
    Cited on: KRI design and statistical thresholds

Internal control, testing and audit

  • Doyle, J., Ge, W., & McVay, S. (2007). Determinants of weaknesses in internal control over financial reporting. Journal of Accounting and Economics, 44(1–2), 193–223. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacceco.2006.10.003 Peer-reviewed
    Across 779 US firms disclosing material weaknesses, control failures were more likely in firms that were smaller, younger, financially weaker, more complex, growing fast or restructuring; entity-wide weaknesses clustered in weaker firms.
    Cited on: Control testing: sampling and assurance confidence
  • Durney, M., Elder, R. J., & Glover, S. M. (2014). Field data on accounting error rates and audit sampling. Auditing: A Journal of Practice & Theory, 33(2), 79–110. https://doi.org/10.2308/ajpt-50669 Peer-reviewed
    Uses data from 160 real audit sampling applications at a large audit firm to study how often errors occur in accounting populations and how auditors sample after the Sarbanes-Oxley reforms.
    Cited on: Control testing: sampling and assurance confidence
  • Elder, R. J., Akresh, A. D., Glover, S. M., Higgs, J. L., & Liljegren, J. (2013). Audit sampling research: A synthesis and implications for future research. Auditing: A Journal of Practice & Theory, 32(Supplement 1), 99–129. https://doi.org/10.2308/ajpt-50394 Peer-reviewed
    Synthesises decades of research on how auditors plan, select and evaluate samples, and sets out open questions for research and practice.
    Cited on: Control testing: sampling and assurance confidence
  • Power, M. (1997). The audit society: Rituals of verification. Oxford University Press.
    Argues that the 'audit explosion' produces comfort and legitimacy as much as real assurance, because checking often focuses on whether systems exist rather than on outcomes.
    Also relevant to: Regulation, compliance and enforcement
    Cited on: Control testing: sampling and assurance confidence

Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability

  • Edmondson, A. (1999). Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams. Administrative Science Quarterly, 44(2), 350–383. https://doi.org/10.2307/2666999 Peer-reviewed
    A study of 51 work teams introduced team psychological safety, a shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks, and linked it to learning behaviour and performance.
    Also relevant to: Whistleblowing, voice and fraud
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
