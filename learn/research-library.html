How to use this list

  • Every source here is cited on at least one page, or recommended as further reading. Each was checked to exist, with its details confirmed against the publisher or an index such as PubMed, before it was added.
  • References follow APA 7th style. The DOI link goes to the publisher's page. Many articles are paywalled, but university and state libraries often give free access, and authors often share accepted versions on their own or their university's website.
  • The summaries are our own short descriptions, not the authors' abstracts. Read the original before relying on a finding.
  • Peer review is a quality filter, not a guarantee. Look at how each study was done, where and when, and whether later research agrees.

Enterprise risk management and risk frameworks

  • Arena, M., Arnaboldi, M., & Azzone, G. (2010). The organizational dynamics of enterprise risk management. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 35(7), 659–675. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2010.07.003 Peer-reviewed
    A seven-year study of three companies showing ERM takes different forms as it meets existing organisational logics, experts and technologies, rather than being implemented in one standard way.
    Cited on: Establishing a GRC system: a step-by-step roadmap, GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research
  • Bromiley, P., McShane, M., Nair, A., & Rustambekov, E. (2015). Enterprise risk management: Review, critique, and research directions. Long Range Planning, 48(4), 265–276. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2014.07.005 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews ERM research and criticises it for weak measures of ERM, limited theory and little evidence on how ERM is actually practised, and sets out research directions.
    Cited on: GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research, Advanced study program, Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  • Ellul, A., & Yerramilli, V. (2013). Stronger risk controls, lower risk: Evidence from U.S. bank holding companies. The Journal of Finance, 68(5), 1757–1803. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12057 Peer-reviewed
    Banks with a stronger, more independent risk management function before the crisis (a higher 'risk management index') had lower tail risk and better performance during the crisis.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: The Three Lines model: research and critique, Advanced study program, Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  • Gordon, L. A., Loeb, M. P., & Tseng, C.-Y. (2009). Enterprise risk management and firm performance: A contingency perspective. Journal of Accounting and Public Policy, 28(4), 301–327. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0278425409000416 Peer-reviewed
    Finds the link between ERM and firm performance depends on matching ERM to five factors: environmental uncertainty, competition, firm size, complexity and board monitoring.
    Cited on: Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  • Hoyt, R. E., & Liebenberg, A. P. (2011). The value of enterprise risk management. Journal of Risk and Insurance, 78(4), 795–822. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6975.2011.01413.x Peer-reviewed
    Studying 275 US publicly traded insurers from 1998 to 2005, finds that firms engaged in ERM have a significantly higher market valuation.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  • McShane, M. K., Nair, A., & Rustambekov, E. (2011). Does enterprise risk management increase firm value? Journal of Accounting, Auditing & Finance, 26(4), 641–658. https://doi.org/10.1177/0148558X11409160 Peer-reviewed
    Using S&P's ERM ratings of insurers, finds firm value rises with traditional risk management capability but not further for firms rated as having more advanced ERM.
    Cited on: Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  • Mikes, A., & Kaplan, R. S. (2015). When one size doesn't fit all: Evolving directions in the research and practice of enterprise risk management. Journal of Applied Corporate Finance, 27(1), 37–40. https://doi.org/10.1111/jacf.12102
    Drawing on a ten-year field project and over 250 interviews with senior risk officers, proposes a contingency theory of ERM: the right 'ERM mix' depends on the organisation's circumstances.
    Cited on: GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research
  • Papazafeiropoulou, A., & Spanaki, K. (2016). Understanding governance, risk and compliance information systems (GRC IS): The experts view. Information Systems Frontiers, 18(6), 1251–1263. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10796-015-9572-3 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews GRC research and interviews professional experts, producing a framework of what to consider when putting GRC systems in place: goals, purpose, stakeholders, requirements before implementation, critical success factors and barriers.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: Establishing a GRC system: a step-by-step roadmap, GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research
  • Power, M. (2009). The risk management of nothing. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 34(6–7), 849–855. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2009.06.001 Peer-reviewed
    A critique of ERM after the financial crisis: an impoverished idea of risk appetite and a 'logic of the audit trail' made ERM boundary-preserving rather than challenging, so its security was limited or illusory.
    Cited on: The Three Lines model: research and critique, GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research, Advanced study program, Does enterprise risk management work? The evidence
  • Racz, N., Weippl, E., & Seufert, A. (2010). A frame of reference for research of integrated governance, risk and compliance (GRC). In B. De Decker & I. Schaumüller-Bichl (Eds.), Communications and multimedia security (Lecture Notes in Computer Science, Vol. 6109) (pp. 106–117). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-13241-4_11 Peer-reviewed
    Develops a definition of integrated GRC from a literature review and earlier surveys, and a frame of reference for research covering its components (governance, risk management, compliance), integration and supporting technology.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research
  • Vicente, P., & Mira da Silva, M. (2011). A conceptual model for integrated governance, risk and compliance. In Advanced information systems engineering (Lecture Notes in Computer Science, Vol. 6741) (pp. 199–213). Springer. https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-21640-4_16 Peer-reviewed
    Proposes a reference conceptual model of the key functions of governance, risk and compliance and how they relate, and evaluates it against OCEG's GRC capability model.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: GRC for complex groups: design choices and the research

Theories of risk, accidents and reliability

  • Kasperson, R. E., Renn, O., Slovic, P., Brown, H. S., Emel, J., Goble, R., Kasperson, J. X., & Ratick, S. (1988). The social amplification of risk: A conceptual framework. Risk Analysis, 8(2), 177–187. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6924.1988.tb01168.x Peer-reviewed
    Proposes that risk events interact with psychological, social, institutional and cultural processes that amplify or attenuate public responses, producing 'ripple effects' far beyond the direct harm.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • La Porte, T. R., & Consolini, P. M. (1991). Working in practice but not in theory: Theoretical challenges of "high-reliability organizations". Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory, 1(1), 19–48. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.jpart.a037070 Peer-reviewed
    Launched high reliability organisation research by studying organisations such as air traffic control that operate hazardous systems with remarkably few failures, and asking how they do it.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • Leveson, N., Dulac, N., Marais, K., & Carroll, J. (2009). Moving beyond normal accidents and high reliability organizations: A systems approach to safety in complex systems. Organization Studies, 30(2–3), 227–249. https://doi.org/10.1177/0170840608101478 Peer-reviewed
    Critiques both normal accident theory and high reliability research, arguing that a systems approach from safety engineering better explains and prevents accidents in complex organisations.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • Perrow, C. (1984). Normal accidents: Living with high-risk technologies. Basic Books.
    Argues that in systems that are both complex and tightly coupled, multiple unexpected failures will interact, so some accidents are 'normal' and cannot be designed away.
    Cited on: Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • Shrivastava, S., Sonpar, K., & Pazzaglia, F. (2009). Normal accident theory versus high reliability theory: A resolution and call for an open systems view of accidents. Human Relations, 62(9), 1357–1390. https://doi.org/10.1177/0018726709339117 Peer-reviewed
    Argues the two theories look at accidents at different points in time and can be reconciled, while noting that both are hard to falsify.
    Cited on: Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • Slovic, P. (1987). Perception of risk. Science, 236(4799), 280–285. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.3563507 Peer-reviewed
    Shows that lay people judge risk on qualitative dimensions, especially 'dread' and 'unknown', not just expected deaths, which explains gaps between expert and public views of risk.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • Vaughan, D. (1996). The Challenger launch decision: Risky technology, culture, and deviance at NASA. University of Chicago Press.
    A sociological study of the Challenger disaster that introduced the 'normalization of deviance': repeated anomalies that caused no harm gradually came to be accepted as normal.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, KRI design and statistical thresholds

Judgement, bias and decision-making

  • Baron, J., & Hershey, J. C. (1988). Outcome bias in decision evaluation. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 54(4), 569–579. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.54.4.569 Peer-reviewed
    Across five studies, people rated the same decision as better reasoned, and the decision-maker as more competent, when it happened to turn out well.
    Also relevant to: Incidents, root causes and learning
    Cited on: Breach significance analysis in practice, Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Esser, J. K. (1998). Alive and well after 25 years: A review of groupthink research. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 73(2–3), 116–141. https://doi.org/10.1006/obhd.1998.2758 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews 25 years of historical case studies and laboratory tests of groupthink, identifies theoretical and methodological problems in the research, and concludes that the idea remains a useful guide.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Fischhoff, B. (1975). Hindsight is not equal to foresight: The effect of outcome knowledge on judgment under uncertainty. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 1(3), 288–299. https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-1523.1.3.288 Peer-reviewed
    The first experimental demonstration of hindsight bias: people told an outcome judge it to have been more likely, and more foreseeable, than people who were not told.
    Also relevant to: Incidents, root causes and learning
    Cited on: Breach significance analysis in practice, Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Flyvbjerg, B. (2006). From Nobel Prize to project management: Getting risks right. Project Management Journal, 37(3), 5–15. https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302 Peer-reviewed
    Explains why project forecasts are systematically optimistic and sets out reference class forecasting: basing estimates on how comparable past projects actually turned out.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Gigerenzer, G., & Gaissmaier, W. (2011). Heuristic decision making. Annual Review of Psychology, 62, 451–482. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-120709-145346 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews research showing that simple heuristics, which ignore part of the information, can make accurate decisions in uncertain environments, challenging the view that heuristics are simply errors.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Janis, I. L. (1982). Groupthink: Psychological studies of policy decisions and fiascoes (2nd ed.). Houghton Mifflin.
    The original account of groupthink: cohesive groups under pressure suppress dissent and reach poor decisions.
    Also relevant to: Boards, directors and corporate governance
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Kahneman, D., & Lovallo, D. (1993). Timid choices and bold forecasts: A cognitive perspective on risk taking. Management Science, 39(1), 17–31. https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.39.1.17 Peer-reviewed
    Explains why decision-makers are both too timid (judging each risk in isolation) and too bold (forecasting from an 'inside view' of their own plans), and recommends taking an 'outside view' based on reference classes.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions, Scenario analysis and stress testing
  • Klein, G. (2007). Performing a project premortem. Harvard Business Review, 85(9), 18–19. https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem
    A short practitioner piece introducing the premortem: before a decision, imagine it has failed and list the reasons why.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Lichtenstein, S., Fischhoff, B., & Phillips, L. D. (1982). Calibration of probabilities: The state of the art to 1980. In D. Kahneman & P. Slovic & A. Tversky (Eds.), Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases (pp. 306–334). Cambridge University Press.
    A review of calibration research: people's confidence usually exceeds their accuracy, especially on hard questions.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Moore, D. A., & Healy, P. J. (2008). The trouble with overconfidence. Psychological Review, 115(2), 502–517. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.115.2.502 Peer-reviewed
    Separates three kinds of overconfidence (overestimation, overplacement and overprecision) and shows overprecision, being too sure of one's own estimates, is the most persistent.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Morewedge, C. K., Yoon, H., Scopelliti, I., Symborski, C. W., Korris, J. H., & Kassam, K. S. (2015). Debiasing decisions: Improved decision making with a single training intervention. Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2(1), 129–140. https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215600886 Peer-reviewed
    Two longitudinal experiments found that a single training session, a game or an instructional video, produced medium to large reductions in several biases, and the improvements persisted at later follow-up.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Samuelson, W., & Zeckhauser, R. (1988). Status quo bias in decision making. Journal of Risk and Uncertainty, 1(1), 7–59. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00055564 Peer-reviewed
    Experiments and field data (including retirement plan choices) show people disproportionately stick with the current option or default, even when alternatives are better.
    Also relevant to: Superannuation and pensions
    Cited on: Behavioural economics of super: defaults, inertia and choice
  • Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409–425. https://doi.org/10.1086/651235 Peer-reviewed
    A meta-analysis of 50 experiments found an average choice overload effect of about zero, with large variation between studies: overload happens in some conditions but is not universal.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Behavioural economics of super: defaults, inertia and choice
  • Staw, B. M. (1976). Knee-deep in the big muddy: A study of escalating commitment to a chosen course of action. Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 16(1), 27–44. https://doi.org/10.1016/0030-5073(76)90005-2 Peer-reviewed
    In a simulated investment decision, 240 business students committed the most extra money to a losing course of action when they were personally responsible for the original choice.
    Cited on: Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions
  • Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124 Peer-reviewed
    The classic account of three heuristics people use to judge probability (representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment) and the systematic errors each produces.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Human factors and cognitive bias in risk decisions

Incidents, root causes and learning

  • Card, A. J. (2017). The problem with '5 whys'. BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(8), 671–677. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005849 Peer-reviewed
    Critiques the popular '5 whys' technique: it assumes a single, linear chain of causes, oversimplifies complex events and has little evidence that it works for serious incidents.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Dekker, S. (2016). Just culture: Restoring trust and accountability in your organization (3rd ed.). CRC Press.
    A practitioner-focused book on responding to incidents in ways that balance accountability with learning, including restorative approaches.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Dekker, S. W. A., & Breakey, H. (2016). 'Just culture:' Improving safety by achieving substantive, procedural and restorative justice. Safety Science, 85, 187–193. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925753516000321 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that a just culture needs more than rules about when to punish: organisations should also attend to fair process and to restoring relationships after an incident, so people keep reporting.
    Also relevant to: Culture, conduct, incentives and accountability
    Cited on: Reasonable steps and consequence management, Advanced study program, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Hibbert, P. D., Thomas, M. J. W., Deakin, A., Runciman, W. B., Braithwaite, J., Lomax, S., Prescott, J., Gorrie, G., Szczygielski, A., Surwald, T., & Fraser, C. (2018). Are root cause analyses recommendations effective and sustainable? An observational study. International Journal for Quality in Health Care, 30(2), 124–131. https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzx181 Peer-reviewed
    Australian study of 227 root cause analyses of sentinel events in Victoria (2010 to 2015): of 1,137 recommendations, 8% were strong, 44% medium and 48% weak, and about 15% of analyses made only weak recommendations.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Kellogg, K. M., Hettinger, Z., Shah, M., Wears, R. L., Sellers, C. R., Squires, M., & Fairbanks, R. J. (2017). Our current approach to root cause analysis: Is it contributing to our failure to improve patient safety? BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(5), 381–387. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005991 Peer-reviewed
    A review of 302 root cause analyses at a US hospital system found that many proposed solutions were weak, mostly training, process changes and enforcing existing policy.
    Cited on: Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Leveson, N. (2004). A new accident model for engineering safer systems. Safety Science, 42(4), 237–270. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0925-7535(03)00047-X Peer-reviewed
    Proposes STAMP, an accident model based on systems theory: accidents arise from inadequate control of interactions in a system, not just chains of failed components, so analysis must include management and organisational control.
    Also relevant to: Theories of risk, accidents and reliability
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls, Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability
  • Peerally, M. F., Carr, S., Waring, J., & Dixon-Woods, M. (2017). The problem with root cause analysis. BMJ Quality & Safety, 26(5), 417–422. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjqs-2016-005511 Peer-reviewed
    Sets out problems with root cause analysis as applied in healthcare, including a misleading search for a single cause and weak, poorly followed-up fixes. Argues it was borrowed from other industries without enough attention to what makes it work there.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls
  • Reason, J. (2000). Human error: Models and management. BMJ, 320(7237), 768–770. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.320.7237.768 Peer-reviewed
    Contrasts the 'person approach' to error (blame, retraining) with the 'system approach', and introduces the Swiss cheese model of layered defences with active failures and latent conditions.
    Also relevant to: Theories of risk, accidents and reliability
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Root cause analysis: techniques and pitfalls, Theories of risk: perception, amplification, normal accidents and high reliability

Risk measurement, capital and stress testing

  • Acerbi, C., & Tasche, D. (2002). On the coherence of expected shortfall. Journal of Banking & Finance, 26(7), 1487–1503. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0378-4266(02)00283-2 Peer-reviewed
    Compares definitions of expected shortfall and identifies the one that is a coherent risk measure for any loss distribution, supporting its use in place of value at risk.
    Cited on: Quantitative operational risk
  • Artzner, P., Delbaen, F., Eber, J.-M., & Heath, D. (1999). Coherent measures of risk. Mathematical Finance, 9(3), 203–228. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9965.00068 Peer-reviewed
    Proposes four properties a sensible risk measure should have (the 'coherence' axioms) and shows that value at risk can fail one of them, subadditivity, so it can penalise diversification.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Quantitative operational risk
  • Borio, C., Drehmann, M., & Tsatsaronis, K. (2014). Stress-testing macro stress testing: Does it live up to expectations? Journal of Financial Stability, 12, 3–15. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1572308913000454 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that macro stress tests before the 2008 crisis failed to flag the vulnerabilities that mattered, because models could not capture the non-linear, self-reinforcing dynamics of real crises; stress tests are better at crisis management than at early warning.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Scenario analysis and stress testing
  • Bradfield, R., Wright, G., Burt, G., Cairns, G., & van der Heijden, K. (2005). The origins and evolution of scenario techniques in long range business planning. Futures, 37(8), 795–812. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.futures.2005.01.003 Peer-reviewed
    Traces the history of scenario methods and classifies them into three main schools, showing that 'scenario analysis' covers quite different techniques with different purposes.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Scenario analysis and stress testing
  • Breuer, T., Jandačka, M., Rheinberger, K., & Summer, M. (2009). How to find plausible, severe, and useful stress scenarios. International Journal of Central Banking, 5(3), 205–224. https://www.ijcb.org/journal/ijcb09q3a7.pdf Peer-reviewed
    Proposes a systematic search for the worst scenarios within a defined region of plausibility, instead of relying on hand-picked scenarios that may miss the combinations that hurt most.
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Scenario analysis and stress testing
  • Chernobai, A., Jorion, P., & Yu, F. (2011). The determinants of operational risk in U.S. financial institutions. Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis, 46(6), 1683–1725. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022109011000500 Peer-reviewed

  • Krueger, P., Sautner, Z., & Starks, L. T. (2020). The importance of climate risks for institutional investors. The Review of Financial Studies, 33(3), 1067–1111. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhz137 Peer-reviewed
    A survey of institutional investors finding they believe climate risks matter financially and have begun to materialise, and that many prefer engagement to divestment.
    Also relevant to: Superannuation and pensions
    Cited on: Advanced study program, Climate risk: the research

Financial crime and AML/CTF

  • Ferwerda, J. (2009). The economics of crime and money laundering: Does anti-money laundering policy reduce crime? Review of Law and Economics, 5(2), 903–929. https://doi.org/10.2202/1555-5879.1421 Peer-reviewed
    Builds an economic model in the Becker tradition and tests it across countries, finding that some AML policy elements, such as the chance of being caught and convicted, are associated with lower crime.
    Cited on: Does AML/CTF work? The research
  • Levi, M., Reuter, P., & Halliday, T. (2018). Can the AML system be evaluated without better data? Crime, Law and Social Change, 69, 307–328. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10611-017-9757-4 Peer-reviewed
    Finds there are no credible estimates of how much money is laundered and little serious use of data in FATF evaluations and national risk assessments, so claims about AML effectiveness are hard to test.
    Also relevant to: Regulation, compliance and enforcement
    Cited on: Does AML/CTF work? The research
  • Pol, R. F. (2020). Anti-money laundering: The world's least effective policy experiment? Together, we can fix it. Policy Design and Practice, 3(1), 73–94. https://doi.org/10.1080/25741292.2020.1725366 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that on available evidence AML policy affects well under 1% of criminal finances while compliance costs far exceed the criminal funds recovered, and calls for outcome-focused evaluation. Its estimates are contested and rely on limited data.
    Also relevant to: Regulation, compliance and enforcement
    Cited on: Does AML/CTF work? The research
  • Takáts, E. (2011). A theory of 'crying wolf': The economics of money laundering enforcement. Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization, 27(1), 32–78. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=979035 Peer-reviewed
    A formal model showing that heavy fines for failing to report can push banks to over-report, diluting the information value of suspicious matter reports ('crying wolf').
    Also relevant to: Regulation, compliance and enforcement
    Cited on: Does AML/CTF work? The research

Trust, redress and remediation

  • Gillespie, N., & Dietz, G. (2009). Trust repair after an organization-level failure. Academy of Management Review, 34(1), 127–145. https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2009.35713319 Peer-reviewed
    A framework for repairing trust after organisational failures, arguing that repair must address the whole system: leadership, culture, strategy, structures and policies, external governance and reputation.
    Cited on: Remediation calculations in practice, Advanced study program
  • Kim, P. H., Ferrin, D. L., Cooper, C. D., & Dirks, K. T. (2004). Removing the shadow of suspicion: The effects of apology versus denial for repairing competence- versus integrity-based trust violations. Journal of Applied Psychology, 89(1), 104–118. https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.1.104 Peer-reviewed
    Experiments showing apologies repair trust better after competence failures, while integrity failures are harder to repair by apology, which can confirm the violation.
    Cited on: Remediation calculations in practice, Advanced study program
