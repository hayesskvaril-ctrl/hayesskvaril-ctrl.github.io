Platform trustees in the spotlight: what ASIC's Report 833 means

ASIC's review of super platform trustees found persistent gaps in the controls meant to stop members' savings being drained by harmful advice fees, risky switching and unusual investments. The lessons reach well beyond platforms.

Intermediate About 8 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

In brief

  • ASIC's Report 833 (29 June 2026) reviewed six platform trustees responsible for over $300 billion in retirement savings, about three-quarters of the money managed by platform trustees.
  • It found persistent gaps in monitoring advice fee deductions, unusual fees and investment patterns, and high-risk switching. In some areas, protections had gone backwards over two years.
  • The review follows the Shield and First Guardian collapses, which affected around 11,000 people and about $1.1 billion. ASIC is pursuing several parties, including two trustees.
  • For risk and compliance teams, the report is a case study in detective controls, escalation and "awareness" dates.

What ASIC found

A platform trustee offers members a menu of investment options, often chosen by the member with a financial adviser. ASIC looked at how well six platform trustees watch for signs that members are being harmed. Its findings included:

  • Advice fees: persistent gaps in controls over advice fees deducted from members' accounts. One trustee proposed allowing advice fees of up to $30,000, well above caps identified in earlier regulatory work.
  • Unusual patterns: weaknesses in spotting unusual fees and investment patterns, and high-risk switching, such as many members moving into the same product at once.
  • Slow escalation: in one case, a trustee took no further action for 13 months after becoming aware of suspicious activity by an advice licensee's representative.

ASIC Commissioner Simone Constant said: "It's clear some trustees are not doing enough to protect their members, despite repeated warnings from ASIC and APRA about the dangers of poor oversight."

The background: Shield and First Guardian

Many members were advised, often after cold calls and lead generation, to move their super onto platforms and into the Shield Master Fund or First Guardian Master Fund. Both funds later collapsed. ASIC has made enforcement over the collapses a top priority. It has taken action against advice licensees, a research house and trustees. It is seeking compensation for members, declarations and civil penalties over alleged failures by Equity Trustees and Diversa. One platform operator, Netwealth, admitted contraventions and agreed to compensate affected members more than $100 million. Many matters are still before the courts, and allegations are unproven unless admitted or decided. See Managed investment schemes.

Our analysis: the control questions it raises

Report 833 is about platforms, but the control problems will be familiar to anyone who designs or tests controls in super. Each maps to an obligation covered elsewhere on this site.

RiskExamples of controlsLink to obligations
Harmful or excessive advice fees deducted from members' accountsFee caps and thresholds, independent checks of consent and fee authorisation, exception reporting on high or unusual fees, periodic samplingBest financial interests duty; control design
Members switched into high-risk or illiquid productsSurveillance for clusters of switches linked to the same adviser or product, and escalation triggers when inflows spikeDesign and distribution obligations
Poor-quality investment options on the menuDue diligence before listing, ongoing monitoring of liquidity, valuation and related parties, clear criteria for removalSPS 530 investment governance
Slow response to red flagsDefined escalation paths and timeframes, recording the date the trustee became aware, and breach assessmentReportable situations; incident management
Members already harmedScoping and remediation with beneficial assumptions, including lost earningsRG 277 remediation

Detective controls deserve more attention

Many of the failures described are failures to notice: unusual fees, patterns of switching, suspicious adviser behaviour. Preventive controls, like fee caps, matter. But detective controls that look for patterns across many accounts are often what catches misconduct that looks normal one transaction at a time. Testing whether those controls actually operate, and whether their alerts lead to action, is where control testing adds most value.

Awareness starts the clock

The 13-month case is a reminder that reporting timeframes generally run from when an entity becomes aware of, or is reckless about, relevant facts, not from when it finishes looking into them. Clear escalation rules and a recorded awareness date protect both members and the organisation.

This is general commentary on published regulator material, not advice. Enforcement matters referred to may still be before the courts.
