APRA's governance reset: what draft CPS 510 would change

APRA wants one governance standard for banks, insurers and super trustees. The draft would tighten rules on tenure, board make-up and conflicts, and is the biggest shake-up of prudential governance in more than a decade.

About 8 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

In brief

  • Released on 16 June 2026, draft CPS 510 would replace five standards (CPS 510 and SPS 510 Governance, CPS 520 and SPS 520 Fit and Proper, and SPS 521 Conflicts of Interest) with one cross-industry standard.
  • Headline proposals include a 12-year default tenure limit for non-executive directors, a minimum of five directors for super trustees, and conflicts rules extended to banks and insurers.
  • Consultation closed on 28 August 2026. The final standard and guide (CPG 510) are expected in late 2026, applying from early 2028.
  • This is a draft. Details can change, and some commentators have noted that super trustee independence settings remain largely set by the SIS Act.

Why APRA is doing this

APRA has said the governance standards date largely from the last major reform in 2012. It points to recurring weaknesses in the entities it supervises:

  • gaps in directors' skills and capabilities
  • narrow fit and proper assessments
  • too little attention to board performance reviews
  • problems linked to very long tenure
  • poor management of conflicts of interest.

Bringing the five standards together also removes differences between industries that APRA no longer sees as justified.

What the draft would change

AreaDraft CPS 510 proposal (summary)Who notices most
StructureOne cross-industry standard covering governance, fit and proper, and conflicts, supported by a single practice guide (CPG 510).Everyone: policies, board charters and compliance mapping will need updating.
Director tenureA 12-year default limit for non-executive directors, backed by renewal and succession planning. Boards could approve an extension of up to 12 months in limited circumstances and would have to tell APRA.Boards with long-serving directors, including some super trustee boards.
Board sizeThe minimum of five directors would extend to super trustees (RSE licensees).Smaller super trustee boards.
IndependenceFor banks and insurers, a revised definition of "independent director" aligned with the ASX Corporate Governance Principles. Independence would be assessed entity by entity, not presumed at subsidiary level from the parent board's assessment.Banking and insurance groups with subsidiary boards.
Conflicts of interestA conflicts management framework, previously required only of super trustees under SPS 521, would apply across all APRA-regulated industries.Banks and insurers (super trustees already have similar requirements).
Residency and ownershipThe Australian residency requirement would apply to all locally incorporated entities, including foreign-owned ones. For entities other than super trustees, the shareholding threshold linked to board representation would rise from 15% to 20%.Foreign-owned and closely held entities.
ProportionalityRequirements would apply to all, but with a higher bar for significant financial institutions in areas such as board performance assessments and committees.Large entities.

One earlier idea didn't make it into the draft. APRA had floated requiring at least two independent directors, including the chair, to sit on no other board in a bank's or insurer's group. It removed that proposal after earlier feedback.

Our analysis

Tenure limits are about renewal, not age

A default 12-year limit is a blunt tool. The underlying goal is for boards to refresh their skills and keep independent challenge. Boards that already run genuine skills matrices, succession plans and external performance reviews are likely to find the change less disruptive than boards that rely on long-standing members' knowledge. See Board structure and accountability.

For super, the conflicts change is familiar. The board rules are not.

Super trustees have worked under SPS 521 since 2013, so a conflicts framework is nothing new for them. In fact, the draft brings banks and insurers closer to super practice. The bigger changes for super are likely to be tenure, minimum board size and a single fit and proper regime. Commentators have noted that independence requirements for super trustee directors come largely from the SIS Act, and the draft doesn't change that. Whether that gap is appropriate is likely to stay debated. See Conflicts of interest.

It fits the wider accountability push, even as FAR paperwork is trimmed

The draft lands just as APRA and ASIC have proposed reducing the Financial Accountability Regime's administrative load. The message seems consistent: fewer forms, but higher expectations of how boards actually work. Governance substance, rather than documentation, looks set to be the focus of supervision. See Financial Accountability Regime.

Questions boards and governance teams are likely to be asking

  • Which directors would reach 12 years before or soon after early 2028, and does the succession plan cover them?
  • Does the board skills matrix show the capabilities the organisation will need, not just the ones it has?
  • How would fit and proper assessments and board performance reviews change under a single standard?
  • For banking and insurance groups: how would independence be assessed for each subsidiary board?

This is general commentary on a draft standard, not advice. The final standard may differ, so check APRA's website for the current position.
