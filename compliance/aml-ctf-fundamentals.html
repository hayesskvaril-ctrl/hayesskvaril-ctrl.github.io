AML/CTF fundamentals

Criminals need legitimate businesses to hide and move money. Anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing (AML/CTF) law makes those businesses the first line of defence.

Beginner About 14 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Money laundering disguises the criminal origin of money; terrorism financing moves money (which may be clean) to support terrorism.
  • Businesses that provide designated services are reporting entities under the AML/CTF Act 2006 (Cth), regulated by AUSTRAC.
  • Core obligations: assess ML/TF risk, have AML/CTF policies (a program), know your customer (customer due diligence), report to AUSTRAC, keep records and train staff.
  • Major reforms changed obligations for existing reporting entities from 31 March 2026, and brought in lawyers, accountants, real estate professionals and others ("tranche 2") from 1 July 2026.
  • Penalties can be huge: Westpac paid $1.3 billion in 2020, the largest civil penalty in Australian history at the time.

Terrorism financing is different: the money may come from legitimate sources (such as wages or donations), and the amounts are often small. The focus is on where money is going, not where it came from. The reforms also require entities to consider proliferation financing: funding weapons of mass destruction.

Who is regulated?

The Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006 (Cth) applies to businesses that provide designated services, listed in the Act. Providing one makes the business a reporting entity, which must enrol with AUSTRAC. Examples:

  • Financial services: banks and other lenders, super funds, some life insurers, stockbrokers, remittance providers, foreign exchange dealers.
  • Gambling: casinos, online and on-course bookmakers, clubs and pubs with gaming machines.
  • Bullion and digital currency: bullion dealers and digital currency (virtual asset) exchanges.
  • "Tranche 2" (from 1 July 2026): certain services provided by lawyers, conveyancers, accountants, real estate professionals, trust and company service providers, and dealers in precious metals and stones.

Enrolment for tranche 2 entities opened on 31 March 2026. A business must generally enrol within 28 days of starting to provide a designated service.

What reporting entities must do

ObligationIn brief
EnrolEnrol with AUSTRAC (and register, if a remitter or virtual asset service provider).
ML/TF risk assessmentIdentify and assess the money laundering, terrorism financing and proliferation financing risks the business may reasonably face, considering customers, services, delivery channels and countries dealt with. Keep it up to date.
AML/CTF policies (the program)Policies, procedures, systems and controls, proportionate to the risk assessment, to mitigate and manage those risks and meet the obligations below.
GovernanceOversight by the governing body; an AML/CTF compliance officer who coordinates day-to-day compliance and reports to the governing body at least every 12 months; independent evaluation of the program at least every three years.
PersonnelDue diligence on staff in relevant roles, and risk-awareness training.
Customer due diligenceIdentify and verify customers (and beneficial owners), understand the purpose of the relationship and the ML/TF risk, and apply enhanced due diligence to higher-risk customers such as politically exposed persons. Continue with ongoing due diligence and transaction monitoring.
ReportingSuspicious matter reports (3 business days; 24 hours for terrorism financing); threshold transaction reports for $10,000+ in physical currency (10 business days); international funds transfer instruction reports (10 business days); and annual compliance reports.
Record keepingKeep records of transactions, customer identification and the program, generally for seven years.

No tipping off

It is an offence to tell a customer, or anyone else not permitted, that a suspicious matter report has been made or is being considered. Front-line staff need to know how to handle a suspicious situation without alerting the customer.

The 2024–2026 reforms

The Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment Act 2024 (Cth) was the biggest change to the regime since it began. The AML/CTF Rules 2025 set out the detail. The reforms aimed to:

  1. Extend the regime to tranche 2 sectors (from 1 July 2026), bringing Australia closer to the international standards set by the Financial Action Task Force (FATF).
  2. Modernise the regulation of digital currency, virtual assets and payments technology, including "travel rule" information that must accompany value transfers.
  3. Simplify and clarify obligations, focusing on outcomes: the old Part A / Part B program structure was replaced with a clear ML/TF risk assessment plus AML/CTF policies, and customer due diligence rules were restated.

Existing reporting entities had to meet the reformed obligations from 31 March 2026. AUSTRAC has published transitional rules and regulatory expectations for how it will approach implementation, so check its website for the latest.

Red flags

Examples of warning signs that might prompt further enquiry or a suspicious matter report (context matters, and none is proof on its own):

  • Cash deposits deliberately kept just under $10,000 ("structuring").
  • Transactions that don't fit the customer's profile or stated purpose.
  • Rapid movement of funds in and out of an account with no clear reason.
  • Reluctance to provide identification or information, or use of third parties to transact.
  • Complex ownership structures with no obvious business reason.
  • Links to high-risk countries, or to people on sanctions lists.
  • In super: unusual early release claims, sudden changes to bank details followed by a withdrawal, or rollovers to newly created funds.

These cases show that the most common failures aren't about individual criminal customers, but about systems: reports not lodged, transaction monitoring not working, and risk assessments not reflecting how the business actually operates.

Check your understanding

Which stage, or what to do?

1. A person deposits $9,500 in cash at five different branches on the same day.

Getting cash into the system in amounts just under the $10,000 threshold is a classic placement and structuring pattern. It could warrant a suspicious matter report.

2. Funds move through eight accounts held by different shell companies in three countries within a week.

Rapid, complex movements to obscure the trail are layering.

3. A branch officer forms a suspicion about a customer. What should they NOT do?

Telling the customer could be tipping off, which is an offence.
