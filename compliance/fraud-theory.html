Fraud theory beyond the triangle

The fraud triangle explains some fraud but not all. What research adds about psychology, groups and organisations, and what it means for controls.

Advanced About 20 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • The fraud triangle (pressure, opportunity and rationalisation) is the most widely used model of why people commit fraud.
  • Research has extended it with capability, psychological pathways such as moral disengagement, and the role of groups.
  • Many frauds involve co-offenders, and the bonds between them shape how fraud happens.
  • Critics argue the triangle focuses on risky individuals and overlooks organisational and systemic causes.
  • Good fraud risk management addresses opportunity through controls, but also culture, incentives and the social ties that enable collusion.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the fraud triangle and its main extensions.
  2. Apply psychological research on moral disengagement and rationalisation to fraud risk.
  3. Analyse how co-offending and collusion change fraud risk and controls.
  4. Critically evaluate the fraud triangle as a basis for fraud risk management.

The idea in plain English

Why do trusted employees commit fraud? The best-known answer is the fraud triangle: people commit fraud when they face pressure (such as debt), have the opportunity (weak controls) and can rationalise it ("I'll pay it back"). It shapes auditing standards, fraud risk assessments and training. This page looks at what research has added and where the triangle falls short. It builds on fraud risk.

Theory and research

From triangle to meta-model

Dorminey et al. (2012) revisited the fraud triangle in the light of later research and anti-fraud practice and proposed a broader meta-model of fraud for teaching and research. Later models added factors such as the capability to commit and conceal fraud, which depends on position and skills. Free (2015) reviewed research built on the triangle and called for new directions, in particular attention to the social and organisational influences that a model focused on the individual leaves out.

Psychological pathways

Murphy and Dacin (2011) identified three psychological pathways to fraud. Some people commit fraud without recognising it as wrong (a lack of moral awareness, supported by moral disengagement). Others recognise it and rationalise it. A third pathway runs through negative emotions, such as anger or a sense of unfairness. The model suggests prevention should address awareness and fairness, not only opportunity.

Fraud as a group activity

Many frauds involve more than one person. Free and Murphy (2015) interviewed 37 people convicted of fraud committed in groups and found that the reasons for co-offending depended on the type of bond between offenders, for example loyalty to a leader or friendship. Collusion defeats controls such as segregation of duties that assume people act independently, which is why fraud risk assessments should consider relationships and hierarchy, not just individual access.

A critical history of the triangle

Morales et al. (2014) traced how the triangle became dominant and argued that it constructs fraud as a matter of risky individuals and vigilant organisations. The way fraud is framed shapes how it is controlled: the triangle directs attention to screening and monitoring employees, and less to how organisations themselves create pressure and opportunity. This connects to wider research showing misconduct spreads through peers and clusters in some firms (Dimmock et al., 2018; Egan et al., 2019).

Critical perspectives

  • Hard to measure rationalisation. Pressure and opportunity can be observed; rationalisation happens in people's heads and is hard to assess in advance.
  • Evidence from convicted offenders. Much fraud research relies on detected and convicted fraudsters, who may differ from those never caught.
  • Individual versus organisational fraud. The triangle fits employee theft better than fraud committed for the organisation, such as misleading investors or charging fees for no service, where pressure comes from targets and culture.
  • Controls can't remove all opportunity. Collusion and senior management override defeat many controls, so culture, incentives and speak-up channels are essential complements.

Seminar questions

  1. Apply the fraud triangle to a case of fees charged for no service. Does it explain the conduct well? What does it miss?
  2. Using Murphy and Dacin's three pathways, design training that would reduce fraud risk more effectively than a rules-based module.
  3. Free and Murphy show co-offending depends on bonds between offenders. How should this change the design of segregation of duties?
  4. Morales and colleagues argue the triangle focuses on risky individuals. What would a fraud risk assessment look like if it focused on the organisation instead?

    Revisits the fraud triangle in the light of later research and anti-fraud practice, and proposes a broader meta-model of fraud for teaching and research.
  • Free, C. (2015). Looking through the fraud triangle: A review and call for new directions. Meditari Accountancy Research, 23(2), 175–196. https://doi.org/10.1108/MEDAR-02-2015-0009 Peer-reviewed
    Reviews research built on the fraud triangle and argues for new directions, including group and organisational influences that the individual-focused triangle leaves out.
  • Murphy, P. R., & Dacin, M. T. (2011). Psychological pathways to fraud: Understanding and preventing fraud in organizations. Journal of Business Ethics, 101(4), 601–618. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-011-0741-0 Peer-reviewed
    Identifies three psychological pathways to fraud: lack of awareness (moral intuition and disengagement), rationalisation, and negative emotion, with implications for prevention.
  • Morales, J., Gendron, Y., & Guénin-Paracini, H. (2014). The construction of the risky individual and vigilant organization: A genealogy of the fraud triangle. Accounting, Organizations and Society, 39(3), 170–194. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aos.2014.01.006 Peer-reviewed
    A history of how the fraud triangle became dominant, arguing that it constructs fraud as a matter of risky individuals and vigilant organisations, which shapes how fraud is understood and controlled.
