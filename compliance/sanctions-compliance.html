Sanctions compliance

How Australian sanctions laws work, why a breach can be a crime even without intent, and how organisations screen customers and payments to stay compliant.

Intermediate About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Australia has two sanctions frameworks: UN sanctions, which implement UN Security Council decisions, and autonomous sanctions, which Australia imposes itself, including country regimes such as Russia and thematic regimes for human rights abuses, serious corruption and significant cyber incidents.
  • The Australian Sanctions Office (ASO), in the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, administers sanctions and keeps the Consolidated List of designated people and entities.
  • Breaching sanctions is a serious crime. For companies it is a strict liability offence: no intent is needed. The defence is proving the company took reasonable precautions and exercised due diligence.
  • Sanctions compliance relies on screening customers, owners, counterparties and payments against the Consolidated List (and often other countries' lists), and handling alerts carefully.
  • Sanctions sit alongside, but are separate from, AML/CTF obligations. A sanctions match may also require an AUSTRAC report.

What sanctions do

Sanctions restrict dealings with particular countries, people, entities or goods to put pressure on those responsible for threats to international peace and security, human rights abuses or other serious conduct. Common measures include:

  • Targeted financial sanctions: it is an offence to use or deal with the assets of a designated person or entity, or to make assets available to them, directly or indirectly. In practice this means freezing their assets.
  • Travel bans on designated individuals.
  • Trade restrictions: bans on supplying or importing certain goods (such as arms or dual-use goods) or services to or from sanctioned countries.
  • Investment and service restrictions in some country regimes.

The legal framework

FrameworkLegislationExamples
UN sanctionsCharter of the United Nations Act 1945 and regulationsRegimes against North Korea (DPRK), ISIL (Da'esh) and Al-Qaida, and others set by the UN Security Council.
Autonomous sanctions: country regimesAutonomous Sanctions Act 2011 and Autonomous Sanctions Regulations 2011Russia and parts of Ukraine, Iran, Myanmar, Syria and others.
Autonomous sanctions: thematic regimesSame Act, as amended in December 2021 ("Magnitsky-style" sanctions)Serious violations or abuses of human rights, serious corruption, and significant cyber incidents, regardless of country.

Penalties are severe. For individuals: up to 10 years' imprisonment and a fine of up to 2,500 penalty units or three times the value of the transaction, whichever is greater. For companies: a fine of up to 10,000 penalty units or three times the value of the transaction. The ASO can grant permits for otherwise prohibited activities in limited circumstances where it is in the national interest.

A sanctions compliance program

  • Risk assessment: which products, customers, countries and payment flows expose the organisation to sanctions risk. International payments, trade finance and customers with overseas connections are typically higher risk.
  • Screening: checking names of customers, beneficial owners, directors and counterparties at onboarding and whenever lists change, and screening payments in real time before they are processed.
  • Ownership and control: sanctions can apply to assets owned or controlled, directly or indirectly, by a designated person, so screening beneficial owners matters. Check the ASO's guidance on how ownership and control are assessed.
  • Other countries' lists: many Australian organisations also screen against US, UK and EU lists, because of their contractual obligations, correspondent banking relationships or overseas operations.
  • Governance: a policy, clear escalation paths, trained staff, records of decisions and regular testing of the screening system.

Handling a screening alert

Screening systems use "fuzzy matching" to catch spelling variations and transliterations, which generates many false positives. Tuning the system is a balance: too tight and true matches are missed; too loose and analysts are overwhelmed. Tuning decisions should be tested, documented and approved.

Sanctions and AML/CTF: how they differ

FeatureSanctionsAML/CTF
RegulatorAustralian Sanctions Office (DFAT); enforcement by the AFPAUSTRAC
Who it applies toEveryone: all people and businesses in Australia, and Australians overseasReporting entities providing designated services
ApproachProhibitions: no dealing, whatever the amountRisk-based program, customer due diligence and reporting
FaultStrict liability for companies, with a due diligence defenceCivil penalty and criminal regimes

In practice, banks and other reporting entities run sanctions screening alongside their AML/CTF customer due diligence, and one event may need to be considered under both.
