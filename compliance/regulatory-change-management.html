Regulatory change management

How organisations spot new and changed obligations early, work out what they mean, make the changes in time and prove the changes work.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 10 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Regulatory change management is the process for spotting new or changed obligations, assessing their impact, implementing the changes and confirming they work.
  • Start early. Most significant changes are visible long before they start, through consultations, exposure drafts and Acts with delayed commencement.
  • Impact assessment is a business task, not only a compliance task: the people who own the products, systems and processes must say what has to change.
  • A change is not finished until the obligations register, controls, documents, systems and training are updated, and there is evidence it works.
  • Changes that are missed, misread or implemented late are a common cause of breaches, so the process itself needs monitoring.

The idea in plain English

The rules that apply to a bank, insurer, super fund or licensee change all the time: new Acts, amended regulations, new prudential standards, updated regulatory guides, court decisions that change how a rule is read, and industry codes. A licensee must comply with the financial services laws as they stand (Corporations Act 2001, section 912A(1)(c)), and an APRA-regulated super trustee's risk management strategy must describe how it monitors and ensures ongoing compliance with all prudential requirements (SPS 220, paragraph 22(b)(iv)). Regulatory change management is how an organisation keeps up: a repeatable process that turns "the law has changed" into "we have changed, on time, and we can show it".

It is one of the core parts of a compliance program, and it feeds the obligations register that the rest of the program relies on.

Six stages

StageWhat happensTypical outputs
1. ScanTrack consultations, exposure drafts, Bills, new and amended laws and instruments, regulator guidance and letters, speeches, enforcement actions and court decisions. Sources include the Federal Register of Legislation, regulators' websites and newsletters, industry bodies and legal updates.An entry in the change log for each item.
2. TriageDecide whether it applies, to which entities, products and services, how significant it is and when it starts. Assign an accountable owner.A rating (see below), an owner and a due date.
3. Assess impactMap the change to the obligations register and work out what must change: processes, systems, data, documents and disclosures, contracts with service providers, controls, training and customer communications. Include a gap analysis against current practice.An impact assessment agreed by the business owners, with a list of required actions.
4. Plan and implementPlan the work back from the start date, with time to test. Update policies, procedures, systems, documents and contracts, and train the people affected.A plan with milestones, and status reports.
5. VerifyCheck the change works as intended before or soon after the start date, for example through control testing or a post-implementation review, and get the owner to attest.Test results, attestation and any issues raised.
6. Embed and reportUpdate the obligations register, controls library and compliance monitoring plan so the new requirement is monitored like any other. Report status, delays and lessons to senior management and the board.An updated register and monitoring plan, and closure of the change log item.

Rating a change

Not every change needs a project. A simple rating helps decide how much effort, governance and reporting each item needs. One common approach:

RatingTypical featuresTypical handling
HighA new regime or major change; affects core products, systems or many customers; significant penalties; tight timeframe.A formal project with a sponsor, regular reporting to the executive and board, and independent verification.
MediumChanges to existing processes or documents in one or two areas; manageable timeframe.An owner-led action plan, tracked in the change log, with compliance review before closing.
LowMinor or clarifying change; little or no change to practice.Assess, record the decision and update the obligations register.
WatchProposed only (a consultation or exposure draft).Track it, consider making a submission, and start an early impact view if it is likely to proceed.

Worked example: privacy policies and automated decisions

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 received Royal Assent on 10 December 2024. One part of it, adding Australian Privacy Principles 1.7 to 1.9, starts 24 months later, on 10 December 2026. From then, if an organisation has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do something substantially and directly related to making it, that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual's rights or interests, and personal information is used, its privacy policy must describe the kinds of personal information used and the kinds of decisions involved.

  1. Scan: the change was visible from late 2024, giving two years to prepare.
  2. Triage: rated medium to high for an organisation that uses automated rules or models in customer decisions, for example fraud checks that hold a payment or automated underwriting rules. Owner: the head of privacy, with data and technology owners.
  3. Assess: build an inventory of automated decision processes; for each, record the personal information used, whether the decision is made solely by the program or the program does something substantially and directly related to it, and whether it could significantly affect individuals.
  4. Implement: update the privacy policy, and add a step to the model and AI change process so that new automated decisions trigger a privacy policy review.
  5. Verify: before 10 December 2026, check the policy wording against APP 1.8 and test the inventory against the systems register.
  6. Embed: add APP 1.7 to the obligations register and include it in the compliance monitoring plan.

An illustrative application for learning, not advice about any organisation's obligations. See Privacy law and AI governance.

Common failures

  • Late identification: the change is spotted weeks before it starts, leaving no time to build and test.
  • Compliance does it alone: the impact assessment is written by compliance without the people who run the processes, so changes are missed.
  • No single owner: several teams each assume another is handling it.
  • Documents but not systems: the policy is updated but the system, form or letter template is not.
  • Service providers forgotten: an administrator or other provider performs the affected process, but nobody tells them or checks their change.
  • No evidence: the change was made but there is no record of testing, so it cannot be shown to a regulator or auditor.
  • Register not updated: the new obligation never reaches the obligations register, so it is never monitored.

A change that is missed or implemented late can lead to a breach of the new obligation itself, which may need to be assessed for reporting. Recurring problems with the process are worth treating as an issue in their own right, through issue and action management.

Who does what

RoleTypical responsibilities
Board or risk committeeOversees significant changes and the health of the process; asks whether the organisation is ready.
Business owners (first line)Assess impact on their processes, implement the changes, attest that they are complete.
Compliance and legal (second line)Run horizon scanning and the change log, interpret the requirements, challenge impact assessments, review completion, update the obligations register.
Internal audit (third line)Periodically reviews whether the regulatory change process works.

See the three lines model for how these roles fit together.

Questions a board can ask

  • Which significant regulatory changes start in the next 12 to 24 months, and how ready are we for each?
  • Who owns each change, and which are behind schedule?
  • How do we know changes we have made actually work?
  • Have any breaches in the past year come from changes we missed or implemented late?

Frequently asked questions

Should we act on proposals that are not yet law?

Usually you track them and start thinking about the impact, without building anything final, because details often change between consultation and the final law. For large changes with short lead times, early preparation can make sense. The regulatory tracker lists current proposals and start dates.

Is there a standard for this?

No law prescribes a particular method. ISO 37301 (compliance management systems) treats keeping up with changing compliance obligations as part of a compliance management system, and is a useful benchmark. See ISO 37301.
