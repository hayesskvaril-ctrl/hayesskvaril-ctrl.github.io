Remediation calculations in practice

The methodology decisions behind a remediation program, from the rate for foregone returns to beneficial assumptions and assurance, with a calculator and a worked super example.

Advanced About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Remediation calculations combine the population, the base loss, the time value of money, other losses and payment mechanics.
  • Use the returns people would actually have earned where you can. ASIC indicates the RBA cash rate plus 6% is acceptable where actual returns can't be worked out and it benefits consumers.
  • Decide deliberately how to treat negative returns, and document it.
  • Beneficial assumptions stop uncertainty falling on the people harmed, but shouldn't replace analysis.
  • Super trustees must decide who bears the cost, and every calculation program needs independent methodology sign-off, re-performance and reconciliation.

The idea in plain English

Remediation puts people back where they would have been if the error or misconduct hadn't happened. That sounds simple until you try to calculate it: records are incomplete, people have left, money would have been invested, and thousands of individual calculations need to be right. Most of the real decisions in a remediation program are methodology decisions, and each one moves money between the organisation and the people affected.

This page works through those decisions, with a calculator, a worked super example and the assurance a calculation needs. It builds on RG 277 Consumer remediation.

The building blocks of a remediation calculation

ComponentWhat it isTypical questions
PopulationEveryone who was, or may have been, affected.Which products, periods and account types? How do we treat people with missing data?
Base lossThe direct amount lost: the overcharge, the underpayment, the fee for no service.What should have happened for each person, and what did happen?
Time value of moneyForegone investment earnings or interest from when the loss occurred until it is repaid.Which rate? From when to when? How are negative returns treated?
Other lossesConsequential effects, for example lost insurance cover or tax effects.Are they foreseeable and measurable? Do they need an individual assessment?
Payment mechanicsHow and when money reaches people, including former customers.Credit to account, bank transfer or cheque? What if they can't be found?

Choosing the rate for the time value of money

RG 277 expects compensation for foregone returns or interest, using rates that return people as closely as possible to where they would have been. In practice:

  • Super and investment products: use the returns the member's money would actually have earned, usually the returns of their investment option over the period, applied through unit prices or crediting rates.
  • Where a rate is prescribed by law for the situation, that rate may be used.
  • Where you can't work out what the person would have earned: ASIC has indicated that the RBA cash rate plus 6% a year is an acceptable default where it benefits consumers, and other reasonably justified assumptions can also be used.

Negative returns raise a genuine policy question. If the member's option lost money over the period, strictly restoring their position could mean paying back less than the overcharge. Many organisations adopt a beneficial assumption that compensation will not fall below the amount taken. Whatever you decide, decide it deliberately, apply it consistently, and document why.

Worked example: a fee error in super

A fund charged an administration fee 0.05% a year above the PDS rate for 18 months. For a member with a $300,000 balance, that is $12.50 a month. The member's Balanced option returned about 7% a year, and remediation will be paid nine months after the error stopped.

  • Base loss: 18 × $12.50 = $225.00
  • Foregone earnings: each month's overcharge is grown at the option's return until the payment date.
  • Using the calculator's default settings, total compensation is about $248, of which about $23 is foregone earnings.

Across 12,000 members with different balances, join dates and exit dates, the calculation must be run member by member using actual data. Members who left the fund part-way through, switched options or died during the period each need specific treatment.

Methodology decisions and beneficial assumptions

SituationBeneficial approach (illustrative)
Records for the early part of the period are missingAssume the member was affected for the whole period, rather than excluding them.
Unclear which investment option the member heldUse the option with the higher return for the period (or the default option, if that is higher).
Unclear whether a fee was chargedAssume it was, where the evidence suggests it probably was.
Rounding and small amountsRound in the member's favour; pay current members regardless of amount.
Former members owed small amountsApply the RG 277 options for amounts of $5 or less, and document the approach.

Beneficial assumptions shouldn't become arbitrary generosity to avoid analysis. The aim is that uncertainty doesn't fall on the people who were harmed.

Who pays, and from where?

For super trustees, the source of remediation funds is itself a governance decision. Paying compensation out of the fund's general assets would make other members fund the correction. Trustees commonly use their own resources, or where appropriate the operational risk financial requirement (ORFR) reserve, which exists to address losses from operational risk events. Where a service provider caused the error, contractual recovery may follow, but it shouldn't delay paying members. Document the decision and its reasons.

Assurance over calculations

  1. Methodology sign-off before calculation starts, by someone independent of the team that caused the issue.
  2. Independent re-performance of a sample of individual calculations, including edge cases (leavers, deaths, switches, very small and very large amounts).
  3. Reconciliation of totals: population count, total base loss and total compensation against source systems and the general ledger.
  4. Payment verification: amounts credited or paid match the calculation file.
  5. Closure review: all in-scope people paid or accounted for under the residual approach; root cause fixed.

Check your understanding

1. What rate does ASIC indicate can be used where a licensee can't work out a consumer's actual foregone returns, if it benefits consumers?

RG 277 allows prescribed rates, reasonably justified assumptions, or the RBA cash rate plus 6% where beneficial.

2. For a super fund fee error, the most accurate time value measure is usually:

The member's money would have been invested in their option.

3. Why is paying remediation from the fund's general assets a concern for super trustees?

Trustees need to consider who bears the cost, often using trustee resources or the ORFR where appropriate.
