Remediation calculations in practice

The methodology decisions behind a remediation program, from the rate for foregone returns to beneficial assumptions and assurance, with a calculator and a worked super example.

Advanced About 24 minutes Last reviewed: 7 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Remediation calculations combine the population, the base loss, the time value of money, other losses and payment mechanics.
  • Use the returns people would actually have earned where you can. ASIC indicates the RBA cash rate plus 6% is acceptable where actual returns can't be worked out and it benefits consumers.
  • Decide deliberately how to treat negative returns, and document it.
  • Beneficial assumptions stop uncertainty falling on the people harmed, but shouldn't replace analysis.
  • Super trustees must decide who bears the cost, and every calculation program needs independent methodology sign-off, re-performance and reconciliation.

Put it into practice: Playbook: Run a consumer remediation

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Build a remediation calculation from loss, time value of money and beneficial assumptions.
  2. Choose and justify a rate for foregone returns or interest in a super remediation.
  3. Relate remediation to the theory of self-reporting and to research on repairing trust.
  4. Critically assess who bears remediation costs and whether remediation restores trust.

The idea in plain English

Remediation puts people back where they would have been if the error or misconduct hadn't happened. That sounds simple until you try to calculate it: records are incomplete, people have left, money would have been invested, and thousands of individual calculations need to be right. Most of the real decisions in a remediation program are methodology decisions, and each one moves money between the organisation and the people affected.

This page works through those decisions, with a calculator, a worked super example and the assurance a calculation needs. It builds on RG 277 Consumer remediation.

The building blocks of a remediation calculation

ComponentWhat it isTypical questions
PopulationEveryone who was, or may have been, affected.Which products, periods and account types? How do we treat people with missing data?
Base lossThe direct amount lost: the overcharge, the underpayment, the fee for no service.What should have happened for each person, and what did happen?
Time value of moneyForegone investment earnings or interest from when the loss occurred until it is repaid.Which rate? From when to when? How are negative returns treated?
Other lossesConsequential effects, for example lost insurance cover or tax effects.Are they foreseeable and measurable? Do they need an individual assessment?
Payment mechanicsHow and when money reaches people, including former customers.Credit to account, bank transfer or cheque? What if they can't be found?

Choosing the rate for the time value of money

RG 277 expects compensation for foregone returns or interest, using rates that return people as closely as possible to where they would have been. In practice:

  • Super and investment products: use the returns the member's money would actually have earned, usually the returns of their investment option over the period, applied through unit prices or crediting rates.
  • Where a rate is prescribed by law for the situation, that rate may be used.
  • Where you can't work out what the person would have earned: RG 277 gives the RBA cash rate plus 6% a year as an example of a rate that may be fair and reasonable if justified in the circumstances, and other reasonably justified, beneficial assumptions can also be used. The cash rate changes (it rose to 4.60% from 30 September 2026), so use the rate for each part of the remediation period.

Negative returns raise a genuine policy question. If the member's option lost money over the period, strictly restoring their position could mean paying back less than the overcharge. Many organisations adopt a beneficial assumption that compensation will not fall below the amount taken. Whatever you decide, decide it deliberately, apply it consistently, and document why.

Try it: compensation for a monthly overcharge

Illustrative. Compounds monthly at a constant rate. Real calculations use actual unit prices or rates for each period and each person's own transaction history. The cash rate defaults to 4.60%, the RBA's rate from 30 September 2026; enter the rate for your period.

Worked example: a fee error in super

A fund charged an administration fee 0.05% a year above the PDS rate for 18 months. For a member with a $300,000 balance, that is $12.50 a month. The member's Balanced option returned about 7% a year, and remediation will be paid nine months after the error stopped.

  • Base loss: 18 × $12.50 = $225.00
  • Foregone earnings: each month's overcharge is grown at the option's return until the payment date.
  • Using the calculator's default settings, total compensation is about $248, of which about $23 is foregone earnings.

Across 12,000 members with different balances, join dates and exit dates, the calculation must be run member by member using actual data. Members who left the fund part-way through, switched options or died during the period each need specific treatment.

Methodology decisions and beneficial assumptions

SituationBeneficial approach (illustrative)
Records for the early part of the period are missingAssume the member was affected for the whole period, rather than excluding them.
Unclear which investment option the member heldUse the option with the higher return for the period (or the default option, if that is higher).
Unclear whether a fee was chargedAssume it was, where the evidence suggests it probably was.
Rounding and small amountsRound in the member's favour; pay current members regardless of amount.
Former members owed small amountsApply the RG 277 options for amounts of $5 or less, and document the approach.

Beneficial assumptions shouldn't become arbitrary generosity to avoid analysis. The aim is that uncertainty doesn't fall on the people who were harmed.

Who pays, and from where?

For super trustees, the source of remediation funds is itself a governance decision. Paying compensation out of the fund's general assets would make other members fund the correction. Trustees commonly use their own resources, or where appropriate the operational risk financial requirement (ORFR) reserve, which exists to address losses from operational risk events. Where a service provider caused the error, contractual recovery may follow, but it shouldn't delay paying members. Document the decision and its reasons.

Special cases

Most of the effort in a real program goes into the people who don't fit the standard calculation. Typical approaches (illustrative; each program sets its own, with legal and tax input):

SituationIssueCommon approach
Member rolled over to another fundThe money can't simply be paid to them in cash if it is preserved super.Generally pay to their current super fund, because preservation rules still apply; check the treatment for each case.
Member has retired and been paid outThey may now be able to receive cash.Pay directly where the member has met a condition of release.
Member has diedWho is entitled: the estate or the death benefit beneficiaries?Follow the death benefit or estate process, with legal input.
Member switched investment optionsWhich return applies?Follow the actual option held at each point, or a beneficial assumption where records are unclear.
Insurance premiums wrongly charged or cover wrongly cancelledA refund may not be enough if the person couldn't claim.Refund premiums with earnings, and consider claims that would have been paid had cover been in place.
Tax effectsContributions tax, fee deductions and tax on earnings may change the true loss.Get tax advice on the method; don't leave people worse off because of tax treatment.
Very small amountsCost of paying exceeds the amount.Pay current members regardless; apply RG 277's options for former members owed $5 or less.

Measures for program oversight

Boards and executives tracking a remediation program commonly look at: people in scope and paid (number and dollars), people still to be found, time since the issue was identified, total compensation versus the original estimate, quality assurance error rates, and whether the root cause has been fixed. Long-running programs and growing estimates are warning signs. ASIC's reviews have repeatedly found remediation taking too long; see RG 277 for its findings, including licence conditions imposed in 2026 for slow remediation.

Common mistakes

  • Using one rate for the whole period when actual returns or rates changed.
  • Letting negative returns reduce compensation below the amount taken, without a deliberate, documented decision.
  • Calculating at the aggregate level instead of member by member.
  • Forgetting leavers, deaths and switches, which are usually where errors cluster.
  • Funding remediation from other members in a pooled fund.
  • No independent re-performance before payment, leading to a second remediation.

Assurance over calculations

  1. Methodology sign-off before calculation starts, by someone independent of the team that caused the issue.
  2. Independent re-performance of a sample of individual calculations, including edge cases (leavers, deaths, switches, very small and very large amounts).
  3. Reconciliation of totals: population count, total base loss and total compensation against source systems and the general ledger.
  4. Payment verification: amounts credited or paid match the calculation file.
  5. Closure review: all in-scope people paid or accounted for under the residual approach; root cause fixed.

Theory and research

Remediation in enforcement theory

In the economics of enforcement, remediation is one of the benefits of self-reporting. Innes (1999) showed that when firms that report their own violations can be required to fix the harm, the harm is fixed every time a violation is reported, whereas firms that hide violations only fix harm when they are caught. That gives regulators a reason to reward self-reporting that comes with full remediation, and it reflects the logic linking breach reporting to consumer remediation under ASIC's framework (Kaplow & Shavell, 1994). The calculation methods on this page are where that principle becomes real money for each customer.

Can remediation repair trust?

Paying people back is necessary but may not be enough to restore trust. Gillespie and Dietz (2009) argue that repairing trust after an organisational failure requires changes across the system: leadership, culture, strategy, structures and policies, external governance and reputation, not just a response to the immediate harm. Experimental research by Kim et al. (2004) found that apologies repaired trust more effectively after failures of competence than after failures of integrity, where an apology can confirm the wrongdoing. For remediation programs, this suggests that how the error is explained matters: an error in a calculation is a competence failure, while charging fees for no service can look like an integrity failure, which is harder to repair with an apology alone.

Critical perspectives

  • Precision versus speed. Exact calculations for every customer take time, while simplified methods deliver money sooner but may under- or over-pay. ASIC's guidance favours beneficial assumptions where precision isn't possible, but the right balance is contested.
  • Who pays in a mutual fund? In super, a trustee may have limited resources outside the fund, so remediation funded from reserves or the fund can mean some members pay for other members' losses. This remains a difficult policy question.
  • Unclaimed remediation. Payments to former customers who can't be found may never reach them. Whether unclaimed amounts go to charity, the regulator or the fund changes who ultimately benefits.
  • Limited evidence on outcomes. We found little published research on whether large remediation programs restored consumer trust in Australian financial services, beyond regulator reports on their scale and speed.

Seminar questions

  1. Using the calculator, compare the result with an interest-based rate and a fund return rate. Which is more appropriate for a super fee error, and why?
  2. Innes argues remediation strengthens the case for self-reporting. How should a regulator treat a firm that self-reported but remediated slowly?
  3. Using Kim and colleagues' distinction, is charging fees for no service a competence or an integrity failure? How should the remediation communication differ?
  4. In a profit-for-member super fund, who should bear the cost of remediation? Consider current members, affected members and the trustee.

Check your understanding

1. What rate does ASIC indicate can be used where a licensee can't work out a consumer's actual foregone returns, if it benefits consumers?

RG 277 allows prescribed rates, reasonably justified assumptions, or the RBA cash rate plus 6% where beneficial.

2. For a super fund fee error, the most accurate time value measure is usually:

The member's money would have been invested in their option.

3. Why is paying remediation from the fund's general assets a concern for super trustees?

Trustees need to consider who bears the cost, often using trustee resources or the ORFR where appropriate.
