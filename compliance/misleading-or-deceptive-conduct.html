Misleading or deceptive conduct: the legal tests

How courts decide whether a communication is misleading, the cases behind the tests, and how to apply them when reviewing marketing, disclosure and member communications.

Advanced About 21 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • The test is the overall impression on ordinary, reasonable members of the audience. Literal truth is not enough, and no intention to mislead is needed.
  • For financial products and services the key provisions are Corporations Act s 1041H and ASIC Act s 12DA (damages and other orders), and ASIC Act s 12DB and s 12DF (civil penalties).
  • Statements about the future are taken to be misleading without reasonable grounds, and silence can mislead where disclosure would reasonably be expected.
  • Fine print may not fix a misleading headline: the dominant message counts.
  • ASIC's greenwashing penalties against Mercer, Vanguard and Active Super show product claims must match what the product actually does, across every channel.

Put it into practice: Playbook: Review a disclosure document or campaign

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Apply the four-step analysis (audience, message, reaction, truth) to financial product communications.
  2. Explain the leading High Court cases, including the dominant message principle.
  3. Use research on consumer attention and disclosure to explain why fine print rarely corrects a misleading headline.
  4. Critically assess greenwashing, substantiation and the limits of disclosure as consumer protection.

The idea in plain English

A statement can be true word for word and still be misleading. The law doesn't ask "is every sentence accurate?" It asks: what overall impression would ordinary members of the audience take away, and is that impression wrong? That is why disclosure review is a judgement exercise, not a proofreading exercise.

This page sets out the legal tests the courts apply, the cases that shaped them, and how to use them when reviewing marketing, disclosure and member communications. It builds on Disclosure obligations.

Where the rules sit

For financial products and services, the prohibitions are in the Corporations Act 2001 and the ASIC Act 2001. The Australian Consumer Law's general rule (s 18) doesn't apply to financial services; the ASIC Act mirrors it instead.

Main provisions for financial products and services (summary)
ProvisionWhat it coversConsequence
Corporations Act s 1041HConduct in relation to a financial product or financial service that is misleading or deceptive, or likely to mislead or deceive.Not a civil penalty provision, but people who suffer loss can recover damages, and courts can grant injunctions and other orders.
ASIC Act s 12DAThe same general rule for conduct in trade or commerce in relation to financial services.As above: damages and other orders, but no pecuniary penalty.
ASIC Act s 12DBSpecific false or misleading representations, for example about the standard, quality, value or grade of financial services, or about sponsorship or approval.Civil penalty provision. ASIC's greenwashing penalties were largely under s 12DB and s 12DF.
ASIC Act s 12DFConduct liable to mislead the public about the nature, characteristics, suitability for purpose or quantity of financial services.Civil penalty provision.
ASIC Act s 12BB; Corporations Act s 769CRepresentations about future matters (forecasts, projections, promises) are taken to be misleading if the person had no reasonable grounds for making them.Shifts the practical burden: you need evidence of reasonable grounds.

Disclosure documents such as a PDS have their own regime: defective statements are dealt with mainly through the product disclosure provisions of the Corporations Act, and the general prohibitions are carved back for some of that conduct. The interaction is technical, so check the provisions and seek advice for any specific question.

A breach of s 1041H(1) or s 12DA(1) is also one of the deemed-significance tests for breach reporting, so a misleading statement by an AFS licensee is usually a reportable situation.

The four-step analysis

The cases that shaped the tests

Key High Court and Federal Court decisions (summaries in our own words)
CasePrincipleWhy it matters for reviewers
Parkdale Custom Built Furniture v Puxu (1982) 149 CLR 191Conduct is judged as a whole and in context, by reference to the class of people likely to be affected.Review the whole communication in the form the audience sees it, not sentence by sentence.
Campomar Sociedad v Nike International (2000) 202 CLR 45Where conduct is directed at the public or a section of it, ask how ordinary or reasonable members of that class would react, disregarding extreme or fanciful reactions.Define the audience precisely. A claim that's fine for financially literate readers can mislead a general retail audience.
Butcher v Lachlan Elder Realty (2004) 218 CLR 592Context, including clear disclaimers and the nature of the parties, can affect whether conduct is misleading.Qualifications can matter, but only if they are genuinely part of the impression a reasonable reader takes away.
Miller & Associates Insurance Broking v BMW Australia Finance (2010) 241 CLR 357Silence can be misleading where, in all the circumstances, there is a reasonable expectation that a fact would be disclosed.Ask what is missing: fees, risks, conditions, exclusions or the loss of insurance on switching.
Google Inc v ACCC (2013) 249 CLR 435A party that merely passes on another's representation, without adopting or endorsing it, doesn't itself make the representation.Relevant to platforms and intermediaries, but less so when an organisation adopts third-party content (for example ratings or awards) in its own marketing.
ACCC v TPG Internet (2013) 250 CLR 640The dominant message of an advertisement matters. Fine print that contradicts a prominent headline may not correct the impression it creates.If the headline needs a qualification to be true, put the qualification in the headline, or change the headline.
ASIC v Mercer Superannuation (2024)First greenwashing civil penalty: $11.3 million for statements that "Sustainable Plus" options excluded certain industries when they held investments in them.Product-level claims must match what the product actually holds. Screens and exclusions need monitoring, not just policy.
ASIC v Vanguard Investments Australia (2024)$12.9 million penalty for overstating the ESG screening applied to a bond fund.Claims about methodology ("screened against…") must be accurate for the whole portfolio, not just most of it.
ASIC v LGSS (Active Super) (2025)$10.5 million penalty for claims that investments were eliminated or restricted by ESG screens when the fund held them.Claims spread across impact reports, websites, a PDS and media interviews were all in scope. Every channel counts.

Case summaries are simplified for education. For precise holdings, read the judgments.

Worked example: reviewing a super fund ad

A fund proposes this social media ad, aimed at people aged 25 to 40: "Australia's top performing Balanced fund. 11.2% returns. Join in 2 minutes." Small print: "Past performance is not a reliable indicator of future performance. Based on returns for the year to 30 June."

StepAnalysis
1. AudienceYounger retail members with a range of financial literacy, scrolling on a phone.
2. MessageDominant message: this fund is the best, and returns of about 11% are what you'll get. Implied: joining quickly is safe and doesn't require comparing insurance or fees.
3. ReactionAn ordinary member could reasonably think the fund is consistently the top performer and that 11.2% is typical. Few would register the one-year basis in the small print.
4. Truth"Top performing" depends on which survey, category, period and fee basis. One strong year is not the long-term picture for a product held for decades. There may be misleading silence about switching (for example, losing insurance in another fund). The fine print doesn't neutralise the headline.
OutcomeHigh risk as drafted. A lower-risk version: state the source, period and category of any ranking in the main text; show returns over a period matching the option's objective; remove implied promises; and prompt people to check insurance and fees before switching.

Try the same situation as a decision exercise in the marketing campaign scenario.

Substantiation: what evidence each type of claim needs

Claim typeEvidence to hold before releaseCommon problems
Performance and rankingsSource, date, category, period, fee and tax basis; the full data set, not just the highlight.Cherry-picked periods; outdated rankings; comparing against a different category.
Awards and ratingsThe award's criteria, date and issuer; permission to use; whether it was paid for.Presenting a narrow award as general endorsement; expired awards.
Fees ("low fees", "no fees")Fee calculations on a stated balance; all fee types including indirect costs."No fees" when costs are charged another way; comparisons that ignore insurance or indirect costs.
Sustainability and ESGScreening methodology, holdings data showing the claim is true across the whole product, monitoring and breach process.Absolute terms ("no fossil fuels") with exceptions; policy says one thing, portfolio holds another.
Future outcomes and projectionsReasonable grounds: assumptions, models and their limitations, documented at the time.Projections presented as likely outcomes; stale assumptions.
Safety, security and guaranteesExactly what is and isn't protected, and by whom."Safe" or "secure" without qualification for products that can lose value.

Grey areas that need judgement

Puffery or a representation?
Vague, obviously subjective praise ("a great place for your super") is less likely to be treated as a factual claim than a specific, checkable one ("the best-performing fund"). The line moves with context and audience, so treat superlatives as factual claims unless you're confident otherwise.
Literal truth and half-truths
Each statement can be accurate while the combination misleads, for example quoting the one period where performance was strong. Test the impression, not the sentences.
Short-form channels
Character limits don't reduce the standard. If a claim can't be made fairly in the space available, it probably shouldn't be made there.
Third-party content
Sharing a rating, award, influencer post or media article in your own marketing can amount to adopting its claims.
Sophisticated audiences
Material for wholesale or professional investors is judged against that audience, but it often reaches retail readers too. Consider who will actually see it.

Designing a review process that works

  • Risk-based review tiers: heavier review for performance, fee, ESG and comparative claims, and for campaigns aimed at vulnerable or less experienced audiences.
  • A claims register: each recurring claim, its evidence, owner and expiry date, so outdated claims are withdrawn.
  • Review the final form: the actual layout on the actual device, not a text draft.
  • Close the loop: monitor complaints, social media comments and call centre feedback for signs people have been misled.
  • Escalation: a misleading statement by an AFS licensee is likely to be a reportable situation, so route issues into the breach process quickly.

Before approving a communication

Theory and research

Why fine print fails: evidence on attention

The dominant message principle in TPG is a legal rule, but it rests on a claim about how people read, and research supports it. Bakos et al. (2014) tracked 48,154 shoppers visiting 90 software companies' websites and found that only one or two in every 1,000 opened the licence agreement, and most who did read very little of it. If people barely read contracts they must accept, they are even less likely to read qualifications at the bottom of an advertisement.

Loewenstein et al. (2014) reviewed the psychology of mandatory disclosure more broadly. Limited attention, motivated reasoning (people notice what they want to believe) and biased probability judgements can weaken disclosure or even reverse its intended effect. Their review found that simplified, standardised and comparative disclosures tend to work better than longer or more detailed ones. For a review process, the lesson is to test what an ordinary reader would take away from the whole communication, which is what the courts ask.

Greenwashing

Delmas and Burbano (2011) explain greenwashing as the product of external drivers (regulatory and market pressures), organisational drivers (incentives, culture and poor communication between teams) and individual drivers (such as optimism and narrow framing). They argue that weak and uncertain regulation is a major enabler. The greenwashing penalties ASIC obtained against Mercer, Vanguard and Active Super show that uncertainty is being resolved through enforcement, and the organisational drivers explain why review processes need to reach product design and investment teams, not just marketing.

Critical perspectives

  • Disclosure is a weak consumer protection. Research on attention suggests that disclosure alone rarely changes decisions. This is one reason for design and distribution obligations and product intervention powers, which act on products, not just information.
  • The "ordinary reasonable member" is a legal construct. Courts don't usually rely on survey evidence of what consumers actually understood. There is debate about whether they should.
  • Substantiation standards are still developing. What evidence is enough for a sustainability or performance claim is being clarified case by case, which creates uncertainty for issuers.
  • Research is mostly overseas. The attention studies use US data. Australian super members may read differently, for example because super is compulsory and engagement is low.

Seminar questions

  1. Apply the four-step analysis to a real financial product advertisement. What is the dominant message, and would the fine print change it for an ordinary reader?
  2. Bakos and colleagues found almost nobody reads licence agreements. What does this imply for how courts should treat disclaimers?
  3. Loewenstein and colleagues argue disclosure can backfire. Give an example from superannuation where more information could make decisions worse.
  4. Using Delmas and Burbano's drivers, explain how a super fund could end up greenwashing without anyone intending to mislead. Which controls would address each driver?

Check your understanding

1. A statement in an ad is literally true. Can it still be misleading?

Courts assess the overall impression on ordinary members of the audience. Literal truth isn't enough, and intention isn't required.

2. According to the High Court in ACCC v TPG, fine print that qualifies a headline:

The dominant message matters. Qualifications must be clear and prominent enough to form part of the impression.

3. A forecast of future returns is taken to be misleading if:

ASIC Act s 12BB and Corporations Act s 769C deem future representations misleading without reasonable grounds.

4. When can silence be misleading?

Miller v BMW endorsed the reasonable expectation of disclosure approach.
