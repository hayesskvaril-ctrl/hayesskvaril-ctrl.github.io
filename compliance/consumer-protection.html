Consumer protection

Since the 2019 Royal Commission, Australia's consumer protection laws for financial services have shifted from "tell the customer" towards "design, sell and treat the customer fairly".

Intermediate About 18 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • For financial products and services, consumer protection mostly sits in the ASIC Act, enforced by ASIC. The Australian Consumer Law, enforced by the ACCC, covers most other goods and services.
  • Protections now cover the whole product lifecycle: design (DDO, product intervention), sale (hawking ban, add-on insurance rules), contract terms (unfair contract terms), service (claims handling, hardship) and complaints (IDR and AFCA).
  • Unfair contract terms have attracted penalties since 9 November 2023.
  • Complaints must be handled within maximum timeframes: generally 30 calendar days, and 45 days for most super complaints.
  • The Scams Prevention Framework, which became law in 2025, adds new obligations on banks, telcos and digital platforms as sectors are designated.

Which law applies?

FeatureFinancial products and servicesOther goods and services
Main lawASIC Act 2001, Part 2 Division 2 (plus the Corporations Act and National Credit Act)Australian Consumer Law (Schedule 2 to the Competition and Consumer Act 2010)
RegulatorASICACCC and state and territory fair trading agencies
Examples of protectionsMisleading or deceptive conduct, unconscionable conduct, unfair contract terms, implied warranties for financial servicesSimilar protections, plus consumer guarantees for goods and services

The two regimes are designed to mirror each other, so the core ideas (don't mislead, don't act unconscionably, don't use unfair standard terms) are the same whichever applies.

Protection across the product lifecycle

Design: DDO and product intervention

The design and distribution obligations (Part 7.8A of the Corporations Act, in force since 5 October 2021) require issuers of most financial products for retail clients to:

  • make a target market determination describing who the product is for, with distribution conditions and review triggers;
  • take reasonable steps so that distribution is consistent with the TMD;
  • monitor the product and review the TMD when triggers occur; and
  • notify ASIC of significant dealings inconsistent with the TMD within 10 business days.

Distributors must also take reasonable steps to sell consistently with the TMD and report complaints and other information to issuers. ASIC's guidance is in Regulatory Guide 274.

ASIC also has a product intervention power (Part 7.9A, since 2019) to make orders banning or restricting financial and credit products that are likely to cause significant consumer detriment.

Sale: hawking and add-on insurance

  • Hawking prohibition (since 5 October 2021): a person must not offer or request applications for financial products to retail clients during, or because of, unsolicited real-time contact such as a cold call. Consent to be contacted must be positive, voluntary and clear. ASIC's guidance is in RG 38.
  • Add-on insurance deferred sales model (since 5 October 2021): add-on insurance sold alongside a car or other major purchase generally can't be sold until a pause period after the main sale, to stop pressure selling.
  • Advice and lending duties: advisers must act in clients' best interests; credit licensees must not provide unsuitable credit (responsible lending under the National Credit Act).

Contract: unfair terms and insurance duties

A term in a standard form consumer or small business contract can be unfair if it would cause a significant imbalance in the parties' rights and obligations, isn't reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the business, and would cause detriment to the customer if relied on. Examples include terms allowing a business to change the contract or fees at will, or limiting the customer's rights in one-sided ways.

  • From 5 April 2021, the unfair contract terms regime in the ASIC Act has applied to standard form insurance contracts.
  • From 9 November 2023, proposing or relying on unfair terms became prohibited and can attract civil penalties, and the regime was expanded to more small businesses.

Insurance also has its own rules under the Insurance Contracts Act 1984 (Cth), including the duty of utmost good faith owed by both insurer and insured and, for consumer insurance since 5 October 2021, a duty to take reasonable care not to make a misrepresentation, which replaced the old duty of disclosure.

Service: fairness, claims and hardship

  • Licensees must provide services efficiently, honestly and fairly.
  • Since 1 January 2022, handling and settling insurance claims has been a financial service, so it must be done efficiently, honestly and fairly and is subject to licensing and breach reporting.
  • Industry codes (such as the Banking Code of Practice and General Insurance Code of Practice) and credit law set expectations for supporting customers in financial hardship and customers experiencing vulnerability, such as family violence, illness or language barriers.

Complaints: IDR and AFCA

Licensees serving retail clients must have an internal dispute resolution process that meets ASIC's standards in Regulatory Guide 271 (parts of which are enforceable), and be a member of AFCA.

Selected maximum IDR response times under RG 271. Some other categories have different timeframes.
Complaint typeMaximum time to give a written IDR response
Standard complaints30 calendar days
Superannuation complaints (other than death benefit distribution objections)45 calendar days

If a complaint isn't resolved in time or the customer is unhappy with the outcome, they can take it to AFCA, which is free for consumers and whose decisions are binding on the financial firm if the consumer accepts them. Complaints data is also an important source of risk information: it shows where products, processes or staff are letting customers down.

Scams

The Scams Prevention Framework Act 2025 (Cth) became law in February 2025. It sets overarching obligations for designated sectors to prevent, detect, disrupt, report and respond to scams, with sector-specific codes. Banks, telecommunications providers and certain digital platforms were designated as the first sectors in May 2026. Treasury released draft framework rules and sector codes on 28 May 2026. Businesses providing regulated services in these sectors had to be AFCA members from 1 September 2026, and AFCA handles complaints under the framework. Most obligations, including the sector codes, are due to apply from 31 March 2027, or later if the codes are registered after that date. The final codes had not been registered when this page was last reviewed, so check the ACCC and ASIC websites for the current position. The ACCC is the general regulator, with ASIC responsible for banking. Timing can change, so check the ACCC's website for the current position.

Check your understanding

Which protection is most relevant?

1. A call centre cold-calls people who once got a quote and offers them life insurance on the spot.

Offering a financial product in unsolicited, real-time contact is prohibited hawking, unless an exception applies.

2. A high-risk investment product designed for experienced investors is promoted heavily to retirees through social media.

Distribution appears inconsistent with the target market, which could also be a significant dealing to report to ASIC.

3. A standard insurance policy lets the insurer cancel cover for any reason at any time, but gives the customer no equivalent right.

A one-sided term in a standard form consumer contract may be unfair. Insurance contracts have been covered since April 2021.

4. A super member complains about a fee error and hears nothing for two months.

Most super complaints must receive an IDR response within 45 calendar days. The member can also go to AFCA.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Where in our customer journey are customers most likely to be harmed, and how do we know?
  2. How do complaints data and AFCA outcomes feed into product and process changes?
  3. Are we ready for the scams framework's sector code obligations?
