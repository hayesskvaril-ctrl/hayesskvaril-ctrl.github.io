Interpreting legislation for compliance professionals

How courts read legislation, how the layers of financial services rules fit together, and a practical method for working out what an obligation actually requires.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Acts sit above regulations and legislative instruments such as APRA prudential standards. Regulator guidance is generally not law, and courts have the final word.
  • Courts start with the text, read in context and in light of the Act's purpose. The interpretation that best achieves the purpose is preferred (Acts Interpretation Act s 15AA).
  • Explanatory memoranda and other extrinsic materials can confirm meaning or resolve ambiguity (s 15AB).
  • Always check the right version, definitions, modifications such as ASIC relief, and neighbouring provisions.
  • Words like "reasonable steps", "adequate", "likely" and "as soon as practicable" carry objective standards. Record your reasoning, and seek advice on high-stakes or ambiguous questions.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Place an obligation in the hierarchy of Acts, regulations, legislative instruments, standards and guidance.
  2. Apply the High Court's approach to statutory interpretation: text, context and purpose.
  3. Explain the theory of rules versus principles and how it shapes financial services regulation.
  4. Critically assess the uncertainty created by principles-based obligations and how organisations manage it.

The idea in plain English

Every obligations register, policy and breach assessment rests on someone's reading of the law. Most of the time the meaning is clear. But the hard questions in compliance usually turn on a few words: "as soon as practicable", "reasonable steps", "adequate", "likely", "material". Reading legislation well is a skill, and courts follow settled principles when they do it.

This page explains those principles, the hierarchy of instruments you'll meet in financial services, and a practical method for working out what an obligation means. It isn't a substitute for legal advice on a specific question, but it will help you frame the question well and recognise when you need that advice.

The hierarchy of instruments

APRA's prudential standards are legislative instruments made under powers in the industry Acts (for example the Banking Act and the SIS Act), so they are binding law for the entities they cover. Prudential practice guides are not. See key ASIC regulatory guides for how ASIC guidance works.

How courts read legislation

PrincipleIn briefSource
Text, context and purposeStart with the words, read in their context (the whole Act and the provision's place in it) and in light of the Act's purpose.SZTAL v Minister for Immigration (2017); Alcan (NT) Alumina v Commissioner of Territory Revenue (2009)
Purpose is preferredAn interpretation that best achieves the Act's purpose or object is preferred to one that doesn't.Acts Interpretation Act 1901 s 15AA
Extrinsic materialsExplanatory memoranda, second reading speeches and similar material can be used to confirm the ordinary meaning, or to work out the meaning where a provision is ambiguous or leads to a manifestly absurd result.Acts Interpretation Act 1901 s 15AB
Context in the widest senseContext, including the problem the law was meant to fix, is considered at the start, not only when there is ambiguity.CIC Insurance v Bankstown Football Club (1997)
Read provisions togetherProvisions should be read so they work together harmoniously, giving each a meaning consistent with the others.Project Blue Sky v Australian Broadcasting Authority (1998)

A practical method for reading an obligation

  1. Get the right version. Use the Federal Register of Legislation and check the version in force at the relevant date, including commencement and transitional provisions.
  2. Find the definitions. Check the definition section of the Act or instrument (for the Corporations Act, the dictionary in s 9 and any definitions in the relevant Part). Defined terms often mean something narrower or wider than everyday English.
  3. Identify who, what, when and the consequence. Who must act? What exactly must they do or not do? By when? Is it an offence, a civil penalty provision, or neither?
  4. Check for modifications. Regulations, ASIC relief instruments and exemptions can change how a provision applies. Breach reporting relief is a good example.
  5. Read the neighbours. Surrounding provisions, exceptions and notes often answer the question.
  6. Check the purpose and extrinsic materials. The explanatory memorandum usually explains what the provision was meant to do.
  7. Check guidance and cases. Regulator guidance shows the regulator's view; court decisions show the law.
  8. Record the interpretation. Write down the reasoning, especially where you take a position on an uncertain point, and when you sought advice.

Words that do a lot of work

WordsWhat to watch for
"must" and "may""Must" imposes a duty; "may" usually gives a discretion. But a discretion can carry duties about how it is exercised.
"as soon as practicable", "within X days"Where both appear, the fixed period is an outer limit, not a target. Practicability depends on the circumstances.
"reasonable steps", "adequate", "appropriate"Objective standards that depend on the nature, scale and complexity of the business. What was reasonable is judged by what a reasonable person in that position would have done, not by hindsight.
"reasonable grounds to believe" or "to suspect"Belief needs more than suspicion. Both are tested objectively on the facts known, which is why reporting clocks can start before an investigation finishes.
"likely"Often means a real and not remote chance, which can be well below 50%. Its meaning can vary by context.
"includes"Usually extends the ordinary meaning rather than limiting it to the listed items.

When to seek legal advice

Get advice when the consequences are serious (for example, a decision not to report a possible breach), when the text is genuinely ambiguous, when the position depends on how courts have read similar words, or when the organisation's interpretation differs from the regulator's published view. Record the question asked and the advice received.

Worked example: breaking an obligation into elements

Applying the method above to a well-known obligation. A simplified summary; read the provision and ASIC's RG 78:

ElementBreach reporting to ASIC (simplified)

TriggerThere are reasonable grounds to believe a reportable situation has arisen.
KnowledgeThe licensee first knows that there are such grounds, or is reckless about whether there are.
ActionLodge a report with ASIC in the approved form.
TimeWithin 30 days.

Frequently asked questions

Can I rely on a regulator's guidance to interpret the law?

Guidance shows how the regulator reads the law and intends to act, which is useful. But it isn't law, and courts can interpret the legislation differently.

What if two provisions seem to conflict?

Courts try to read them so both can operate. Specific provisions usually prevail over general ones, and later provisions can prevail over earlier ones. Hard cases need legal advice.

Theory and research

Rules, principles and certainty

Much of financial services law mixes precise rules (a 30-day deadline) with broad principles ("efficiently, honestly and fairly", "best financial interests"). Legal theory has a lot to say about the trade-off. The intuitive view is that rules give certainty and principles don't. Braithwaite (2002), an Australian regulatory theorist, argued the opposite can be true in complex areas: as rules multiply to cover every situation, they interact in unpredictable ways and invite creative compliance, so certainty falls. He proposed binding principles supported by non-binding rules and guidance, which is close to how APRA's standards and practice guides, and ASIC's regulatory guides, now work.

Principles-based regulation in practice

Black (2008) distinguished several forms of principles-based regulation and identified seven "paradoxes" it faces. Principles are meant to encourage firms to think about outcomes, but they depend on regulators and firms sharing an understanding of what the principles mean, and that understanding tends to be built through guidance, enforcement and dialogue, which can harden principles back into rules. Discussing the UK Financial Services Authority's move to principles, Black et al. (2007) set out what regulators and firms need for principles to work, including consistent guidance and credible, predictable enforcement. When a regulator or court later gives a principle a specific meaning, organisations that read it narrowly can find themselves exposed in hindsight.

Interpretation as a social process

The High Court's approach (text, read in context and in light of purpose) gives the method, but the meaning of broad obligations in practice is shaped over time by regulator guidance, enforcement outcomes and industry practice. This is why the practical method on this page asks readers to check guidance and recent cases, not only the words of the Act.

Critical perspectives

  • Who bears the uncertainty? Principles push interpretive risk onto firms. Large firms can afford legal advice; smaller licensees and trustees may find it harder to know what is required.
  • Guidance can become quasi-law. Regulatory guides are not binding, yet they strongly shape behaviour and enforcement. Some argue this blurs the line between law-making and regulation.
  • Hindsight in enforcement. When a principle is given specific content in a later case, conduct that seemed reasonable at the time can be judged harshly, the same hindsight problem discussed on the root cause analysis page.
  • The research is largely theoretical. Much of the rules-versus-principles literature is conceptual, and empirical evidence on which approach produces better compliance outcomes is limited.

Seminar questions

  1. Apply the text, context and purpose method to a phrase such as "as soon as practicable". What sources would you consult, and in what order?
  2. Braithwaite argues that in complex areas principles can provide more certainty than rules. Test this claim against the best financial interests duty.
  3. Which of Black's paradoxes of principles-based regulation do you see in Australian financial services? Give an example.
  4. If a regulator's guidance and a plain reading of the Act point in different directions, how should a compliance team decide what to do?

Further reading

  • Braithwaite, J. (2002). Rules and principles: A theory of legal certainty. Australian Journal of Legal Philosophy, 27, 47–82. https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/journals/AUJlLegPhil/2002/2.html Peer-reviewed
    Argues that in complex areas precise rules can deliver less certainty than principles, because rules multiply and invite gaming, and proposes combining binding principles with non-binding rules.
  • Black, J. (2008). Forms and paradoxes of principles-based regulation. Capital Markets Law Journal, 3(4), 425–457. https://doi.org/10.1093/cmlj/kmn026 Peer-reviewed
    Distinguishes forms of principles-based regulation and sets out seven paradoxes it faces, including interpretation, compliance, enforcement and trust: principles need shared understanding to work.
  • Black, J., Hopper, M., & Band, C. (2007). Making a success of principles-based regulation. Law and Financial Markets Review, 1(3), 191–206. https://doi.org/10.1080/17521440.2007.11427879 Peer-reviewed
    Discusses the UK Financial Services Authority's move to principles-based regulation and what regulators and firms need for it to work, including guidance, dialogue and credible enforcement.

Check your understanding

1. Under s 15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act, which interpretation is preferred?

Section 15AA prefers the interpretation that best achieves the purpose or object of the Act.

2. Are APRA prudential standards binding law?

Prudential standards are binding legislative instruments. Prudential practice guides are guidance.

3. A provision says 'as soon as practicable, and in any case within 30 days'. What does the 30 days mean?

The fixed period is a maximum.
