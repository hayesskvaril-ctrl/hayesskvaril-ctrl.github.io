Financial advice regulation

The rules that apply when someone gives financial product advice to retail clients: who can advise, what the advice must be, how it's documented and how advisers can be paid.

Intermediate About 14 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • The key distinction is between general advice (not based on a person's circumstances) and personal advice (where the adviser considered, or should have considered, the client's objectives, financial situation or needs). Personal advice carries much heavier obligations.
  • Advisers giving personal advice must act in the client's best interests, give appropriate advice, and put the client's interests first where there is a conflict.
  • Conflicted remuneration, such as commissions and volume-based payments that could influence advice, is banned, with limited exceptions such as some insurance commissions.
  • Ongoing fee arrangements must be renewed annually, with the client's consent needed for fees to be deducted.
  • Advisers must be registered with ASIC and meet professional standards: a qualification, an exam, continuing professional development and a code of ethics. Further reforms are in progress.

General advice and personal advice

FeatureGeneral advicePersonal advice
What it isA recommendation or opinion that doesn't take the person's circumstances into account.Advice where the provider considered, or a reasonable person might expect them to have considered, the client's objectives, financial situation or needs.
ExampleA fund's website article: "Consider consolidating your super to save on fees."An adviser recommends a specific client move their super to a particular fund, after reviewing their situation.
Key obligationsA general advice warning; no misleading statements.Best interests duty and related obligations, a Statement of Advice, conflict and remuneration rules.

The line can blur. A phone conversation in which a staff member asks about a member's age and balance and then suggests an option may become personal advice, whatever the intention. This is a common compliance risk in super funds and banks. "Intra-fund advice" and scaled advice (advice limited to a specific topic) are forms of personal advice with a narrower scope.

The personal advice process and its obligations

Core obligations

ObligationIn plain English
Best interests duty (s 961B)Act in the client's best interests when giving personal advice. The law lists steps (a "safe harbour") that, if followed, show the duty was met, such as identifying the client's objectives and making reasonable inquiries.
Appropriate advice (s 961G)Only give advice if it would be reasonable to conclude it is appropriate for the client, having complied with the best interests duty.
Priority rule (s 961J)Where there is a conflict, give priority to the client's interests over the adviser's, their licensee's or associates'.
Warnings where information is incomplete (s 961H)Warn the client if advice is based on incomplete or inaccurate information.
Statement of AdvicePersonal advice must generally be documented in an SOA explaining the advice, the basis for it, fees and conflicts. Records are required for some further advice.
Conflicted remuneration banBenefits that could reasonably be expected to influence advice (such as product commissions) are banned, with limited exceptions, including some life insurance commissions subject to caps.
Ongoing fee arrangementsArrangements for ongoing fees must be renewed each year, and the client's written consent is needed for fees to be deducted from their account.
Licensee obligationsThe AFS licensee must supervise and monitor its advisers, ensure they are competent, manage conflicts, and report breaches. See Licensing basics.

Who can give personal advice

  • Authorisation: advice must be given under an AFS licence, by the licensee or an authorised representative.
  • Registration: since 2023, individual advisers ("relevant providers") must be registered with ASIC before providing personal advice to retail clients. Their details appear on the public Financial Advisers Register.
  • Professional standards: advisers must meet a qualifications standard, pass the financial adviser exam, complete a professional year (for new entrants), undertake continuing professional development each year and comply with the Code of Ethics. Existing advisers had until 1 January 2026 to meet the qualifications standard, either through an approved qualification or the "experienced provider pathway" for those with at least 10 years' experience and a clean disciplinary record.

Why the rules look like this

Most of today's framework came from responses to advice failures. The Future of Financial Advice (FOFA) reforms introduced the best interests duty and the conflicted remuneration ban from 2013. The Financial Services Royal Commission (2019) led to annual renewal of ongoing fee arrangements, the end of grandfathered commissions and stronger professional standards. More recently, the collapses of the Shield and First Guardian master funds, into which many people were advised to switch their super, have put advice licensee oversight, lead generation and conflicts under intense scrutiny. See Managed investment schemes.

When advice firms fail without paying compensation, eligible consumers may be paid by the Compensation Scheme of Last Resort, funded by industry levies.

Current reforms

The Government's Delivering Better Financial Outcomes package responds to the Quality of Advice Review. The position is still moving:

  • Tranche 1 (legislated 2024): among other things, from 10 January 2025 fee disclosure statements were removed and client consents for ongoing fees were streamlined, and super trustees gained greater certainty about paying members' advice fees from their accounts.
  • Later tranches: after a pause while the Shield and First Guardian collapses were examined, the Government indicated in 2026 that it would proceed with further reforms, including replacing Statements of Advice with advice records and clarifying advice that super funds can provide and charge for collectively. Other proposals, such as modernising the best interests duty by removing the safe harbour steps and creating a new class of adviser, have been flagged, but their final form and timing are uncertain.

Check ASIC's DBFO page and Treasury for the latest position before relying on any of these details.
