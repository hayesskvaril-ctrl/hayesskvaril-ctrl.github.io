Does AML/CTF work? The research

What research says about whether anti-money laundering rules reduce crime, why effectiveness is so hard to measure, and how over-reporting can backfire.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • The global AML/CTF system is large and costly, but there is little rigorous evidence on how well it reduces crime.
  • There are no credible estimates of how much money is laundered, so "less laundering" can't easily be measured.
  • Economic theory shows heavy penalties for under-reporting can push banks to over-report, diluting the value of reports ("crying wolf").
  • Some cross-country evidence links elements of AML policy, such as the chance of conviction, with lower crime.
  • Critics argue compliance costs far exceed criminal funds recovered; these estimates are contested but highlight the need for outcome-based evaluation.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why the effectiveness of AML/CTF regimes is hard to evaluate.
  2. Apply the economics of reporting to suspicious matter reporting incentives.
  3. Evaluate the evidence on whether AML policy reduces crime.
  4. Critically assess cost-benefit critiques of AML/CTF and the risk-based approach.

The idea in plain English

Banks, super funds and other businesses spend large sums on anti-money laundering and counter-terrorism financing (AML/CTF) programs: checking customers, monitoring transactions and reporting suspicious matters to AUSTRAC. Australia has recently extended the regime to new sectors. Yet a simple question is surprisingly hard to answer: does it work? This page looks at what research says. It builds on AML/CTF fundamentals.

Theory and research

The evaluation problem

Levi et al. (2018) examined how the global AML system is evaluated. They found there are no credible estimates of the total amount laundered, globally or nationally, so a fall in laundering can't serve as an outcome measure. They also found little serious use of data in the Financial Action Task Force's (FATF) country evaluations and in national risk assessments. Without better data, they argue, judgements about which countries' systems are effective risk being impressionistic.

Crying wolf: the economics of reporting

Suspicious matter reports are the core product of the system. Takáts (2011) built the first formal economic model of money laundering enforcement. Banks decide what to report, and regulators fine them for failing to report. When fines are very high, banks protect themselves by reporting more, including transactions that are only weakly suspicious. The flood of low-value reports dilutes the information that matters, just as a boy who "cries wolf" too often is ignored. The model suggests corrective measures such as lower fines and fees for reporting, though these would be controversial in practice.

Does AML policy reduce crime?

Ferwerda (2009) built an economic model of crime and money laundering and tested it across countries. The results suggest that some elements of AML policy, including the probability of being caught for laundering and of being convicted for the underlying crime, are associated with less crime. Cross-country studies like this are useful but cannot fully separate the effect of AML rules from other differences between countries.

Costs and benefits

Pol (2020) argued, on the available evidence, that the global AML regime affects only a tiny share of criminal finances while compliance costs far exceed the criminal funds recovered. The figures behind this argument depend on estimates of criminal income and compliance cost that are themselves uncertain, and supporters of the regime point to benefits that are hard to count, such as deterrence and intelligence for investigations. But the critique has pushed debate towards evaluating outcomes rather than counting activity.

Critical perspectives

  • Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Weak measurement makes it hard to show the regime works, but also hard to show it doesn't. Deterrence in particular is almost impossible to observe.
  • The risk-based approach. Regimes now ask businesses to focus effort where risk is highest. In theory this improves effectiveness; in practice, fear of enforcement can still drive defensive over-compliance and "de-risking" of whole customer groups.
  • Enforcement shapes behaviour. Very large penalties in Australian cases have sharpened attention on AML/CTF compliance. Takáts's model suggests the design of penalties also shapes the quality, not just the quantity, of reporting.
  • Who bears the cost? Compliance costs fall on businesses and customers, including people who struggle to prove identity, raising questions of fairness and financial inclusion.

Seminar questions

  1. Design a set of outcome measures for an AML/CTF program at a super fund. Which could you actually measure?
  2. Using Takáts's "crying wolf" model, explain how an organisation's reporting thresholds could reduce the value of its suspicious matter reports. How would you guard against it?
  3. Pol argues compliance costs far exceed criminal funds recovered. Is this the right comparison? What benefits does it leave out?
  4. Levi, Reuter and Halliday say AML evaluations make little serious use of data. What data would you collect to evaluate Australia's regime?

Further reading

  • Levi, M., Reuter, P., & Halliday, T. (2018). Can the AML system be evaluated without better data? Crime, Law and Social Change, 69, 307–328. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10611-017-9757-4 Peer-reviewed
    Finds there are no credible estimates of how much money is laundered and little serious use of data in FATF evaluations and national risk assessments, so claims about AML effectiveness are hard to test.
  • Takáts, E. (2011). A theory of 'crying wolf': The economics of money laundering enforcement. Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization, 27(1), 32–78. https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=979035 Peer-reviewed
    A formal model showing that heavy fines for failing to report can push banks to over-report, diluting the information value of suspicious matter reports ('crying wolf').
  • Ferwerda, J. (2009). The economics of crime and money laundering: Does anti-money laundering policy reduce crime? Review of Law and Economics, 5(2), 903–929. https://doi.org/10.2202/1555-5879.1421 Peer-reviewed
    Builds an economic model in the Becker tradition and tests it across countries, finding that some AML policy elements, such as the chance of being caught and convicted, are associated with lower crime.
  • Pol, R. F. (2020). Anti-money laundering: The world's least effective policy experiment? Together, we can fix it. Policy Design and Practice, 3(1), 73–94. https://doi.org/10.1080/25741292.2020.1725366 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that on available evidence AML policy affects well under 1% of criminal finances while compliance costs far exceed the criminal funds recovered, and calls for outcome-focused evaluation. Its estimates are contested and rely on limited data.
