Designing a compliance program

A compliance program is the set of people, processes and systems an organisation uses to know its obligations, meet them, and prove it. This page walks through how to build one.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • A compliance program (or compliance framework, or compliance management system) has the same core parts everywhere: governance, obligations, risk assessment, controls, monitoring, breach management and reporting.
  • The obligations register is the backbone: every obligation mapped to an owner, the processes it affects and the controls that meet it.
  • AFS licensees must have adequate arrangements to comply with financial services laws (s 912A of the Corporations Act). APRA-regulated entities must have a designated compliance function with a reporting line independent of the business (CPS 220).
  • Keeping up with regulatory change is a core process, not an afterthought.
  • Scale matters: the program should fit the size, complexity and risk of the business. ISO 37301:2021 gives an international model.

Why have a compliance program?

Any organisation must comply with the law, but for regulated financial services businesses, having a working compliance program is itself a legal requirement:

  • AFS and credit licensees must, among other general obligations, comply with financial services laws, take reasonable steps to ensure their representatives do too, and maintain adequate resources and risk management systems (Corporations Act s 912A). ASIC's Regulatory Guide 104 describes the "compliance measures" it expects licensees to have.
  • APRA-regulated entities must have a designated compliance function that helps senior management manage compliance risk, staffed by competent people with sufficient authority and a reporting line independent from the business lines (CPS 220). Compliance risk is also one of the operational risks that CPS 230 requires entities to manage.
  • Everyone benefits: a credible program reduces breaches, helps show regulators that problems are isolated rather than systemic, and can be relevant when regulators and courts consider penalties.

The building blocks

Step by step

1. Governance, roles and policy

  • A compliance policy (or framework document), approved by the board or its committee, setting out the approach, roles and responsibilities.
  • Clear accountability: business owners are responsible for complying in their area (first line); the compliance function sets the framework, advises, monitors and challenges (second line); internal audit provides independent assurance (third line). See The Three Lines model.
  • A head of compliance with enough seniority, independence and access to the board or risk committee.
  • Resources: people with the right skills, and systems to support them.

2. Identify obligations: the obligations register

List every obligation that applies, broken down to a level where it can be owned and tested. Sources include legislation, regulations, prudential standards, ASIC instruments, licence conditions, industry codes and key contracts. Many organisations use a subscription obligations library as a starting point, but it still needs to be tailored.

3. Assess compliance risk

Not every obligation carries the same risk. Rate each one (or each group) on how likely a breach is and how harmful it would be, taking into account the controls in place. The ratings drive where you put monitoring effort and training. See Risk assessment methodologies.

4. Build compliance into the business

  • Controls embedded in processes and systems, so that complying is the default. Automated controls are usually more reliable than reminders.
  • Policies and procedures written for the people who use them.
  • Training that is targeted to roles (a claims officer needs different training from a board member) and tested for understanding.
  • Advice: an accessible compliance team that the business can ask early, for example when designing a new product or marketing campaign.

5. Manage regulatory change

The rules change constantly. A regulatory change process typically involves:

  1. Horizon scanning

    Tracking consultations, new laws, regulator guidance, enforcement actions and court decisions.

  2. Impact assessment

    Working out what changes, for which products and processes, and by when.

  3. Implementation

    Assigning owners, updating the obligations register, systems, documents and training.

  4. Confirmation

    Checking the change was made correctly and on time, often with a post-implementation review.

6. Monitor and test

A risk-based compliance monitoring plan sets out what will be reviewed, how and when, with the highest-risk obligations reviewed most often. Methods include first-line attestations, quality assurance, second-line compliance reviews, data analytics and thematic reviews across the business. See Control design and testing.

7. Manage breaches and incidents

An easy way for anyone to report a possible breach, a consistent assessment method, timely regulatory reporting, root cause analysis and remediation. See Incident and breach management and Breach and incident reporting obligations.

8. Report and get assurance

  • Regular reporting to senior management and the board (or risk committee) on the compliance profile, breaches, monitoring results, regulatory change and regulator engagement.
  • Management attestations or certifications, where appropriate.
  • Periodic independent review of the program, for example by internal audit or an external reviewer.

9. Improve

Use breaches, complaints, audit findings and regulator feedback to strengthen the program. Look for themes: the same root cause behind several breaches is a sign of a systemic problem.

The international standard ISO 37301:2021 Compliance management systems, which replaced ISO 19600:2014, sets out requirements for a compliance management system and, unlike its predecessor, can be certified against. Few Australian financial services firms seek certification, but many use it as a reference model.

Common pitfalls

Compliance "owns" compliance

If the business treats compliance as the compliance team's job, obligations fall through the gaps. Ownership has to sit with the people who run the processes.

A register that nobody uses

An obligations register built once for a project and never maintained quickly becomes wrong. It needs owners, regular attestation and a link to regulatory change.

Monitoring that only checks paperwork

Reviewing whether a form was signed tells you little about whether the customer got a fair outcome. Good monitoring looks at outcomes as well as process.

Reports that hide the message

Long reports full of green ticks can bury the one issue the board needs to know about. Lead with what matters and what decisions are needed.
