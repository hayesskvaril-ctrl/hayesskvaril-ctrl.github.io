Disclosure obligations

Financial products are complex and the stakes are high, so the law requires businesses to tell customers key facts at key moments, and never to mislead them.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Retail clients must receive specific documents at specific points: a Financial Services Guide, a Statement of Advice for personal advice, and a Product Disclosure Statement before acquiring most products.
  • Ongoing disclosure matters too: periodic statements, notices of significant changes and, for super, fees and costs disclosure, performance test notices and more.
  • Everything an organisation says, including advertising, websites and calls, must not be misleading or deceptive. ASIC's greenwashing cases show this applies to sustainability claims too.
  • Disclosure alone doesn't guarantee good outcomes. ASIC's own research says it is necessary but often not sufficient, which is one reason the design and distribution obligations exist.
  • A disciplined disclosure review process, with evidence for every claim, is a core compliance control.

Disclosure across the customer journey

The main documents

DocumentWhenWhat it covers
Financial Services Guide (FSG)Before providing a financial service to a retail clientWho the provider is, services offered, how they and associates are paid, conflicts, and how to complain
General advice warningWhen giving general (not personal) advice to a retail clientThat the advice doesn't take the client's circumstances into account
Statement of Advice (SOA)When giving personal advice to a retail client (a shorter Record of Advice can apply in some further-advice situations)The advice, the basis for it, fees, commissions and conflicts
Product Disclosure Statement (PDS)Generally before a retail client acquires a financial product (Part 7.9, Corporations Act)Features, benefits, risks, fees and costs, cooling-off, complaints. Shorter PDS rules apply to super and simple managed investment schemes
Target market determination (TMD)Must be made and published before a product is distributed (design and distribution obligations, since 5 October 2021)Who the product is for, distribution conditions and review triggers
Periodic statementsAt least annually for many products, including superBalances, transactions, fees and costs, returns
Significant event noticesWhen material changes or significant events occurWhat has changed and what it means for the client

Superannuation: extra disclosure

  • Fees and costs: PDSs and periodic statements must disclose fees and costs in a prescribed way. ASIC's Regulatory Guide 97 explains how; ASIC has said it will review RG 97 from 2026.
  • Performance test notices: under the Your Future, Your Super reforms, APRA tests MySuper and trustee-directed products each year. If a product fails, the trustee must write to members holding it within 28 days of being told. A product that fails two years in a row is closed to new members.
  • Dashboards and portfolio holdings: trustees publish product dashboards and portfolio holdings information.
  • Annual members' meetings: trustees of most APRA-regulated funds must hold an annual members' meeting (SIS Act s 29P), where members can ask questions of directors, executives and auditors.

Listed companies: continuous disclosure

Entities listed on the ASX must immediately tell the market about information that a reasonable person would expect to have a material effect on the price or value of their securities, unless an exception applies (ASX Listing Rule 3.1). Breaches can also contravene s 674 of the Corporations Act.

Misleading and deceptive conduct

Over all of this sits a broad rule: you must not engage in conduct, in relation to financial products or services, that is misleading or deceptive or likely to mislead or deceive (Corporations Act s 1041H; ASIC Act s 12DA), and must not make false or misleading representations (ASIC Act s 12DB). This applies to everything: ads, websites, social media, brochures, call scripts, app screens and statements.

  • The overall impression counts. Fine print rarely fixes a misleading headline.
  • Silence can mislead where there's a reasonable expectation that something would be disclosed.
  • Claims need evidence. Statements about returns, fees, "best" or "lowest", or sustainability must be accurate and substantiated.

Greenwashing: recent enforcement

ASIC's first greenwashing civil penalty case ended in 2024 with the Federal Court ordering Mercer Superannuation to pay $11.3 million for misleading statements about "Sustainable Plus" investment options. Vanguard Investments Australia was later ordered to pay $12.9 million over ESG screening claims, and Active Super was also penalised $10.5 million. The message: sustainability claims are held to the same standard as any other claim.

ASIC's Regulatory Guide 234 gives good-practice guidance on advertising financial products and services (including credit). ASIC consulted on updates to RG 234 in 2026.

Why disclosure isn't enough

In 2019, ASIC and the Dutch Authority for the Financial Markets published Disclosure: Why it shouldn't be the default (ASIC Report 632). Drawing on behavioural research, it found that disclosure and warnings are often less effective than expected, and can even backfire. People don't read long documents, are overloaded with information, and are influenced by how choices are framed. Simplifying disclosure doesn't remove complexity in the product itself.

This thinking underpins newer obligations that focus on outcomes rather than documents, such as the design and distribution obligations and the product intervention power. See Consumer protection.

How a disclosure review works

Most financial services organisations have a structured review and approval process for customer-facing material. A typical process:

  1. Brief and draft

    The business owner drafts the document or campaign, with the target audience and purpose in mind.

  2. Substantiation

    Every factual claim (numbers, rankings, "no fees", sustainability) is linked to evidence in a substantiation file.

  3. Specialist review

    Product, legal and compliance check content requirements, accuracy, balance and overall impression; tax, actuarial or investment teams where relevant.

  4. Approval

    Sign-off by authorised approvers, with version control and an audit trail.

  5. Publication and monitoring

    Check what actually went out; track expiry dates and triggers for review (rate changes, fee changes, law changes).
