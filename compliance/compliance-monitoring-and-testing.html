Designing a compliance monitoring and testing program

How to plan compliance monitoring and testing by risk, choose techniques that produce real evidence, and turn findings into fixes, with a risk-based planner.

Advanced About 17 minutes Last reviewed: 28 September 2026

Key takeaways

  • Monitoring (continuous, often automated), testing (point-in-time, evidence-based), thematic reviews and independent assurance work together.
  • Prioritise coverage by inherent risk, control strength, time since last tested, and change or warning signals.
  • Full-population data analytics suits high-volume, rules-based obligations and often finds systematic errors that samples miss.
  • Judgement-heavy obligations need file reviews, call listening or thematic reviews.
  • Findings should be rated consistently, assessed for breaches, traced to root causes and closed only when fixes are validated.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Distinguish compliance monitoring, testing and assurance, and prioritise coverage by risk.
  2. Choose testing techniques suited to different obligations and evidence.
  3. Use research on compliance systems to explain why formal programs don't always change behaviour.
  4. Critically evaluate the risk of 'decoupled' or cosmetic compliance and how monitoring can detect it.

The idea in plain English

A compliance framework on paper says how obligations should be met. Monitoring and testing find out whether they actually are. A well-designed program spends its limited effort where the risk of non-compliance and harm is highest, tests things in ways that produce real evidence, and turns findings into fixes.

This page covers how to design that program: the difference between monitoring and testing, how to prioritise coverage, which techniques suit which obligations, and how to report results. It builds on designing a compliance program and control testing and sampling.

Monitoring, testing and assurance

ActivityWhat it isExample
MonitoringOngoing tracking of indicators and exceptions, often automated, to spot problems as they emerge.A daily report of rollovers approaching the legal deadline; complaint ageing dashboards.
TestingA point-in-time examination of whether a control or obligation was met, using evidence.Sampling 40 benefit payments to check identity verification was completed before payment.
Thematic reviewA deeper look at one theme across the business, often prompted by regulator focus or incident trends.A review of how hardship and vulnerable member cases are handled across teams.
AssuranceAn opinion, usually by an independent function, on whether the framework is working overall.An internal audit review of the compliance framework.

Good programs use all four. Monitoring is continuous but shallow; testing is deep but periodic. The second line typically designs and runs much of the program, with the first line doing its own monitoring and internal audit providing independent assurance over both. See assurance mapping.

Prioritising coverage by risk

You can't test everything every year. Common factors for deciding what to test and how often:

  • Inherent compliance risk of the obligation: consequences for customers and members, penalties, regulator focus.
  • Control strength: manual or automated, preventive or detective, and results of previous testing.
  • Time since last tested: assurance decays as processes, people and systems change.
  • Change and signals: new systems, products or providers; incidents, breaches, complaints or audit findings.

Choosing the right technique

TechniqueBest forLimitations
Full-population data analyticsHigh-volume, rules-based obligations: fee calculations, premium deductions, payment timeframes, complaint deadlines.Needs reliable data and clear rules; can miss judgement-based failures.
Sample testingControls that leave evidence item by item: approvals, checks, documentation.Gives confidence, not certainty; sample size matters (see sampling).
File reviewsJudgement-heavy work: advice, claims decisions, hardship, complaints.Resource-intensive; reviewer consistency needs calibration.
Call listening and mystery shoppingConduct and disclosure in conversations, sales and service.Small samples; privacy and consent considerations.
Walkthroughs and design reviewsNew or changed processes, before testing operation.Show a control exists, not that it operates.
Trend analysisComplaints, incidents and breaches: finding systemic issues.Depends on consistent categorisation.

Where data allows, testing the whole population is increasingly expected. It removes sampling risk and often finds errors that samples miss, especially small systematic ones such as a fee rate error affecting every member slightly.

From findings to outcomes

  1. Rate consistently: use a defined rating scale for results and for the severity of findings, so trends mean something.
  2. Assess breaches: a failed test may reveal a breach that needs significance assessment and reporting.
  3. Find root causes: not just "control not performed", but why. See root cause analysis.
  4. Track actions to validated closure: see issue and action management.
  5. Report themes: committees and boards need the pattern (which obligations are weak, where, and why), not a list of tests.
  6. Feed back: results change risk ratings, the obligations register and next year's plan.

Common failure modes

Coverage planned by calendar rather than risk; testing what's easy rather than what matters; tests that confirm a control exists but not that it works; findings closed without validation; and results that never reach the people who could fix the root cause.

Theory and research

Do compliance systems work?

The most relevant Australian study is by Parker and Nielsen (2009), who surveyed 999 large Australian businesses about their competition and consumer law compliance systems. Some elements of formal systems were associated with better management of compliance in practice, but management commitment to compliance values, managerial oversight and planning, and resources were just as important. A compliance program on paper is not the same as compliance managed in practice.

Other research is more sceptical. McKendall et al. (2002) studied 108 large US corporations and found that ethics codes, training, communication and related HR practices were not associated with fewer workplace safety violations. Weaver et al. (1999) explain why this can happen: external pressure from regulators and stakeholders tends to produce programs that are decoupled from everyday operations, adopted for legitimacy rather than to change behaviour, while programs integrated into how the business runs depend on genuine top management commitment.

What this means for monitoring and testing

If formal programs can be decoupled from practice, monitoring should test outcomes and behaviour, not just the existence of policies and training records. The risk-based planning approach on this page, which weights testing towards change, complexity and past problems, reflects what research on control weaknesses suggests (Doyle et al., 2007). Monitoring results are also a key input to a regulator's view of whether a firm polices itself effectively, one reason self-reported breaches and credible testing go together (Toffel & Short, 2011).

Critical perspectives

  • Measuring compliance is hard. Studies use proxies such as recorded violations, which depend on detection. A program that improves detection can look like it increases non-compliance.
  • Context matters. Parker and Nielsen studied competition and consumer law, and McKendall and colleagues studied workplace safety. Financial services compliance, with prudential and conduct obligations, may behave differently.
  • Testing can become ritual. Checking that controls and policies exist is easier than testing whether customers were treated as the law requires. The difference is central to the idea of decoupling.
  • Independence versus insight. A second-line function independent enough to challenge may lack the operational knowledge to test well, and one close enough to understand the business may lose its independence.

Check your understanding

1. What is the main difference between monitoring and testing?

Both matter: monitoring spots emerging issues, testing gives evidence of compliance.

2. Which technique best suits checking that every member's fee was calculated at the PDS rate?

High-volume, rules-based obligations suit full-population testing where data is reliable.

3. A compliance test fails. What should happen before closing the finding?

Findings should be assessed, fixed at the root, and validated.
