Modern slavery reporting

Large organisations must report each year on the risk of modern slavery in their operations and supply chains, and stronger obligations are on the way.

Intermediate About 15 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Modern slavery covers serious exploitation such as forced labour, debt bondage, human trafficking, deceptive recruitment and the worst forms of child labour.
  • Under the Modern Slavery Act 2018 (Cth), entities with annual consolidated revenue of more than $100 million must publish an annual modern slavery statement addressing seven mandatory criteria, approved by their board.
  • Statements are published on the government's public Modern Slavery Statements Register.
  • The Act currently requires reporting, not particular actions, and has no penalties for non-compliance. That is set to change: in July 2026 the Government announced a proposed criminal offence for large companies that fail to prevent modern slavery in their supply chains, and civil penalties for reporting failures.
  • For financial institutions, modern slavery risk sits mostly in supply chains (such as cleaning, security, technology hardware and outsourced services) and in investments and lending.

What modern slavery is

The Act uses a broad definition drawn from Australian criminal law and international conventions. It includes trafficking in persons, slavery, servitude, forced labour, forced marriage, debt bondage, deceptive recruitment for labour or services, and the worst forms of child labour. It does not cover poor working conditions or underpayment on their own, though these can be warning signs.

Who must report

  • Entities based or operating in Australia with annual consolidated revenue of more than $100 million, and the Commonwealth. Other entities can report voluntarily.
  • A statement is due within six months after the end of the entity's reporting period.
  • It must be approved by the principal governing body (usually the board) and signed by a responsible member, such as a director.
  • Joint statements can cover several entities in a group.

The seven mandatory criteria

#The statement must…What good looks like
1Identify the reporting entityClear legal names and ABNs.
2Describe its structure, operations and supply chainsSpecific: main categories of goods and services bought, and the countries they come from.
3Describe the modern slavery risks in its operations and supply chains, and those of entities it owns or controlsRisks identified by sector, product and geography, not generic statements.
4Describe actions taken to assess and address those risks, including due diligence and remediationConcrete actions: supplier questionnaires, audits, contract clauses, grievance mechanisms, and what happened when issues were found.
5Describe how it assesses the effectiveness of those actionsMeasures and indicators, and how they're reviewed over time.
6Describe consultation with entities it owns or controlsHow group entities were involved in preparing the statement.
7Include any other relevant informationFor example, the impact of events such as COVID-19 or progress against past commitments.

Criteria summarised from s 16 of the Act.

Managing modern slavery risk

  • Prioritise by risk, not spend. A small cleaning contract can carry more risk than a large software licence. Higher-risk categories often include cleaning, security, facilities management, construction, electronics and textiles (such as uniforms), and goods from regions with known forced labour concerns.
  • Look beyond direct suppliers. The highest risks are often several tiers down, for example in the raw materials of electronics.
  • Use contracts: modern slavery clauses, audit rights, and requirements to pass obligations down the chain.
  • Remediate, don't just exit. Walking away from a supplier can leave affected workers worse off. Good practice focuses on outcomes for the people harmed.
  • Financial institutions: consider modern slavery risk in lending and investment decisions, stewardship and engagement with investee companies, not only in procurement.

These activities fit naturally within third-party risk management.

Reform: from reporting to prevention

A statutory review of the Act, led by Professor John McMillan, reported in May 2023 with 30 recommendations. The Government's December 2024 response agreed in full, in part or in principle with 25 of them, while keeping the $100 million reporting threshold. An independent Australian Anti-Slavery Commissioner was appointed in 2024.

Proposed, not yet law (as at September 2026)

On 16 July 2026 the Attorney-General announced proposed reforms with three pillars:

  • A new criminal offence for companies with annual consolidated revenue over $100 million that fail to prevent modern slavery in their supply chains, with a defence for companies that took reasonable steps to prevent it.
  • Civil penalties and enforcement powers for failing to meet existing reporting obligations.
  • Guidance and education.

Consultation closed on 25 September 2026, and the Government has proposed a 12 to 18 month implementation period after legislation commences. The detail may change, so check the Attorney-General's Department for the current position.

The proposed offence follows the model of the 2024 foreign bribery reforms. See Anti-bribery and corruption for how "failure to prevent" offences and their defences work.

Worked example: assessing one supplier

A super fund uses a contract cleaning company for its offices. Cleaning is a sector with known modern slavery risks.

  1. Identify the risk: low-paid, often migrant workers; subcontracting; cash payments.
  2. Ask questions: how are workers recruited and paid; are there subcontractors; can workers raise complaints safely?
  3. Contract: include terms on pay, subcontracting, audit rights and remediation.
  4. Check: spot audits or worker interviews, rather than relying only on a questionnaire.
  5. Respond: if problems are found, work with the supplier to fix them and make sure workers are paid what they are owed. Ending the contract straight away can leave workers worse off.

Illustrative only.

Common mistakes

  • Statements that describe policies but not what was actually done or found.
  • Ignoring investment portfolios, which for funds are often the largest exposure.
  • Relying on supplier questionnaires that suppliers fill in themselves.
  • No way to measure effectiveness, one of the seven mandatory criteria.

Questions a board can ask

These are prompts for discussion, not requirements.

  1. Where in our supply chain and investments is the risk of modern slavery highest?
  2. What did we find last year, and what did we do about it?
  3. Would we be able to show reasonable steps if the proposed failure to prevent offence becomes law?

Frequently asked questions

Do entities below $100 million need to report?

Not under the Act, although they can report voluntarily. They may still be asked about modern slavery by larger customers.

Is the proposed offence law yet?

No. It was proposed in July 2026 and consulted on. See the reform section above.
