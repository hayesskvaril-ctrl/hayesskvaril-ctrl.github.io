Anti-bribery and corruption

The laws against bribery in Australia and overseas, the 2024 "failure to prevent" offence for companies, and what an anti-bribery program looks like in practice.

Intermediate About 12 minutes Last reviewed: 27 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Bribery means offering or giving a benefit to improperly influence someone in their role. It is a crime under Commonwealth law (for foreign and Commonwealth public officials) and under state and territory laws, which also cover private-sector "secret commissions".
  • Since 8 September 2024, companies can commit a criminal offence of failing to prevent foreign bribery by their associates, including employees, agents and contractors. The only defence is having adequate procedures in place.
  • The Attorney-General's Department has published guidance on what adequate procedures look like. It is principles-based, not a checklist.
  • Most bribery risk sits with third parties such as agents, intermediaries and consultants, so due diligence and monitoring matter as much as internal policies.
  • The National Anti-Corruption Commission (NACC), operating since 1 July 2023, investigates serious or systemic corruption involving Commonwealth public officials, including contractors.

The legal framework

LawWhat it covers
Foreign bribery (Criminal Code, Division 70)Offering or giving a benefit to a foreign public official to obtain or keep business or a business advantage. Applies to Australian citizens, residents and companies acting overseas. Broadened by the 2024 reforms.
Failure to prevent foreign bribery (s 70.5A)A company commits an offence if an associate bribes a foreign public official for the company's benefit, unless the company proves it had adequate procedures to prevent it. No need to prove anyone at the company knew.
Bribery of Commonwealth public officials (Criminal Code, Division 141)Bribes and corrupting benefits given to Commonwealth officials.
State and territory lawsBribery of state officials and "secret commissions": corrupt payments to agents or employees in the private sector, such as a kickback to a procurement manager.
Accounting records offencesFalse or concealed records used to hide bribes can be separate offences.
Financial services obligationsFor licensees, bribery can breach the obligation to act efficiently, honestly and fairly and create reportable situations. It also engages AML/CTF obligations, as bribes are proceeds of crime.

Penalties for companies under the foreign bribery offences can be very large: the greatest of 100,000 penalty units, three times the benefit obtained, or, if the benefit can't be worked out, 10% of annual turnover. Individuals face imprisonment of up to 10 years.

Facilitation payments, small payments to speed up routine government actions, remain a narrow defence under Australian foreign bribery law, with strict record-keeping conditions. They are illegal in many other countries, and many organisations ban them entirely.

What an adequate anti-bribery program includes

Gifts and hospitality

Most bribery risk in financial services looks ordinary: tickets to sport, dinners, travel, gifts from suppliers or fund managers. The question is always whether a benefit could influence, or look like it influences, a decision. A typical policy uses thresholds, a register and pre-approval:

Illustrative examples. Thresholds and rules vary between organisations.
ScenarioTypical treatmentWhy
A $40 bottle of wine from a supplier at ChristmasAccept and record in the registerLow value, customary, no decision pending.
A fund manager offers two grand final tickets to a staff member on the investment committeeDecline, or escalate for approvalHigh value, and the recipient influences decisions affecting the giver.
A tenderer offers dinner to the procurement team during a live tenderDeclineTiming makes improper influence likely, or looks likely.
A payment to a foreign official to "speed up" a licenceRefuse and escalatePotential bribery; many organisations ban facilitation payments.
A donation to a charity nominated by a government officialPre-approval and due diligenceCan be a disguised benefit to the official.

These overlap with conflicts of interest policies and, for advice licensees, the conflicted remuneration rules.

Third parties: the biggest source of risk

Many foreign bribery cases involve intermediaries: agents, consultants, distributors or joint venture partners who pay bribes on a company's behalf. Under the failure to prevent offence, their conduct can make the company liable. Common red flags include:

  • Fees or commissions that are high for the services provided, or paid in cash, to third countries or to unrelated accounts.
  • A third party recommended by, or connected to, a public official.
  • Vague descriptions of services ("consulting", "facilitation").
  • Reluctance to agree to anti-bribery clauses, audits or disclosure of ownership.
  • Operating in countries or sectors with high corruption risk.

Proportionate due diligence (ownership checks, reputation and sanctions screening, references), anti-bribery contract terms, payment controls and ongoing monitoring address these risks. See Third-party risk.

The public sector and the NACC

The National Anti-Corruption Commission investigates serious or systemic corrupt conduct involving Commonwealth public officials, which includes staff of contractors providing services to the Commonwealth. Businesses that contract with government, including financial services firms, can be drawn into NACC investigations. States and territories have their own anti-corruption bodies.
