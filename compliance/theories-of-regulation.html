Theories of regulation: responsive, risk-based and principles-based

Why regulators behave as they do: the theories behind ASIC and APRA's approaches, what the research shows and where the debates remain.

Advanced About 25 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Different theories explain regulation differently: as protecting the public, as captured by industry, or as a way of changing incentives.
  • Responsive regulation, with its enforcement pyramid, has strongly shaped Australian regulators, who start with persuasion and escalate when firms don't cooperate.
  • Risk-based regulation targets supervisory resources at the greatest risks; principles-based regulation relies on broad outcomes rather than detailed rules.
  • Each approach has known weaknesses: capture, under-enforcement, neglect of lower risks and uncertainty about what principles require.
  • Understanding the theory helps compliance professionals predict how regulators will respond and design their own engagement.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Compare public interest, capture and deterrence theories of regulation.
  2. Explain responsive regulation and the enforcement pyramid, and apply them to ASIC and APRA.
  3. Evaluate risk-based and principles-based regulation using research on how they work in practice.
  4. Critically assess what these theories imply for how regulated entities engage with regulators.

The idea in plain English

Why does ASIC sue some firms and negotiate with others? Why does APRA supervise a large bank intensively and a small one lightly? Why do some rules say exactly what to do while others only say "act in members' best financial interests"? Each of these choices reflects a theory about how regulation works. This page sets out the main theories, the research that tests them and the debates that remain. It assumes you know the basics of the regulatory landscape.

Theory and research

Why regulate? Public interest and capture

The traditional view is that regulation corrects market failures, such as information gaps between consumers and financial firms, in the public interest. Stigler (1971) challenged this with the economic theory of regulation: industries have strong incentives to organise and influence regulators, so as a rule, regulation is "acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit". This is the origin of the idea of regulatory capture. The Hayne Royal Commission's criticism that regulators had been too ready to negotiate with the institutions they regulated is a reminder that capture concerns are not merely theoretical.

Deterrence

Deterrence theory treats regulated firms as calculating actors who compare the benefit of a violation with its expected cost (Becker, 1968). It explains why penalties need to exceed the gains from misconduct, but it assumes firms are rational and informed. The enforcement and penalties page covers this in more depth.

Responsive regulation and the enforcement pyramid

Ayres and Braithwaite (1992) argued that regulation need not choose between cooperation and punishment. Their enforcement pyramid starts at the base with persuasion and education, used most often, and escalates through warnings, civil penalties and criminal sanctions to licence cancellation at the top, used rarely. The credible threat of the top of the pyramid makes cooperation at the bottom work. The idea is Australian in origin (Braithwaite is at the Australian National University) and has deeply shaped ASIC and APRA.

Later scholarship refined the idea. Baldwin and Black (2008) argued that "really responsive" regulators must respond not only to a firm's attitude, but to its internal systems and culture, the wider institutional environment, the different logics of regulatory tools and changes in all of these. Parker (2013), marking twenty years of the theory, recognised its influence while noting persistent questions about how escalation works in practice. Mascini (2013) offered a sharper critique: the pyramid became popular partly because it endorsed regulators' professional discretion, and in being reduced to the pyramid the theory lost much of its original concern with the normative questions of how capitalist economies should be regulated.

Risk-based regulation

Regulators have limited resources, so most now allocate supervision according to risk: the likelihood and impact of a firm failing to meet its objectives. APRA's supervision framework is an example. Black and Baldwin (2010) examined how risk-based regulation works in practice and how it can be made more responsive. A known weakness is the neglect of lower-risk firms, which receive little attention even though many small failures can add up, and risks can grow unnoticed below the threshold.

Principles-based regulation

Principles set outcomes ("efficiently, honestly and fairly", "best financial interests") rather than detailed rules. Braithwaite (2002) argued that in complex areas principles can give more certainty than rules, because detailed rules multiply and invite gaming. Black (2008) identified the paradoxes principles face: they depend on shared understanding between regulators and firms, and that understanding tends to be built through guidance and enforcement that can turn principles back into rules. The interpreting legislation page applies this to Australian law.

Responsive regulation predicts regulators will start low on the pyramid and escalate. Australian examples from across this site:

Frequently asked questions

Did the Royal Commission change Australian regulators' approach?

It pushed ASIC towards more litigation (often summarised as "why not litigate?"), and regulators have since used a wider range of tools. Whether the balance is right remains debated.

Is principles-based regulation still used?

Yes. Many key obligations, such as acting efficiently, honestly and fairly, are principles-based, and courts give them concrete content case by case, as in the RI Advice and FIIG cyber cases.

Critical perspectives

  • Theories are lenses, not laws. Each explains some regulatory behaviour and misses other parts. Real regulators combine them, and the mix shifts with politics, inquiries and leadership.
  • Evidence is uneven. Responsive regulation has a large literature, but rigorous tests of whether it produces better outcomes than alternatives are harder to find. Much of the evidence is qualitative.
  • The swing after inquiries. After the Royal Commission, Australian regulators moved towards more litigation. Whether this is responsive regulation working as designed, or a departure from it, is debated.
  • Regulated entities shape regulation too. Submissions, industry codes and informal engagement all influence rules. Capture theory warns about this; responsive regulation sees it as a potential strength.

Seminar questions

  1. Using Stigler's theory of capture, analyse the criticisms of Australian financial regulators before the Hayne Royal Commission. Does capture explain what happened, or are there better explanations?
  2. Place ASIC's "why not litigate?" approach on the enforcement pyramid. Is it consistent with responsive regulation? Draw on Mascini's critique.
  3. APRA supervises by risk. What risks might a risk-based regulator miss, and how could it guard against them?
  4. Is the best financial interests duty a good example of principles-based regulation? Use Black's paradoxes to evaluate it.
  5. If you were designing a regulator's approach to a new sector, such as payments or digital assets, which theories would you draw on and why?

Further reading

  • Ayres, I., & Braithwaite, J. (1992). Responsive regulation: Transcending the deregulation debate. Oxford University Press.
    The classic statement of responsive regulation and the enforcement pyramid: start with persuasion and escalate to tougher sanctions only when firms don't cooperate.
  • Baldwin, R., & Black, J. (2008). Really responsive regulation. The Modern Law Review, 71(1), 59–94. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2230.2008.00681.x Peer-reviewed
    Extends responsive regulation: regulators should respond not only to a firm's attitude but to its operating and cognitive frameworks, the institutional environment, the logic of different tools and how all of these change.
  • Stigler, G. J. (1971). The theory of economic regulation. The Bell Journal of Economics and Management Science, 2(1), 3–21. https://doi.org/10.2307/3003160 Peer-reviewed
    Argues that regulation is often acquired by the industry it regulates and designed and operated primarily for its benefit: the foundation of 'regulatory capture' theory.
  • Black, J. (2008). Forms and paradoxes of principles-based regulation. Capital Markets Law Journal, 3(4), 425–457. https://doi.org/10.1093/cmlj/kmn026 Peer-reviewed
    Distinguishes forms of principles-based regulation and sets out seven paradoxes it faces, including interpretation, compliance, enforcement and trust: principles need shared understanding to work.
