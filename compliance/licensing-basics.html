Licensing basics (AFSL, RSE licence and more)

In Australia you need a licence to provide most financial services. The licence is the regulator's gate, and the conditions attached to it are the rules you live by afterwards.

Beginner About 17 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • An Australian financial services licence (AFSL) from ASIC is needed to carry on a financial services business, such as giving financial product advice, dealing in financial products or acting as a super trustee.
  • Consumer lending and broking need an Australian credit licence from ASIC. Banks, insurers and super trustees also need an authorisation or licence from APRA.
  • Licensees must meet ongoing general obligations, led by the duty to provide services efficiently, honestly and fairly.
  • Many large organisations hold several licences at once, from different regulators.
  • Licensees are responsible for their authorised representatives, and individual financial advisers must meet professional standards and be registered.

Why licensing?

Licensing lets regulators check that a business is competent, honest and adequately resourced before it starts dealing with the public, and gives them a lever afterwards: licences can have conditions added, be suspended or be cancelled, and individuals can be banned. For businesses, the licence conditions and the general obligations that come with it are a major source of compliance obligations.

The AFS licence up close

What counts as a financial service?

Under the Corporations Act, financial services include providing financial product advice, dealing in a financial product (for example issuing or arranging it), making a market, operating a registered managed investment scheme, providing a custodial or depository service, providing a superannuation trustee service, and, since 1 January 2022, handling and settling insurance claims. A licence is authorised for specific services and products, so a licensee can only do what its authorisations cover.

Retail versus wholesale clients

Many consumer protections (disclosure documents, best interests duty, complaints handling, design and distribution) apply only to retail clients. Wholesale clients, such as large businesses and people who meet wealth or income tests, receive fewer protections. Super fund members are generally retail clients, as are individuals buying common types of insurance such as car and home insurance.

The general obligations

Section 912A(1) of the Corporations Act sets general obligations for all AFS licensees. In summary, a licensee must:

  • do all things necessary to ensure its financial services are provided efficiently, honestly and fairly (a civil penalty provision since 2019);
  • have adequate arrangements for managing conflicts of interest;
  • comply with its licence conditions and with the financial services laws;
  • take reasonable steps to ensure its representatives comply with the financial services laws;
  • have adequate financial, technological and human resources, and maintain the competence to provide its services;
  • ensure its representatives are adequately trained and competent;
  • have a dispute resolution system (internal complaints handling plus AFCA membership) where it provides services to retail clients;
  • have adequate risk management systems; and
  • comply with other obligations such as breach reporting.

Licensees must also meet financial requirements and, if they serve retail clients, have adequate compensation arrangements (usually professional indemnity insurance). ASIC explains its expectations in regulatory guides including RG 104 (general obligations), RG 105 (organisational competence and responsible managers), RG 166 (financial requirements) and RG 126 (compensation and insurance arrangements).

Authorised representatives

A licensee can authorise individuals or companies to provide financial services on its behalf. The licensee remains responsible for what its representatives do, which is why monitoring and supervision of representatives is a core compliance activity.

Financial advisers

Individuals who give personal advice to retail clients about relevant financial products ("relevant providers") must meet professional standards, including a qualifications standard, an exam, continuing professional development and a code of ethics, and must be registered, with their details shown on ASIC's Financial Advisers Register. Licensees must keep the register accurate. See ASIC: professional standards.

The RSE licence up close

A trustee that operates an APRA-regulated super fund must hold an RSE licence from APRA under the SIS Act. The RSE licence is focused on prudential matters: governance, fitness and propriety of responsible persons, risk management, investment governance and member outcomes, backed by APRA's SPS prudential standards. Each MySuper product needs a separate APRA authorisation.

Because operating a super fund is also a financial service (a "superannuation trustee service"), RSE licensees must also hold an AFS licence from ASIC authorising that service. So a super trustee answers to APRA on prudential matters and to ASIC on conduct and disclosure.

When things go wrong

Regulators have a range of licensing tools, including:

  • ASIC: imposing or varying licence conditions, suspending or cancelling licences, and banning individuals from providing financial services.
  • APRA: imposing licence conditions, issuing directions, requiring additional capital, accepting enforceable undertakings, disqualifying individuals and, in serious cases, cancelling licences or authorisations.

Breaching a licence condition is itself a breach of a core obligation, which can make it a reportable situation. See Breach and incident reporting obligations.

Worked example: does this need a licence?

A start-up wants to launch an app that recommends which super fund people should join, based on their answers to some questions.

  1. Is it a financial product? Yes: superannuation interests are financial products.
  2. Is it a financial service? Recommending a fund is likely to be financial product advice, and because it considers the person's answers, it may be personal advice.
  3. So: the start-up needs an Australian financial services (AFS) licence, or to act as an authorised representative of a licensee, unless an exemption applies.
  4. Then: licence conditions, the best interests duty for personal advice, disclosure, breach reporting, complaints handling and AFCA membership all follow.

Illustrative only. Whether a licence is needed depends on the facts; ASIC's RG 1 to RG 3 and legal advice are the starting points.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming general information isn't advice, when tailoring makes it personal.
  • Growing into new services that the licence doesn't authorise.
  • Forgetting obligations that come with the licence, such as breach reporting and AFCA membership.

Frequently asked questions

What happens if a business operates without a licence?

It can be a criminal offence and a civil penalty contravention, and ASIC can take action to stop it. Customers may also have rights to undo contracts.

Is an authorised representative licensed?

No. They act under the licensee's licence, and the licensee is responsible for their conduct.

Check your understanding

Which licence?

1. A mortgage broking business helps consumers apply for home loans.

Credit assistance for consumer credit needs an Australian credit licence (or authorisation as a credit representative of a licensee).

2. A company wants to become the trustee of a new public offer super fund.

It needs an RSE licence from APRA and an AFS licence from ASIC authorising a superannuation trustee service.

3. A business wants to give personal financial advice to retail clients about shares and managed funds, in its own name.

Financial product advice is a financial service. It needs its own AFS licence, or to act as an authorised representative of a licensee.
