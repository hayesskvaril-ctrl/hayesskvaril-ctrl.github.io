Privacy law

Organisations hold huge amounts of information about people. Privacy law sets the rules for collecting, using, sharing and protecting it, and those rules are getting tougher.

Beginner About 19 minutes Last reviewed: 8 October 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) applies to Australian Government agencies and most businesses with annual turnover over $3 million, plus some smaller ones such as health service providers.
  • The 13 Australian Privacy Principles cover the whole life of personal information: collection, use and disclosure, quality and security, and people's rights to access and correct it.
  • Under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, breaches likely to cause serious harm must be notified to the OAIC and affected people.
  • Maximum penalties for serious interferences with privacy are now very large, and the first civil penalty under the Act was ordered in 2025.
  • Reforms passed in December 2024 added a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy and new transparency duties, with more reform expected.

What counts as personal information?

Personal information is information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable, whether it is true or not and whether it is recorded or not. Names, addresses, dates of birth, account numbers, photos, voice recordings and location data can all be personal information, depending on context.

Sensitive information gets extra protection. It includes health information, genetic and biometric information, racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, sexual orientation and criminal record. It can generally only be collected with consent, unless an exception applies.

Who does the Privacy Act apply to?

The Act applies to "APP entities":

  • Australian Government agencies; and
  • private sector organisations with an annual turnover of more than $3 million; and
  • some smaller businesses regardless of turnover, including health service providers and businesses that trade in personal information.

State and territory government agencies are covered by their own privacy laws. Removing or narrowing the small business exemption has been proposed as part of further reforms, but at the time of writing it remains in place.

The 13 Australian Privacy Principles

Two principles cause the most trouble in practice:

  • APP 6 (use and disclosure): personal information should generally be used or disclosed only for the purpose it was collected for, unless the person consents or another exception applies (such as a related purpose they would reasonably expect).
  • APP 11 (security): entities must take reasonable steps to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss and unauthorised access, and to destroy or de-identify it when it is no longer needed. The 2024 reforms clarified that "reasonable steps" include technical and organisational measures. Keeping data longer than needed increases the damage when a breach occurs.

Notifiable Data Breaches scheme

Since February 2018, APP entities have had to notify certain data breaches. A breach is an eligible data breach if there is unauthorised access to, disclosure of, or loss of personal information that is likely to result in serious harm to any of the individuals, and the entity hasn't been able to prevent that likely harm through remedial action.

  1. Contain

    Stop the breach and limit further harm.

  2. Assess

    If there are reasonable grounds to suspect an eligible data breach, assess it quickly, generally within 30 days.

  3. Notify

    If it is eligible, notify the OAIC and affected individuals as soon as practicable.

  4. Review

    Learn from the breach and prevent a repeat.

APRA-regulated entities may also need to notify APRA under CPS 234. See Breach and incident reporting obligations.

Enforcement and penalties

  • Since late 2022, the maximum civil penalty for a body corporate for serious interference with privacy is the greatest of $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover in the relevant period.
  • The December 2024 reforms added lower tiers of penalties and infringement notices for less serious breaches, giving the OAIC more options.
  • In October 2025, the Federal Court ordered Australian Clinical Labs to pay $5.8 million in the first civil penalties under the Privacy Act, over a 2022 data breach at its Medlab Pathology business. The penalties related to failing to secure personal information (APP 11), failing to properly assess the suspected breach, and failing to notify it as soon as practicable.
  • The OAIC has also brought civil penalty proceedings against Medibank over its 2022 data breach, which affected about 9.7 million people.

The 2024 reforms and what's next

The Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 (Cth) received Royal Assent on 10 December 2024. It was described as a first tranche of reform. Key changes include:

ChangeTiming
New penalty tiers and OAIC infringement notices; clarification of APP 11 security stepsFrom 11 December 2024
Statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, allowing individuals to sueFrom 10 June 2025
Transparency about automated decisions that significantly affect individuals (privacy policies must explain them)From 10 December 2026
A Children's Online Privacy Code, to be registered by the OAICBy 10 December 2026
Criminal offences for "doxxing"From 11 December 2024

A second tranche followed. On 31 August 2026 the Attorney-General's Department released an exposure draft of the Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026, with consultation until 18 September 2026. Its centrepiece is a requirement that collecting, using and disclosing personal information be lawful, fair and reasonable in the circumstances, with consent becoming one factor rather than a gateway in many cases. The Government has said it plans to introduce a bill before the end of 2026. The draft may change, and no start date has been set. We'll update this page as it progresses.

Other privacy rules to know

  • Credit reporting: Part IIIA of the Privacy Act and the Credit Reporting Code set detailed rules for credit providers and credit reporting bodies.
  • Tax file numbers: the Privacy (Tax File Number) Rule restricts how TFNs are collected, used and stored. Very relevant to super funds.
  • Consumer Data Right: separate privacy safeguards apply to data shared under the CDR in banking and energy.
  • Spam and telemarketing: the Spam Act 2003 and Do Not Call Register Act 2006, enforced by the ACMA.

Worked example: is it a notifiable data breach?

An insurer's staff member emails a spreadsheet of 400 customers' names, policy numbers and health claim details to the wrong external address. The recipient can't be reached.

  1. Is it a data breach? Yes: personal information was disclosed without authorisation.
  2. Is serious harm likely? Health information is sensitive, and the recipient hasn't confirmed deletion. Serious harm is likely.
  3. Can the harm be prevented by remedial action? If the recipient confirmed deletion quickly, the breach might not be notifiable. Here they can't be reached.
  4. Decision: it is an eligible data breach. The insurer must notify the OAIC and affected customers as soon as practicable. If it had only suspected a breach, it would have up to 30 days to assess.
  5. Also: record it in the incident register, consider breach reporting to ASIC if relevant, and fix the cause (for example, email warnings for external recipients).

Illustrative only. Each breach must be assessed on its facts.

Common mistakes

  • Keeping personal information forever, which increases the damage from any breach.
  • Waiting until the investigation is complete before telling affected people, when they could protect themselves sooner.
  • Privacy policies that don't match practice.
  • Forgetting service providers, who often hold large amounts of customer data.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Privacy Act apply to small businesses?

Generally not to businesses with annual turnover of $3 million or less, but there are many exceptions, such as health service providers and businesses that trade in personal information.

Who regulates privacy?

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC). States and territories have their own laws for their public sectors.

Check your understanding

Which APP?

1. A fund keeps full copies of former members' identity documents for 15 years "just in case", and they are stolen in a cyber attack.

APP 11 requires reasonable security and destroying or de-identifying information no longer needed (subject to legal retention requirements).

2. An insurer uses claims health data to target members with marketing for an unrelated product, without consent.

Using sensitive information for a different purpose without consent raises APP 6 and APP 7 issues.

3. A customer asks for a copy of the personal information a bank holds about them.

APP 12 gives individuals a right to access their personal information, with limited exceptions.
