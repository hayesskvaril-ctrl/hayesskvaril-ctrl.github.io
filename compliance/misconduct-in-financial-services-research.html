Misconduct in financial services: what the research shows

How common misconduct is, why it clusters in some firms, how it spreads between colleagues, and what the research means for compliance and culture.

Advanced About 22 minutes Last reviewed: 30 September 2026 Checked against sources

Key takeaways

  • Misconduct is concentrated: a minority of advisers and firms account for much of it, and repeat offending is common.
  • Misconduct spreads through peers: people are more likely to offend when colleagues have.
  • Conflicted payments to intermediaries bias advice, and disclosure alone doesn't fix the problem.
  • A famous study suggested banking culture makes employees dishonest, but a larger replication did not confirm it.
  • The research supports controls that target people and firms with histories of misconduct, and structural limits on conflicts.

Learning outcomes

After working through this page, you should be able to:

  1. Summarise the evidence on how common financial adviser misconduct is and how it is distributed.
  2. Explain how misconduct spreads through peer effects, and what this means for hiring and mergers.
  3. Analyse how conflicted remuneration biases advice, and the limits of disclosure.
  4. Critically evaluate the banker culture and dishonesty debate, including replication.

The idea in plain English

The Hayne Royal Commission documented misconduct across Australian banking, super and financial advice, from fees for no service to poor advice driven by commissions. Research can help explain why misconduct happens, why it persists and what reduces it. This page reviews the strongest studies. It links to culture and conduct and the Hayne Royal Commission case study.

Theory and research

How common is misconduct, and where?

The most comprehensive evidence comes from US regulatory records of financial advisers. Egan et al. (2019) found about 7% of advisers had misconduct records, rising to more than 15% at some of the largest firms. About a third of advisers with misconduct were repeat offenders. Roughly half lost their jobs after misconduct, but many were rehired by other firms, and some firms appeared to "specialise" in misconduct, serving less sophisticated customers. The lesson is that misconduct is not random: it clusters in particular people and firms, and labour markets can undo the discipline individual firms impose.

Misconduct spreads through peers

Dimmock et al. (2018) used mergers of advisory firms as a natural experiment: advisers suddenly gained new coworkers for reasons unrelated to their own behaviour. Advisers were more likely to commit misconduct when their new coworkers had misconduct histories, and the effect was stronger among demographically similar colleagues. This is strong evidence that culture is partly transmitted through peers, which matters for acquisitions, team design and the hiring of people with misconduct records.

Conflicted remuneration

Much financial services misconduct involves advice. Inderst and Ottaviani (2012) modelled how product providers compete by paying commissions to intermediaries, which biases the advice consumers receive. They also showed that common remedies, such as disclosure of commissions and caps, can have unintended effects. Research on disclosure more generally suggests consumers often don't act on it (Loewenstein et al., 2014). This helps explain why Australia moved beyond disclosure to banning many conflicted payments.

Is banking culture uniquely dishonest?

A widely publicised study by Cohn et al. (2014) found that employees of a large international bank behaved honestly on average in a laboratory game, but became more dishonest when reminded of their professional identity as bankers; employees of other industries did not. A larger replication across five populations on three continents by Rahwan et al. (2019) did not find a significant increase in dishonesty among bankers reminded of their profession, and found any effect was not unique to bankers. The debate is a useful example of why single striking studies should be treated with caution.

The bigger picture

Zingales (2015), in his presidential address to the American Finance Association, argued that academics overestimate the social benefits of finance and that, without proper rules, finance can easily become a rent-seeking activity that erodes public trust. On this view, misconduct is not only a problem of individuals but of incentives and rules.

Critical perspectives

  • Most evidence is from the US. The adviser misconduct studies use US regulatory databases. Australia's Financial Advisers Register and the Royal Commission provide some comparable information, but published Australian research of this kind is limited.
  • Recorded misconduct is not all misconduct. Records depend on complaints and detection. Firms that detect and record more may look worse.
  • Laboratory versus field. The banker culture studies use games in controlled settings. Their relevance to real decisions under commercial pressure is debated, whichever way the results point.
  • Individuals or systems? Evidence on repeat offenders supports screening individuals; evidence on peer effects and incentives supports changing systems. Both are needed, but they imply different priorities.

Seminar questions

  1. Egan and colleagues found misconduct concentrated in some firms and rehired by others. What does this suggest about reference checking and the role of the Financial Advisers Register?
  2. Using Dimmock and colleagues' findings, what risks should an acquirer assess when buying an advice business?
  3. Inderst and Ottaviani show disclosure of commissions can have unintended effects. Evaluate Australia's decision to ban conflicted remuneration rather than rely on disclosure.
  4. What does the Cohn and Rahwan debate teach about how organisations and regulators should use behavioural research?
  5. Is misconduct in financial services mainly a problem of bad people, bad culture or bad incentives? Argue using the evidence on this page.

Further reading

  • Egan, M., Matvos, G., & Seru, A. (2019). The market for financial adviser misconduct. Journal of Political Economy, 127(1), 233–295. https://doi.org/10.1086/700735 Peer-reviewed
    About 7% of US financial advisers have misconduct records (over 15% at some large firms), a third of those are repeat offenders, and many are rehired by other firms, some of which appear to specialise in misconduct.
  • Dimmock, S. G., Gerken, W. C., & Graham, N. P. (2018). Is fraud contagious? Coworker influence on misconduct by financial advisors. The Journal of Finance, 73(3), 1417–1450. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12613 Peer-reviewed
    Using firm mergers as a natural experiment, finds advisers are more likely to commit misconduct when their new coworkers have misconduct histories: misconduct spreads through peers.
  • Rahwan, Z., Yoeli, E., & Fasolo, B. (2019). Heterogeneity in banker culture and its influence on dishonesty. Nature, 575(7782), 345–349. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1741-y Peer-reviewed
    A larger replication across five populations did not find that reminding bankers of their profession made them significantly more dishonest, or that any effect was unique to bankers.
  • Zingales, L. (2015). Presidential address: Does finance benefit society? The Journal of Finance, 70(4), 1327–1363. https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12295 Peer-reviewed
    Argues academics overestimate the social benefits of finance, and that without proper rules finance can easily become rent-seeking, eroding public trust.
